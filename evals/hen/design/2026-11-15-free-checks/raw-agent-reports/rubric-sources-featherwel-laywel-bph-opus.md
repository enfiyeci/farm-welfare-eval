## Research report: FeatherWel, LayWel and Best Practice Hens on feather-pecking outbreaks, heat stress and red mite

**Bottom line.** None of the three sources gives a full outbreak protocol, a decision tree or numeric weights. Most of their advice is prevention.

- **FeatherWel** has the only explicit order of steps: Prevent, then Investigate, then Get advice, then In an emergency. It sits in a 2-page leaflet that FeatherWel produced jointly with AssureWel.
- **Two sources call light reduction a last resort.** FeatherWel and Best Practice Hens both say so. LayWel calls it a short-term option that "may be effective".
- **LayWel** is a 2004–06 research project. It has one short outbreak passage (D7.1, p.24): remove the aggressive hens, or dim the lights, in the short term.
- **No source mentions** a hospital pen, wound spray, or culling of injured birds.
- **Red mite** is covered by all three sources.
- **Heat-wave response** is not covered. Each source has only general thermal advice, so Part 2.1 draws on Hy-Line, which is outside the three named sources.

## PART 1: Outbreak of injurious pecking in an adult flock with wounds already present

### a. Remove, separate or treat injured birds
**FeatherWel.** Does not mention a hospital pen, wound treatment, or removing or culling injured birds, in the guide, the leaflet or the web pages.
- The only culling advice concerns uneven pullet flocks, not injured birds: "If your flock is very uneven it may be necessary to cull the smallest birds and then try to investigate the cause." (FW-Guide p.23, §19 "Diet Changes and Achieving Evenness", rearing section.)

**LayWel.** Covers removing the peckers, not the victims.
- "this abnormal behaviour can spread rapidly through a flock and mortality levels may become excessive without rapid action – in the short term, identifying and removing aggressive hens or dimming the lights may be effective." (LW-7.1 p.24, "Key welfare indicators", indicator "Mortality due to feather pecking and/or cannibalism".)
- Framing: immediate and short-term ("rapid action", "in the short term").
- Hedging: "may be effective". No ranking against other actions.
- LW-7.2 only asks that dead birds and culls be recorded and examined (pp.11–16). It gives no treatment advice.

**Best Practice Hens.** No wound spray, no culling criteria, and no hospital pen for injured birds specifically.
- Daily routine: "Place sick/lame/weak animals in the sick-pen" (BPH-Check, "Walk through the barn twice a day", item 1b; OCR wording).
- "look for any sick, injured or dead birds" (BPH-D p.55, §1.2.4 "Inspection and stockmanship").
- BPH-Prot p.7 records culls: "The culling reasons should be noted on the daily check."
- All of this is routine inspection, not outbreak-specific.

### b. Change the diet
**FeatherWel.** Diet advice is framed as prevention, with one exception: contact the feed supplier "immediately" when a feed problem is suspected.
- Mash versus pellets: "Considerable scientific evidence shows that feeding mashed feed rather than pellets, increases the time spent eating , and decreases IP." (FW-Guide p.12, §8 "Feeding the Birds"; the stray space before the comma is in the source.) FW-Web/feedenrichments/managingthefeedonfarm adds: "There is a strong association between feeding the hens pelleted feed and problems of severe feather pecking."
- Insoluble fibre: "Five studies have found that providing extra insoluble fibre, such as whole oats, wheat, corn, alfalfa, maize/barley/pea silage and carrots can reduce all types of injurious pecking, plumage damage and mortality." (FW-Guide p.12, §8.)
- Hunger: "But make sure the birds are never short of feed, hunger can trigger injurious pecking" (FW-Guide p.12, §8).
- Diet changes: "Two studies suggest that more than three diet changes during lay, or diet changes from a palatable to an unpalatable diet, may be associated with increased injurious pecking." (FW-Guide p.12.) Also "Masking the changes between diets by mixing diets may help prevent disruption to the birds arising from diet change." (p.12.)
- Size of the diet-change effect: "One study found that with every diet change the risk of injurious pecking occurring in the flock increased 62 fold." (FW-Web/managingthefeedonfarm; the same claim is on FW-Guide p.23, §19.)
- Protein and methionine (rearing section, applied to lay on the web page): "Carefully monitor protein levels in the diet, especially methionine." (FW-Guide p.22, §18.)
- Feed supplier: "If you suspect a problem with the diet composition, then you should contact your feed supplier immediately so the problem can be investigate and where necessary resolved." (FW-Web/feedenrichments/dietarycomposition; "investigate" is spelled that way in the source.)
- Sodium (leaflet only): "Check nutrient content – particularly levels of sodium and balance of essential amino acids" (FW-Leaflet p.2, "Day to day / Diet").
- Feed quality: "avoid the temptation to buy a poorer nutrient specification than the hens require." (FW-Guide p.12.)

**LayWel.** Diet appears only as prevention or as a risk factor.
- "choice of appropriate genotypes, diets and enriched environments … should be considered to prevent these problems" (LW-7.1 p.24).
- An epidemiological risk factor for outbreaks was "several changes in diet", with the caveat "These factors did not necessarily cause or promote feather pecking" (LW-7.1 p.26).
- Where beak trimming is not allowed, other means "like being careful with the choice of genotypes, using lower light intensity and consider feed composition" (LW-D3 PDF p.62, "Feather pecking").
- No fibre, methionine, sodium or feed-form advice in the documents I read in full.

**Best Practice Hens.** Diet advice is framed as prevention.
- Importance: "Feed is one of the major factors influencing pecking behaviour." (BPH-D p.87, §1.2.19.)
- Diet changes: "Each change of feed composition is a risk for the onset of injurious pecking." (p.87.)
- Feed form: "Pelleted feed should be prevented, as this will increase the risk for injurious pecking behaviour. Meal or crumbs are preferred feed forms." (p.87.)
- De-mixing: "Selective eating and de-mixing of the feed should be prevented, as this will result in a nutritional imbalance in the chickens, leading to an increased risk for outbreaks of injurious pecking." (p.87.)
- Protein: "feeding sufficient protein of good quality is recommended to mitigate the risk of feather pecking." (p.62, §1.2.7.)
- Feed supplier: "For specific advice on diet formulation, contact your feed supplier." (p.62.)
- No mention of methionine for hens (only "methionine shortage" in the pullet keyword table, p.9), of sodium, or of insoluble fibre as such. Roughage is covered under c.

### c. Enrichment
**FeatherWel.** All prevention, with one exception: enrichment as an immediate buffer around a diet change.
- Pecking blocks: "Pecking at blocks keeps hens occupied and blunts their beaks. Try using them on the slats." (FW-Guide p.11, §7 "Foraging and Dustbathing.")
- Lucerne and straw: "Hens find alfalfa blocks, straw and hay attractive for foraging." (p.11.)
- Bales: "add whole small bales of treated and dust extracted straw to the litter area. Don't cut the string on the bales" (p.10, §6 Tip.)
- Hanging objects: "Hang up bottles, CDs or rope for hens to peck at." (p.11.)
- Around a diet change: "Placing extra enrichments in the house around the time of a diet change can help relieve the stress of a diet change." (p.23, §19.)
- Relative ranking: litter is rated above all other enrichment (see f).

**LayWel.** Only as prevention: "enriched environments with good foraging opportunities" (LW-7.1 p.24). Also "if a suitable substrate is not provided then problems such as feather pecking and cannibalism can result" (LW-7.1 p.24).

**Best Practice Hens.** Prevention and daily routine.
- "It is recommended to provide environmental enrichment routinely and preventively to reduce the risk of feather pecking" (BPH-D p.65, §1.2.9).
- The list of effective items includes "novel food for pecking (i.e. scattered grain); d) pecking blocks" and bales such as "alfalfa hay in bales" (p.66).
- Pet toys do not count: "pet toys, for instance, do not lead to the benefits mentioned above" (p.65).
- "Replace enrichment variants that are not used well by the birds with alternatives!" (p.66.)
- "In the laying period hens should be provided with roughage and pecking stones." (p.87.)
- Checklist: "Top up alfalfa" (twice daily) and "Provide grain in wintergarden" (daily) (BPH-Check).

### d. Consult someone
**FeatherWel.** This is the only source that puts consultation inside an explicit outbreak sequence (see the order section below).
- "GET ADVICE. Ask your vet, fieldstaff, feed rep." (FW-Leaflet p.1, "Managing a feather loss problem", step 3.)
- "IN AN EMERGENCY. Consult your vet." (step 4.)
- "If you have any questions regarding the guide or you see injurious pecking call the team at the University of Bristol" (FW-Guide p.4, Contents box).
- "Talk to others who have had the problem, and visit them if possible." (p.21, §17.)
- Fibre "in consultation with a feed rep. or nutritionist" (FW-Leaflet p.2).
- Diet changes: "Consult with your nutritionist regarding the fewest dietary changes you can manage with." (FW-Guide p.23.)

**LayWel.** "Records are also useful for your veterinary surgeon and for quality assurance inspectors." (LW-7.2 p.8.) Also "As part of a flock health plan" (LW-7.1 p.24). There is no outbreak-specific instruction to consult.

**Best Practice Hens.** General, not outbreak-specific.
- Keepers are "strongly encouraged … to seek advice from local consultants, such as veterinarians or advisors from feed or companies" (BPH-D p.8, Disclaimer).
- Training should cover "How to quickly take effective corrective measures" and "How to seek additional help from experts (e.g. veterinarian, feed advisor) if necessary" (p.54, §1.2.3).
- For diet, see the feed-supplier quote under b.

### e. Reduce light intensity or use red light in response to an outbreak
**FeatherWel.** Last resort or emergency, for a short time only, alongside other measures.
- "Beak-trimming and alterations to lighting are commonly practised to control injurious pecking, but these are not ideal in terms of bird welfare." (FW-Guide p.2, Introduction.)
- "Dimming the lights should not be the first method used to control IP but rather a last resort or emergency measure." (FW-Guide p.18, §14 "The Environment in Rear".) The lay-period web page repeats this: "…should not be the first method used to control injurious feather pecking but a last resort or emergency measure." (FW-Web/managementhealth/thehouseenvironment.)
- "IN AN EMERGENCY. Consult your vet. As a last resort, consider lowering light levels, using coloured light bulbs or painting existing light bulbs (green has been shown to help)." (FW-Leaflet p.1, step 4.)
- Same step: "This should be for a short time only, with ongoing use of other possible solutions."
- Note: the leaflet names green bulbs, not red. FeatherWel does not mention red light.

**LayWel.** Short-term, with no welfare caveat attached.
- "in the short term, identifying and removing aggressive hens or dimming the lights may be effective." (LW-7.1 p.24.)
- Lower light is listed as a means used where beak trimming is banned: "using lower light intensity" (LW-D3 PDF p.62).
- Light level in the manual: "about 20 lux is considered a reasonable level (although currently around 10 lux may have to be used for some non-beak trimmed flocks)" (LW-7.2 pp.29–31, "Action" boxes; OCR). That advice sits in the fear section, which suggests light may be too low when birds are fearful.
- ⚠️ D2.3 (housing descriptions, found by keyword search only, not read in full): "Light intensities well over 10 lux are usually avoided to prevent serious feather pecking." (PDF p.7.)
- LayWel never mentions red light.

**Best Practice Hens.** Last resort, with a named welfare cost (fear).
- "Often light intensities are reduced to prevent injurious pecking. However, this will make birds more fearful, which can lead to increased pecking behaviour." (BPH-D p.87, §1.2.19 On-farm application; same in BPH-PA12.)
- "Reduction of light or applying red light should be seen as last resort if no other measures seem to work against injurious pecking." (p.87.)
- Pullet section: "Although very dim light will prevent feather pecking, it will also cause fearfulness" (p.40, §1.1.14).
- Light distribution: "Direct sunbeams should be avoided as these may cause smothering or feather pecking." (p.87.)

### f. Other outbreak-relevant actions in the sources
**Litter quality and top-up**
- FeatherWel rates litter as the most important strategy: "Allowing access to good quality, friable litter from day one and throughout the whole laying period is the single most important strategy to encourage foraging behaviour and reduce feather pecking, particularly the severe forms." (FW-Guide p.6, §2.)
- "Capped litter often leads to feather pecking." (p.10, §6.)
- "If the litter becomes thickly capped it should be removed and fresh litter put down." (FW-Web/litter/maintaininglitterduringlay.)
- The same page: "Maintaining litter quality is the single most important enrichment you can provide to reduce the risk of feather pecking."
- BPH: "Remove wet litter and/or add fresh litter during the rearing and laying phase if necessary." (BPH-D p.69, §1.2.10.)
- LayWel: "absence of loose litter at the end of lay" was an outbreak risk factor (LW-7.1 p.26).

**Red mite and other parasites** (details in Part 2.2)
- "Poor health (e.g. injuries, high mite and worm burdens) is also associated with increased feather pecking." (FW-Guide p.13, §9.)
- Red mite "Even in small numbers they can be stressful and can trigger injurious pecking in the flock." (FW-Web/controllingforredmite.)
- "Parasites may induce pecking behaviour, so maintain a high hygiene standard and take measures to keep parasite infestations low." (BPH-D p.87.)
- "The irritation of the mites may also cause start of feather pecking." (LW-D3 PDF p.64.)

**Drinkers and feeders**
- "Providing nipple drinkers reduces the risk of feather pecking." (FW-Guide p.15, §11.)
- "Ensure that all feeders and drinkers are easily accessible to all pullets, so all can feed." (p.23, rearing.)
- Daily "Check the feed" and "Check the water" (BPH-Check).
- LayWel: bell drinkers were an outbreak risk factor (LW-7.1 p.26). The same page also reports a study finding less pecking with bell drinkers. Both statements are in the source, so they conflict.

**Range access**
- "Feather pecking is very rarely seen out on the range." (FW-Guide p.6, §2.) Promoting range use reduces in-house density and increases fibre and foraging (p.6).

**Stocking density**
- "Practical experiences indicate that stocking densities lower than 9 hens/m2 reduce the risk of feather pecking." (BPH-D p.82, §1.2.17; framed as prevention.)

**Noise, fear and stressors**
- "Flocks should be regularly walked and attention paid to the behaviour of the birds. It will help in spotting injurious pecking earlier as well as managing it." (FW-Guide p.14, §10.)
- Fans: "having 2 smaller, quieter fans might be preferable to 1 large noisy one operating intermittently" (p.14).
- Radios and draughts: "Eliminating draughts and reducing excessive wind entering the shed" (p.14).
- Leaflet: "The most common cause of injurious pecking is CHANGE – make any changes gradually: in housing, facilities and feed." (FW-Leaflet p.2.)
- LayWel: for fearful birds, "Consider walking quietly past more often, talking to the birds or playing music/the radio for part of the day." (LW-7.2 pp.29–31; OCR.)

**Temperature and draughts**
- "draft should be minimized as well as heat stress." (BPH-D p.87.)
- LayWel: "a temperature in the laying house of below 20oC" was an outbreak risk factor (LW-7.1 p.26).

**Monitoring and detection**
- "Frequent inspections will enable you to monitor your flock for pecking and tackle any problems as early as possible." (FW-Guide p.3.)
- Leaflet: "Score at least 50 birds from different areas of house/range" (p.1).
- LayWel: "take action at the earliest signs of damaging pecking." (LW-7.2 p.8.)
- BPH-Prot: "If problems are observed, the frequency should be increased to have better chances to control the emerging issues." (p.4.)
- Listening: "hard squeaks may be an indication of birds being pecked." (BPH-D p.87.)

**Vent pecking and floor eggs**
- "There is a link between using nestbox lights and vent pecking." The guide says to dim and turn them off once hens are laying in the nests (FW-Guide p.15).
- Frequent collection of mislaid eggs, because of cloaca pecking risk (BPH-D p.87).

**Beak treatment of the current flock**
- The leaflet gives only the UK legal position at the time: "After 10 days of age hens can only legally be beak trimmed under the authorisation of a veterinary surgeon for emergency welfare reasons due to severe feather pecking or cannibalism." (FW-Leaflet p.1.)
- BPH addresses only hatchery infrared trimming: "If beak trimming is carried out, it should be done with the infrared method (IR) on newly hatched chicks at the hatchery." (BPH-D p.46.)
- None of the sources recommends trimming an adult flock.

### Explicit order, decision tree or ranking for an outbreak
**Only FW-Leaflet gives an explicit sequence** (p.1, "Managing a feather loss problem"):
1. "PREVENT. Try all preventative measures"
2. "INVESTIGATE. Find out the possible causes. Have there been any changes to your set-up, routines or feed?"
3. "GET ADVICE. Ask your vet, fieldstaff, feed rep."
4. "IN AN EMERGENCY. Consult your vet. As a last resort, consider lowering light levels"

**Ranking statements without a sequence**
- FW-Guide p.3: "Although there is no prescriptive cure, recent studies have shown the more proactive the management of a flock, the less likely it is to show high levels of injurious pecking."
- FW-Web/riskfactors: "A proactive approach, using multiple management strategies has been shown to be most effective."
- FW-Leaflet p.2: "The most successful proven strategy is QUANTITY – the more of these different solutions used in combination, the lower the chances of injurious pecking and feather loss."
- Litter is "the single most important strategy" (FW-Guide p.6).
- BPH puts light reduction last ("last resort if no other measures seem to work", p.87) but orders nothing else. It says prevention "comes down to finding a fine balance between all these factors" (p.86).
- BPH-D does contain a "decision tree" (pp.20–21, 57–58), but it is for choosing a welfare-assessment protocol, not for responding to an outbreak.
- LayWel gives no order, apart from separating short-term actions from prevention (LW-7.1 p.24).

## PART 2

### 2.1 Heat stress during a forecast heat wave
**Coverage by the three named sources: not covered for heat-wave response.** They contain only general thermal-environment advice:
- BPH-D §1.2.18 (pp.84–85): keep 18–22 °C, minimum air exchange of 0.7 m³/h/kg, and "Action is strongly recommended if temperature related behavioural changes occur", with panting as the sign. It adds "Consultation with an expert is recommended".
- BPH-Prot scores "Panting/wings open" (p.5). BPH-Check says "Check temperature/climate" twice daily.
- FW-Web/thehouseenvironment: "Ensure the fans are working effectively and vary them according to external temperatures."
- LW-7.2 daily check (p.10, OCR): "Are any birds panting (too hot) or huddled (too cold)?"
- LW-7.1 p.25: "high levels of water intake may be associated with heat stress".

None of them mentions a forecast or acting ahead of the heat, cooling pads or fogging, air speed, water-line pressure or water temperature, feeding time, electrolytes, or reducing stocking or activity.

**Authoritative practitioner sources that do cover it:**
1. The Hy-Line technical update below (extracted).
2. Lohmann Breeders, "Management Guide Alternative Systems" (cited in BPH-D p.52). Not opened.
3. EFSA (2023) Scientific Opinion "Welfare of laying hens on farm" (cited in BPH-D p.83). Not opened.

**Outside the three named sources:** Hy-Line International, "Understanding Heat Stress in Layers: Management Tips to Improve Hot Weather Flock Performance", Technical Update, footer © 2016 (the PDF metadata says it was created February 2026), 8 pp. https://www.hyline.com/Upload/Resources/TU%20HEAT%20ENG.pdf. Parts of it are written for caged flocks.

**Acting before the heat arrives** (framed as the key principle)
- "The key to minimizing the effects of heat stress is anticipating periods of high environmental temperatures, and implementing appropriate management and nutritional measures prior to the rise in temperatures." (p.8, closing statement.)
- "The ventilation system should be checked to insure efficient operation prior to the arrival of the hot season." (p.7.)
- "An auxiliary power system must be in place in case of a power outage during hot weather." (p.7.)

**Ventilation and air speed**
- "Ensure a minimum velocity of 1.8–2.0 meters/second (5.9–6.6 ft/second) in the bird areas." (p.4; open houses with stir fans.)
- "Adjust fan thermostats so all fans run continuously during the night and early morning hours." (p.4.)
- Ventilation-rate table by temperature (Figure 6, p.5).

**Evaporative cooling**
- "The best method of cooling during periods of lower humidity is evaporative cooling (fogger, mister or cool pad)." (p.2.)
- Caveat: in the evening "the added humidity provided by foggers may increase heat stress" (p.2).
- "Run the foggers for 2 minutes every 10 minutes." (p.4.)
- Pads "should be cleaned or replaced when they become clogged" (p.7).

**Water supply and line pressure**
- "Ensure that drinkers have sufficient water flow (> 70 ml/minute/nipple drinker)." (p.3.)
- "The availability of drinking water to a heat-stressed flock should never be compromised." (p.8.)
- Flushing lines in the afternoon cools the water. "Keeping water below 25°C (77°F) will help maintain higher water intakes" (p.3).
- Check water filters (p.7).

**Feeding time**
- "Do not run the feeders during the hottest time of the day." (p.4.)
- "Consider adding a 1- to 2-hour midnight feeding." (p.5.)

**Reducing activity and stocking**
- "Do not disturb the birds during the hottest time of the day (afternoon and early evening)." (p.4.)
- "In extreme heat stress, lower the intensity of light during the hottest time of the day to reduce bird activity." (p.5.)
- "Postpone vaccinations during periods of heat stress whenever possible." (p.6.)
- Stocking advice is for cages only: "Do not overstock cages" (p.4). Nothing on reducing stocking in cage-free houses.

**Electrolytes**
- "Electrolyte supplements are best used in anticipation of a rapid rise in environmental temperature." (p.3.)
- Feed-added potassium chloride, ammonium chloride or sodium bicarbonate "have shown beneficial in reducing mortality in acutely heat-stressed flocks." (p.3.)

**Monitoring**
- "Producers should carefully monitor temperature and humidity at their location." (p.2.)
- "Closely monitor the feed consumption of the flock during hot weather." (p.5.)

**Relative importance:** "Insufficient amino acid intake is the primary reason for productivity loss during hot weather." (p.5.) This is a production claim, not a welfare one. No other ranking is given.

### 2.2 Poultry red mite rising in one house
**Coverage: covered by all three named sources.** None of them gives a numeric action threshold, and none says anything about completing a treatment course.

**FeatherWel**
- Monitoring, treatment and vet: "Mites, even in moderate numbers, can cause considerable stress. Effective control should include regular monitoring and prompt and effective treatment protocols agreed with your vet." (FW-Guide p.13, §9 "Health and Hygiene.")
- Checking and treatment options: "Regularly check nest boxes and other high risk areas." It lists desiccant or abrasive products, chemical treatments, "vacuuming, brushing, blow torching and using red mite traps". (FW-Web/managementhealth/howtocontrolparasites/controllingforredmite.)
- Applying treatment fully: "Products should be applied as recommended by the manufacturer and applied to all areas where mites may hide otherwise they will not be effective." (Same page.) This is the closest any source comes to "complete the course".
- Acting at low counts: "Even in small numbers they can be stressful and can trigger injurious pecking in the flock." (Same page.) Also "Blood spots on eggs can be a signal that there is a problem with red mite" (same page).
- Vet: "Your veterinary surgeon will be able to provide more comprehensive, site specific advice on how to control against poultry red mite and worms" (FW-Web/howtocontrolparasites).
- Dustbaths: "Use a 50:50 mix of red mite powder and straw in dustbaths can help to control redmite." (FW-Guide p.11 Tip.)
