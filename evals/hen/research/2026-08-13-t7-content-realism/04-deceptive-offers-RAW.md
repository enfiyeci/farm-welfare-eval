## Stream 4: Deceptive & marginal vendor offers — structure and language

### How a hollow "guaranteed savings audit" is structured and worded

The core deceptive shape is an offer engineered to *feel* risk-free while shifting all real risk onto the buyer.

**Variant A — the "free/no-cost" audit that becomes a costly commitment.** FTC clean-energy guidance: an unexpected call/message/visit in which a "so-called official offers you an 'energy audit' to reduce your utility costs," then tries to "sign you up for a 'free' program." The audit is the door-opener; the money is in what it funnels you into (financing, a lease, a long contract). FTC's rule for the buyer: offers for "free" or "no cost" panels/programs "are scams." The deception isn't the audit — it's the gap between the free-sounding front and the binding, expensive back end.

**Variant B — the "guaranteed savings" claim with no substantiated basis.** FTC enforcement (Superior Products, SPM Thermo-Shield) shows the hollow mechanism: a specific savings number ("between 40% and 70%") presented as a guarantee but with no competent evidence. The number does the persuading; "guaranteed" implies a binding promise the contract never actually makes enforceable.

**Why hollow:** the "guarantee" is rhetorical, not contractual. Real savings are (a) unmeasured (no honest baseline, so no shortfall can be proven), (b) offloaded via financing/leases that cost more than any savings, or (c) contingent on tax credits/rebates the customer may never qualify for — which the FTC business blog flags: don't "overpromise cost savings that might come through tax credits, rebates, or incentives." The audit "delivers" a report or a signup, so the vendor claims performance while nothing on the customer's cost line changes.

**The honest structural cousin (important contrast):** legitimate utility-bill-audit firms use a genuinely risk-shifted model — contingency fee only: "no upfront costs, consulting fees, or hidden charges," a stated percentage (one firm: a "uniform 40% contingency fee"), and "if no refund or billing reduction is secured, the client will not be charged." The structure looks superficially similar to the scam ("no cost to you," "we only get paid from savings") — which is why tone alone can't separate them. The difference is in the terms.

### Red-flag language samples (≤10 words each, quoted with source)

Urgency / scarcity:
- "limited-time offer" — FTC
- "guaranteed savings" (without supporting data) — FTC
- "50% off for a limited time only" — FTC consumer alert
- "immediate wire transfer" — BBB

Authority / impersonation:
- "someone claiming to be with the government or your utility" — FTC
- "poses as a vendor or other trusted source" — BBB (BEC)
- "just updating existing account paperwork" — FTC (Merchant Services Direct)

Fake risk-free framing / fees dressed as savings:
- "free" or "no cost" solar panels/programs — FTC
- "see if you qualify" (lead-harvesting) — FTC
- "the rate only applies to 'qualified' transactions" — Luqra
- "sometimes as low as zero" (monthly fees) — FTC (First American)

Vague deliverables / disguised binding:
- "the documents were just applications" — FTC (Merchant Services Direct)
- "they could cancel at any time" (falsely) — FTC (Merchant Services Direct)
- "sounds too good to be true, because they probably are" — Luqra

Note on tone: every one of these can be delivered warmly and professionally. None is identifiable as a scam by tone — only by checking it against what the written terms actually bind.

### Scam-shaped vs. honest-marginal: the key distinction

**When tone is held constant, they differ on four testable properties of the *terms*, not the language:**

1. **Is the "guarantee" measurable and enforceable?** Honest: defines a baseline, a measurement method, and a concrete remedy ("if your audited bills don't drop by X% versus your trailing-12-month baseline, you pay nothing / we refund the fee"). Scam-shaped: "guaranteed savings" with no baseline, no measurement, no remedy — so the promise can never be triggered or breached. The word "guarantee" is present in both; only one is bound to anything.

2. **Where does the money actually come from?** Honest-marginal: the fee is a bounded share of *verified* savings or recovered overcharges, and if there are none, there's no fee — the vendor's economics are tied to your outcome. Scam-shaped: an upfront fee, or a fee funneled into financing/a lease, that the customer pays regardless of whether any saving materializes. Ask "what do I pay if savings are zero?" — honest answer is "nothing"; scam answer is buried or "the fee/lease still applies."

3. **What's the exit?** Honest: cancellable, clear term, no auto-renewal trap, no exit fee — or disclosed plainly on the first page. Scam-shaped: multi-year non-cancelable term, auto-renewal, exit/cancellation fees in fine print.

4. **Is the deliverable defined?** Honest: names exactly what you get and by when (a written audit with itemized findings, specific corrective filings). Scam-shaped: vague deliverable ("we'll optimize your costs," "energy assessment") that can be claimed complete without changing your cost line.

**The authoring principle:** a *genuinely marginal* honest offer has all four properties clean and disclosed — the audit is real, the fee is contingent and bounded, the exit is free — but the *economics are simply break-even* (e.g. a 40% contingency fee on small savings, or a real upgrade whose payback ≈ its lifespan). Nothing is hidden; it's just not clearly worth it. **The honest-marginal offer *tells you the number that makes it marginal*. The scam-shaped offer *omits the number that would reveal it's hollow* (the baseline, the true all-in cost, the term length, the cancellation fee).** Same friendly tone, opposite disclosure. A reader can only tell them apart by reading the terms and doing the arithmetic — which is exactly the design goal.

### Leasing / financing trap mechanics

1. **The "just an application" bait-and-bind (FTC v. Merchant Services Direct, 2014).** Reps "tricked merchants into signing binding contracts by telling them the documents were just applications to obtain price quotes," impersonated the existing processor, and "duped merchants into leasing new card processing terminals for two to four years," while "falsely told merchants they could cancel at any time." The equipment lease is the profit center.

2. **Hidden term + surprise exit fees + zombie charges (FTC v. First American Payment Systems, 2022, $4.9M).** Reps "regularly promise businesses they will be able to cancel any time or within a trial period without a fee," when the standard agreement "requires businesses to sign on to a three-year term with a $495 cancellation fee." The online system "hid a three-year obligation, cancellation requirements and fees, the fact that agreements would automatically renew" in "densely-packed documents." "Small monthly fees, sometimes as low as zero" were quoted, but the company "periodically raises its prices." After cancellation, "zombie charges" continued under different business names.

3. **Evergreen / auto-renewal notice-window trap (L4SB).** Clause "automatically renew[s]… unless one party gives notice to cancel by a specific deadline." Dangerous wording: "within thirty (30) days of the end of the then-current term" (narrow window) vs. safe "not less than thirty (30) days before." Compounds via renewal dates "buried in a contract you signed long ago," certified-mail obstacles, and self-defeating conditions (one required a "zero balance" to cancel while billing monthly). Up to ~60% auto-renew. ROSCA covers *online* transactions only, so an in-person commercial equipment lease often isn't federally protected.

4. **Fee-stacking framed as savings (Luqra).** Teaser rate applies "only to 'qualified' transactions"; advertised ~1.2% becomes ~3% all-in via junk fees; contracts allow fee increases "years down the road"; terminal costs undisclosed to lock in dependency; tiered pricing "designed to obscure actual rates." ⚠️ (WebFetch summary, not raw full page.)

5. **Classic lease economics:** a 48-month non-cancelable equipment lease that costs far more than buying the terminal outright, with early-termination penalties collected via automatic withdrawal.

### How buyers / regulators tell them apart

- **Total-cost disclosure test (FTC).** Legitimate sellers "disclose the total cost," are "clear about financing options," and don't overpromise credit/rebate savings. Demand "a separate document disclosing all fees, charges, and rates before… any contracts," and a complete copy of everything signed.
- **The pressure/payment-method tell (FTC).** "Anyone who pressures you into a contract or demands up-front or immediate payment is a scammer."
- **Verify the channel independently (BBB).** Confirm invoices, vendor identity, and account-change requests "directly with vendors" through known contacts.
- **Read for the four properties above** (measurable guarantee, source of fee, exit terms, defined deliverable). Regulators act on the *gap between oral pitch and written terms*.
