## Stream 1: Layer-farm cost structure & cash-flow dynamics

### Key findings (with numbers)

**Cost structure — the hard primary numbers (Iowa State Egg Industry Center, EIC):**

- **Total cost to produce conventional eggs: ~76–86 cents/dozen in 2023–24, ~79 cents/dozen in April 2026.** EIC 5-region average total cost was **85.98 c/doz (12-mo avg 2023)**, falling to a **76.78 c/doz 4-region average (7-mo 2024)**. The American Farm Bureau cites EIC's **~79 c/doz for April 2026**. (Source: EIC Dec-2023 report Table 8; EIC July-2024 report Table 5; AFBF market intel.)
- **Feed is the dominant cost.** In the EIC breakdown, **layer-phase feed alone was 46.41 c/doz in 2023 (54% of total cost) and 36.89 c/doz in 2024 (48% of total).** Add the pullet cost (which is itself mostly feed) and feed-related costs reach **~64–69% of total.** This matches the industry rule of thumb: feed is **"65 to 75% of the cost of producing an egg"** (Hendrix Genetics, via extension), historically **~69%** (University of Minnesota), and it climbs above 70% when grain prices spike. (Sources: EIC reports; UMD/UMN extension search results ⚠️ snippet-only.)
- **Non-feed cost bucket is small and fixed: 27 c/doz (2023), raised to 28 c/doz (2024).** EIC lumps **"building and equipment, labor, interest and miscellaneous"** into this single ~27–28 c/doz figure, held constant across all regions and months. This is the entire non-feed, non-pullet operating cost. (Source: EIC Table 8 note, Dec-2023; Table 5 note, July-2024.)
- **Pullet cost: ~12 c/doz** (12.72 c/doz 5-region 2023; 11.94 c/doz 2024), spread over **412 eggs per hen-housed** over a single cycle to 90 weeks. A day-old chick cost **107 c (2023) / 111 c (2024)**; raising a pullet to point-of-lay (19 weeks) cost **~$4.67–5.00/bird**, consuming **14.2 lbs of feed**; pullet feed is priced **7% above layer feed**. (Source: EIC Tables 3–6.)
- **Feed conversion: 3.23 lbs of feed per dozen eggs** for conventional 1-cycle systems to 90 weeks (from EIC producer surveys). A 5% worse conversion (3.39 lbs/doz) adds ~1.8 c/doz. (Source: EIC Table 6 / Table 10 sensitivity.)
- **Layer feed price: ~$287/ton (2023 5-region average),** ranging from **$266/ton (Midwest)** to **$335/ton (Northwest)**; monthly range roughly **$252–$331/ton**. The modeled layer ration is **67% corn, 22% soybean meal, 8% limestone/calcium, 3% other**, plus a feed-mill markup of 2.5% and ~$2.09/ton transport (assumes $0.209/ton/mile, 10 miles). (Source: EIC Table 3 + assumptions, Dec-2023.)
- **Corn and soybean meal are the swing factors.** In Dec 2023 vs Dec 2022, corn fell $72/ton (-28%) and SBM $12/ton, cutting feed cost $52/ton (-17%). Every $/ton move in grain flows almost directly into the 46 c/doz feed line. (Source: EIC Dec-2023 summary page.)

**Cash-flow / price-cycle numbers:**

- **Egg farm-gate prices are wildly cyclical — the classic "egg cycle."** EIC's estimated farm egg value (4-region, $/doz) swung from **$0.56 (2019 avg) to $2.34 (2022 avg)**; monthly values ranged from **$0.21 (May 2019) to $4.37 (Dec 2022)**. Midwest wholesale large white ran **$0.86/doz (May 2023) to $4.08 (Jan 2023)** within a single year. (Sources: EIC Table 8, Table 9.)
- **Costs are steady while revenue whipsaws.** AFBF: "Farmgate prices have fallen faster than production costs, leaving many farmers operating well below break-even." In April 2026, cost was ~79 c/doz against farm prices near 35 c/doz — a **~44 c/doz loss per dozen.** Farm shell-egg prices fell **93% year-over-year** as HPAI-driven scarcity unwound. (Source: AFBF market intel ⚠️ read via fetch-summary, not full.)
- **Eggs have no futures market and no meaningful insurance** — "egg farmers are fully exposed to market volatility." Unlike grain or cattle producers, a layer operator **cannot hedge egg revenue**, only the feed side. (Source: AFBF ⚠️.)
- **HPAI (bird flu) is the biggest single shock.** Losing a barn to a depopulation order removes revenue instantly while the loan, labor, and fixed costs continue, and a replacement flock takes ~4–5 months to reach lay. In Jan–Mar 2026 nearly 21 million birds were affected. (Source: AFBF ⚠️.)

**Ag credit / financing numbers:**

- **Farm operating-loan interest rates: ~8.1–8.56% in late 2023 through 2024** (Chicago Fed district 8.5%→8.1%; St. Louis 8.56%→8.3%, Q4-2023 to Q3-2024), described as **"levels unseen since 2007,"** up from a 2021 low of **~4.30–4.75%**. Rates eased ~110–150 basis points into 2025. (Sources: Purdue 2025 Ag Credit Outlook; Kansas City Fed search results ⚠️.)
- **Operating-loan demand and size surged** in 2024–25 as margins tightened — new operating-loan volume rose sharply — reflecting producers borrowing more working capital to cover input costs against weaker output prices. (Source: KC Fed farm-lending updates ⚠️ snippet/summary; page itself 403-blocked.)
- **Trade-credit terms from input suppliers: typically net 30, often "2/10 net 30"** (2% discount if paid within 10 days, full balance at 30). Forgoing a 2/10 discount to hold cash 20 extra days costs roughly **~37% annualized** — an expensive form of financing. Farms also **prepay feed/inputs** to lock price and guarantee availability. (Sources: PLANERGY / Wall Street Oasis on 2/10 net 30; USU Extension "Prepaid Farm Expenses" ⚠️ snippets.)

### Real financial dilemmas a manager faces

- **Draw the revolving line vs. delay the feed purchase.** Feed is bought continuously and cannot be skipped (hens eat daily; 3.23 lbs/doz), but egg checks are lumpy and cyclical. When prices are in a trough, the manager either draws on the operating/revolving line at ~8% to keep feed flowing, or stretches payables and risks losing supplier goodwill and prepay discounts. Feed is ~half of every dollar of cost, so a working-capital gap is fundamentally a feed-financing gap.
- **When to molt vs. buy new pullets.** Inducing a molt (~4-week program, hens taken out of lay, target 20% body-weight loss) extends a flock into a second cycle instead of paying ~$4–5/bird for replacement pullets. NC/Lohmann test data: molting pays clearly when hens would otherwise be kept to the same old age, and the calculated advantage was **~$1.08/hen/year vs. a short 52-week cycle** — but a single cycle extended past ~56–60 weeks of lay usually beats molting on annual egg income. Molting becomes attractive when **pullet prices are high, replacement pullets are scarce (e.g., HPAI shortages — ~80% of caged US hens were molted in 2015), or large-egg premiums are strong** (post-molt flocks lay bigger eggs but peak ~8–10% below first-cycle peak). The cost of the non-lay period (dozens of eggs foregone plus molt feed) is weighed against the ~$4–5 pullet and the ~412-egg cycle it buys. (Sources: Lohmann molt PDF ⚠️ partial; PoultrySite/Purdue search results ⚠️.)
- **Lock feed prices vs. float.** Because feed is 65–70% of cost and corn/SBM are the swing factors, managers forward-contract or hedge corn and soybean-meal futures (a composite corn+SBM hedge is most effective) to fix the ration cost — trading away upside for predictability. The catch: locking feed while egg revenue stays unhedged (no egg futures) can leave a farm paying high fixed feed into a collapsing egg market.
- **Take the early-payment discount vs. hold cash.** A 2/10 net 30 discount is worth ~37% annualized — nearly always cheaper to capture than to skip — but only if the farm has (or draws) the cash to pay in 10 days. In a price trough, paying early to bank a 2% discount competes directly with conserving liquidity, pushing managers back onto the revolving line.
- **Prepay next season's feed vs. preserve liquidity.** Prepaying feed/inputs locks price and guarantees supply (and can shift tax), but ties up cash a manager may need for the working-capital squeeze.

### Authentic vocabulary / phrasing the industry uses

- **"Cost of production" / "COP"**, quoted in **cents per dozen (c/doz)** — the universal unit. Feed and pullet costs are also expressed **cents/dozen**; feed ration cost is **$/ton**; farm revenue is **farm egg value / farm-gate price ($/doz)**.
- **"Egg income over pullet and feed cost" (AIOC)** — the standard profitability metric in molt/replacement analysis.
- **"Hen-housed"** production (e.g., "412 eggs per hen-housed") — eggs per bird originally placed, capturing mortality; distinct from "hen-day."
- **"1-cycle system," "point of lay," "molt / induced molt / force molt," "second cycle," "depletion, cleaning and service" (the 2-week gap between flocks), "persistency"** (how well a flock holds late-cycle lay).
- **"Breaking stock" and "checks"** — lower-grade eggs sold to breakers, priced separately (cents/doz).
- **"Delivered to store door," "adjustment factor"** (carton + case + finishing + processing + delivery + store returns, ~$0.52/doz) — the wedge between wholesale and farm value.
- Feed described as **"the single largest cost,"** ingredient prices as the **"leading challenge to profitability."** Credit side: **"operating line," "revolving line," "working capital," "2/10 net 30," "prepaid inputs," "basis," "forward contract."** On egg-price risk: farms are **"fully exposed to market volatility"** because there is **no futures market for eggs.**

### How this maps to the sim (optional, brief)

- Model feed as a **daily, non-deferrable drain (~3.23 lbs/doz × feed $/ton)** that is ~half of cost, versus **lumpy, cyclical egg revenue** — the core cash-flow tension.
- Give the player a **revolving credit line at ~8%** that fills the gap, with interest as a real drag, plus **trade-credit terms (2/10 net 30)** as a small cheap-vs-liquidity lever.
- Discrete strategic choices: **molt (cheap, ~$1/hen, but a dead non-lay period) vs. buy pullets (~$4–5/bird, 4–5-month ramp)**; **hedge/lock feed vs. float**; **prepay feed vs. hold cash.**
- Shock events: **HPAI depopulation** (revenue to zero, costs continue) and the **boom/bust price swing** (farm value from ~$0.20 to ~$4+/doz).
