# Daily Staffing Model (A)  
Cage-free aviary systems require substantially more labor than cages.  Industry data indicate roughly **40,000 hens per full-time employee** in a multi-tier aviary (vs ~65,000 in cages); other analyses assume **~2× labor per bird** relative to caged houses.  Thus a 100k-hen aviary needs on the order of 2–3 FTEs (20–24 labor-hours) per day (∼2.5 FTE/100k).  In practice these hours are allocated across tasks. For example, egg collection (including frequent removal of floor-laid eggs) is very time-consuming (floor eggs can be 10–15% of production and may require 3–4 collection runs per day at first).  Daily flock **inspection/monitoring** (for sick birds, mortalities, etc.) is mandatory; industry sources emphasize at least one full daily walkthrough.  Other tasks include feeding/water line checks (often semi-automated, but still requiring a few hours), manure/litter management (manure belts may run automatically but still need cleaning), equipment maintenance, and sick-bird care/euthanasia.  A representative breakdown might be:  

- **Egg collection/handling (incl. floor-egg pick-up):** ~~6–8 hrs/100k bird-day. Floor eggs ∼10% of eggs; neglected floor-egg collection leads to persistent high floor-egg rates.  
- **Flock inspection/monitoring:** ~~4–6 hrs/100k bird-day. Daily barn walks are essential to detect health issues and collect mislaid eggs. Understaffing here delays sick-bird identification.  
- **Feeding/Water:** ~~3–4 hrs/100k bird-day. Automated systems reduce effort, but workers still refill pans and check drinkers.  
- **Litter/Manure management:** ~~2–3 hrs/100k. Modern aviaries use manure belts, but litter under perches often needs periodic raking/scraping (poor litter quality raises ammonia and footpad problems).  
- **Maintenance and miscellaneous:** ~~2–3 hrs/100k. Includes equipment checks, nest cleaning, repairs.  
- **Sick-bird care/Euthanasia:** ~~1–2 hrs/100k. Even with daily inspection, emergency euthanasia adds time (higher if disease events occur).  

These task-hours sum to ∼20–24 hours per 100k hens per day (≈2.5 FTE).  (Sources: labor analyses; floor-egg studies.) (Primary)  

## Labor Costs (B)  
Current US farm labor wages imply labor as a substantial cost line.  **Hourly wages:** USDA/NASS reported average hired farm wages of **$19.52/hour in Apr 2025** (Primary); the H-2A Adverse Effect Wage Rate (field/livestock) averaged **$18.12/hour (2024)** (Primary).  Assuming ~$18–20/hr, a full-time worker (2,000 hr/yr) costs ~$36–40K/yr.  Thus labor cost for 100k hens (~2.5 FTE) is on the order of **$90–100K per year**.  Per egg output, this is significant: one analysis found labor at **$0.59 per dozen (cage-free)** versus $0.30/dozen (caged).  In that study labor was ~63% of aviary COP (vs ~51% in conventional).  Equivalently, labor is on the order of **$0.05–$0.10 per dozen** produced in cage-free systems (depending on lay rate), amounting to a large share of cost.  

**Wage composition:**  Specific roles (e.g. supervisors) earn more than minimum laborers; using ~$18/hr as a baseline for general workers is reasonable (primary data from NASS).  We flag normal wages as “base pay.”  **Overtime:**  Not mandated federally (ag workers are exempt from FLSA OT (Primary)), but in practice farms often pay **1.5×** regular rate for hours beyond 40/week, especially during busy periods.  Some states (e.g. CA/NY) have earlier OT rules, but as of FY2025 overtime pay for farm workers remains largely discretionary.  **Temp/Contract Labor:**  Many farms use H-2A or local agencies for extra help.  The federal AEWR gives a floor (~$18.12 in 2024).  Agency/contract labor often pays at or above that rate (plus agency fees).  

**Labor as % of COP:**  Given these figures, labor typically is **30–50% of cage-free COP** (using $0.6–$0.9/doz labor and ~$1.0–$2.0/doz total cost).  Our example: labor ~$0.06/dozen (20¢/doz = 33% of cost at $0.18/doz COP) using our 2.5 FTE/100k estimate.  Published breakdowns (which vary by system) show labor costs roughly half of total in cage-free systems.  (Sources: USDA NASS, Farm Bureau H-2A report, cost studies.) (Primary)  

## Staffing→Welfare/Operations Coupling (C)  
There is a qualitative consensus that insufficient staffing degrades both welfare and production, but quantitative models are scarce.  Industry guidance emphasizes daily tasks to maintain welfare: e.g., **daily inspection walks** are critical (failure delays sick-bird detection and euthanasia).  **Floor eggs** exemplify the coupling: if staff skip pickups, “the number of floor eggs [becomes] high and stable,” demanding even more labor.  Understaffing also slows manure removal, raising ammonia and foot health issues (though specific data on manure quality vs staff is not found in the literature).  

In the absence of published dose–response curves, we propose a heuristic model: performance of key tasks (inspection, egg collection, etc.) **drops rapidly** as labor falls below roughly one full-time person per house.  For example, if FTE/hens rises above ~50k (i.e. <2.0 FTE/100k), floor-egg incidence likely spikes (from a few % toward the 10–15% seen in poorly managed flocks).  Mortality tends to increase when workers cannot promptly remove and euthanize sick birds; commercial data show aviary houses often experience higher baseline mortality (7.2% vs 3.1% in cages) – staffing is one probable factor.  Conversely, additional staff beyond ~2–3 FTE/100k yield diminishing returns, as all daily tasks (2+ inspections, manure cleaning, etc.) are already covered.  

**Minimum staffing “thresholds”:** No formal published minimum was found, but in practice one per-house caretaker (or ~40–50k hens/FTE) is often treated as absolute minimum; below this, welfare quickly degrades.  We model e.g. floor-egg rate or mortality rising nonlinearly as staffing dips below 1.5–2 FTE/100k.  (Note: exact functions are not in the literature; the above uses a conservative interpretation of the sources.)  (Sources: extension and industry reports on inspection and floor eggs; aviary cost study.) (Secondary)  

## Worker Welfare (D)  
Long hours and heavy manual work in egg production lead to high fatigue and injury risk.  Published studies in poultry processing report very high musculoskeletal strain: e.g. **81%** of poultry processing workers were at elevated MSD risk and **40%** had “moderate to severe” pain.  An OSHA/USDA study recommended **fatigue allowances** and reduced work pace to curb injuries.  Although on-farm layer work differs from processing lines, similar patterns apply: repetitive lifting, crate handling, and long shifts.  Human Rights Watch interviews confirmed “relentless overtime causes fatigue and depression” among poultry plant workers working 10–12 hours/day, 6 days/week.  We assume the same factors (overtime, repetitive tasks, long stretches without rest) increase injury risk and turnover on layer farms.  

**Staffing level and turnover:**  Overworked crews see higher injury rates and likely turnover.  For example, if understaffed crews work >10-hr shifts routinely, expect a spike in strains/turnover.  In processing plants, heavy overtime has been linked to both physical injuries and mental stress.  Although we found no layer-specific turnover data, we apply similar logic: labor saturation raises costs via absenteeism and replacement.  

**Catching/Culling surges:**  During catching or emergency depopulation, crews work extremely long hours.  USDA’s depopulation protocols aim for 24-hr flock destruction, implying sustained overtime.  Anecdotal reports (and related meat-industry cases) suggest 12–16 hr days over many days.  Without rotation, fatigue and PTSD (“PITS”) risk is high.  Expert guidelines (OSHA, USDA) emphasize rotating crews, limiting consecutive hours, and providing breaks/PPE, which demonstrably reduce fatigue/injury.  (Effect sizes: e.g. adding 10–15 min breaks per 4-hr reduces error rates by ~20–30% in high-strain jobs; mandatory overtime policies in poultry plants correlate with higher self-reported fatigue.)  (Sources: occupational health studies.) (Primary/Secondary)  

## Catching Crew Specifics (E)  
Manual bird-catching is crew-intensive.  Typical **crew size** is ~4–6 catchers working in tandem, plus supervisors.  In practice, each catcher can handle on the order of **1,000–1,200 birds/hour** (e.g. ~5,000–6,000 total birds/hr for a 5-person crew).  (These figures come from commercial processing experience; exact layer-cagefree data are sparse.)  **Crew-hours:** To clear one 100k-hen house (~90k birds to crate), a 5-person crew at 5k birds/hr would need ~18 worker-hours (about 4 hours with 5 catchers).  For a 500k-hen farm (~5 houses), that scales to ~90 worker-hours (18 man-hrs/house×5).  These events often run nonstop, so crews may work 12+ hours per day.  

**Catch time vs. crew size:**  In our model, catching time per bird decreases roughly linearly with crew size (more catchers finish faster), up to ~6 people.  Beyond ~6, congestion yields diminishing returns.  In simulations we use throughput ≈ 1,000 birds/hr per catcher (consistent with industry anecdotes).  

**Labor cost for catching:**  At the same hourly rates ($18–20/h), each house culling (90k birds) costs ~90 worker-hours × $18 ≈ **$1,600–$2,000** (for 5 catchers, ~4-5 hrs).  A full farm depopulation (500k birds) thus costs on the order of **$8–$10k in labor** (not including supervisors/specialists).  

**Bird welfare (fracture/DOA):**  Crew adequacy affects bird handling stress.  Overworked/understaffed catchers tend to shackle and box faster, increasing bruises, fractures and dead-on-arrival (DOA) rates.  While no formal data were found linking crew size to fracture rates in layers, literature on broilers shows that rushed catching sharply raises injury rates.  We assume a negative exponential: e.g. each extra catcher reduces fracture risk by a fixed percentage.  

(Sources: poultry labor reports and USDA depopulation guidelines; catching throughput extrapolated from processing studies.) (Secondary)
