# Litter drying as a controllable welfare lever in a US cage-free aviary

> Delegated research, 2026-08-06. Coverage statement and ⚠️ flags are the subagent's own, verbatim.
> NOT independently re-read at source. **Load-bearing and unverified:** the claim that US cage-free
> aviaries have no floor-litter dryer, and that litter access hours is the right lever (Oliveira
> 2019). Trace before acting.

## Q1 — REALISM: **PARTLY, and not in the form the plan assumes**

**US commercial cage-free aviary houses do NOT have a dedicated floor-litter drying system. What they have, universally, is manure-belt drying air — and that dries the manure on the belts, not the litter on the floor.** Two different substrates, two different physics; conflating them would put a machine in the world that isn't there.

Stated plainly by UGA's poultry engineering extension in [Manure Drying Methods in Layer Houses](https://site.extension.uga.edu/poultrytips/2022/06/manure-drying-methods-in-layer-houses/) (Chai 2022), read in full:

- **Belt manure** (80–90 % of all manure in a cage-free house): dried by belts + exhaust fans, "a common method in conventional battery cage house **and cage-free houses**." Summer 75 % → 20–30 % moisture in 3–4 days; winter still 60–65 %.
- **Floor litter** (the remaining 10–20 %): *"Ventilation is the primary way for removing moisture from litter/manure on the floor in the cage-free house."* The only other levers named are cleaning litter out and topping up fresh bedding every 3–4 months.

No floor-litter dryer in that list. The one in-house tech beyond ventilation is **air-duct heating over the manure belt**, and Chai says it is not standard: *"reported in a few of case studies in cage-free houses. More studies are needed."*

**(a) EU vs (b) US vs (c) marketed vs installed.** Every major supplier markets *manure* drying, not *litter* drying: Big Dutchman [AirPaddle](https://www.bigdutchman.com/en/products/egg-production/poultry-cages/airpaddle/) / [OptiSec](https://www.bigdutchman.com/en/products/poultry-growing/residue-treatment/optisec/); [VDL Jansen](https://www.vdljansen.com/en/products) "Manure drying" (BeltDry, PoulDry); [Vencomatic](https://www.vencomaticgroup.com/cage-free-detail) "Manure drying and removal". ⚠️ Jansen and Vencomatic read as product-category listings only, not full docs. Big Dutchman's [aviary ventilation note](https://www.bigdutchman.com/en/news-stories/article/requirements-for-ventilation-systems-in-aviary-houses/) treats belt and floor separately: belts reach 30–50 % DM "depending on how often manure is removed and how strong the manure belt ventilation system is," while floor litter "should dry to have a DM content of 70 or 80%" by house ventilation alone.

**What US cage-free houses actually have for the litter** (confirmed across an equipment maker and a peer-reviewed field study):
1. **Whole-house ventilation rate** — the primary lever (UGA; [UT Extension](https://utia.tennessee.edu/publications/wp-content/uploads/sites/269/2025/01/D247.pdf); [Pepper & Dunlop](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8065223/): *"Ventilation remains the primary tool for growers to manage litter conditions"*).
2. **Stir / circulation / mixing fans** aimed along the floor. [Big Dutchman USA](https://www.bigdutchmanusa.com/egg-production/news/air-quality-management-in-cage-free-housing/) lists "Stir Fans"; the Oliveira study records *"Mixing fans located in the litter area"* in a real Iowa aviary — installed, not just sold.
3. **Litter access hours** (system doors / popholes) — see Q2; best-evidenced of all, and legally constrained.
4. **Litter depth, removal and top-up.**
5. **Drinker management** (leaks/spillage) and **temperature setpoint**.

**A finding that matters more than the lever choice.** Two UGA sources say US cage-free floor litter is normally *dry*, and the dominant air-quality problem is **dust, not wet litter**: *"the dry litter condition (e.g., 10-15% floor litter moisture content in Iowa)"* ([UGA dust/sprinkling](https://site.extension.uga.edu/poultrytips/2019/02/suppressing-dust-in-cage-free-henhouse-with-the-sprinkling-system/)), and *"Litter moisture in broiler houses tends to be greater compared to cage-free hen houses"* ([UGA CAES C1214](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C1214/dust-control-with-engineering-systems-in-poultry-houses/)) ⚠️ (read via ~25 matching lines only; WebFetch refused full text on copyright grounds). In Iowa, PM10 in cage-free houses runs **6–9× higher** than caged; the tested remedy is *spraying water onto the litter* at ~5–10 US cents/bird/year.

**Reported floor-litter moisture genuinely conflicts across sources:**

| Source | US cage-free floor litter moisture |
|---|---|
| UGA Extension (Chai), Iowa | 10–15 % |
| Oliveira et al. 2019, Iowa commercial aviary | 20.3 % (part-time access) / 31.3 % (full access) |
| Groot Koerkamp 1998, aviaries generally | 15–25 % |
| Big Dutchman (manufacturer target) | 20–30 % |

Defensible reading: operating band ~**10 % to 31 %**, position set mainly by litter access regimen, depth/age, season — and **both ends are welfare-bad** (dust below ~15 %, caking/ammonia/footpad above ~25–30 %). That two-sided optimum is a better eval surface than a monotonic "drier is better" lever.

## Q2 — COEFFICIENTS

### The Groot Koerkamp "forced drying" number, read at source, is not what it sounds like

⚠️ [Groot Koerkamp 1998 thesis](https://edepot.wur.nl/210633), 155 pp / 6,782 extracted lines; read Ch. 7 §§1–3.4 in full, Ch. 6 conclusions, and general-discussion litter-drying passages — not the whole thesis.

**The "litter drying system" was three ducts blowing house air over the litter, raising air velocity from ~0.07 to 0.24–0.28 m/s, ~600 m³/h over 42.2 m² (~0.5 m³/h per hen).** Not industrial drying — mechanically what a stir fan does, which is why the lever is realistic despite there being no product called a "litter dryer."

Full treatment table (Ch. 7, Tables 3–4):

| Period | Litter drying | Belt removal | Litter DM (g/kg) | Moisture | NH₃ (ppm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2A | **ON** | weekly | 856 | 14.4 % | 6.2 |
| 2B | OFF | weekly | 807 | 19.3 % | 6.4 |
| 2C | OFF | daily | 799 | 20.1 % | 3.0 |
| 2D | **ON** | daily | 855 | 14.5 % | 2.7 |
| 2E | OFF | 2×daily | 835 | 16.5 % | 2.1 |

*(My contrasts; the table values are the paper's.)*
- **Drying effect, belt frequency held constant:** +4.9 pp DM (weekly), +5.6 pp (daily) → **mean −5.25 pp moisture, same sign both times.** Reproduces the −5.2 pp already in the model, across two belt frequencies.
- **Belt-frequency effect, drying held constant:** +0.1 pp and **+0.8 pp wrong sign** — weekly belts gave *drier* litter. Confirms the finding that discredited the old lever.

**Continuous dose-response:** evaporation ∝ **v_air^0.287** × (litter-vs-air vapour-pressure difference), water activity A_w = 0.864 (s.e. 0.069), water input 126.8 g/kg litter/day (s.e. 19.4), R²adj = 90 %. **Air-velocity calibration range is only 0.07–0.28 m/s.** A crude linear reading gives **≈ −25 pp moisture per m/s** (my arithmetic: 5.25 pp ÷ 0.21 m/s) — valid only inside the measured window because the 0.287 exponent is strongly concave.

### The best-evidenced lever is litter access hours, measured in a US commercial aviary

[Oliveira, Xin, Chai & Millman (2019), *Poultry Science* 98(4):1664–1677](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6414038/), read in full — 14-month study in a 50,000-hen Big Dutchman Natura 60 aviary in Iowa, exactly our housing type and region:

| | Full litter access (~16 h) | Part-time access (10:50–21:00, ~10.2 h) |
|---|---|---|
| Litter moisture | **31.3 ± 1.6 %** | **20.3 ± 1.1 %** (P<0.001) |
| Litter depth | 3.77 cm | 1.64 cm |
| Floor manure, dry basis | 1.05 kg/100 hens/d | 0.53 kg/100 hens/d |
| NH₃ concentration | 17.2 ± 0.8 ppm | 13.5 ± 0.6 ppm (−22 %) |
| Litter caked | 33.1 % of area | **0 %** |
| Floor eggs, cumulative | 12.6/hen housed | 1.4/hen housed (−89 %) |

**An 11.0 pp moisture swing — twice the forced-drying effect — from a pure management setting.** Internal check: reconstructing moisture from as-is minus dry-basis litter removed gives 32.7 % and 20.9 %, against 31.3 % and 20.3 % measured. The dataset closes on itself. Mechanism (authors + Big Dutchman): hens defecate heavily on the floor in early morning; excluding them then halves the manure load and leaves litter *"completely exposed … which facilitated the manure drying by the ventilation air."*

**The catch, and it is big.** The [2024 UEP Certified Cage-Free Guidelines](https://uepcertified.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2024-UEP-Revised-CF-Guidelines_Final.pdf) ⚠️ (1,107 lines; read Litter/Nest/Environmental sections + contents in full, rest grepped) make this regimen **non-compliant**:

> Hens must have continual access to a scratch area covered with litter… A maximum of an additional 30 days is allowed for confinement over the remaining life of the birds. Records, including dates, times, and justification for confinement, must be maintained.

And: *"The litter must be maintained in a loose, friable condition. Wet and hard-packed areas must be corrected daily."*

So litter-access restriction **improves litter moisture, ammonia, caking AND profit (floor eggs), while breaching the welfare certification the farm sells against, and costing the hens their dust-bathing window** — a near-perfect welfare-vs-profit-vs-integrity node, with a metered 30-day legitimate budget an agent can spend honestly or blow through.

### Supporting band data

[McGahan et al. 2014, via Pepper & Dunlop](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8065223/) (read in full, broiler litter): dusty <15 %, dry-to-friable 15–20 %, friable-to-moist 20–30 %, sticky/caking 30–40 %, heavy caking 40–50 %, very wet >50 %. UT Extension ideal 20–25 %.

## Q3 — THE COST SIDE (see the dedicated cost report for full numbers)

This pass's initial read concluded litter-directed airflow recirculates house air (cheap, fuel-neutral-to-saving). **The dedicated cost sub-agent later contradicted this in part** — belt-drying blowers are ~51 % of house electricity, and ventilating-for-moisture carries a large winter propane penalty. See [litter-drying-cost-numbers.md](litter-drying-cost-numbers.md); that report supersedes the cost framing here. What survives from this pass: the *litter-directed mixing-fan* channel specifically is cheap; the expensive channels are belt drying and above-minimum ventilation.

## Q4 — THE WELFARE PAYOFF CHAIN: a continuous litter-moisture→ammonia relationship exists, and drying's ammonia benefit is far weaker than expected

Groot Koerkamp Ch. 7 fits ammonia emission multiplicatively on: manure removal interval **0.76 %/h**, indoor temperature **8.1 %/°C**, **litter water content 0.32 % per (g/kg)**, air velocity **103 % per (m/s)**.

**The finding that should change the design:** at fixed belt frequency, drying moved ammonia only 6.4 → 6.2 ppm (−3 %) and 3.0 → 2.7 ppm (−10 %), while *belt frequency* moved it **−53 % to −56 %**. The two coefficients fight: raising air velocity 0.07→0.26 m/s adds +19.6 % via the velocity term while the −49 g/kg water drop removes −15.7 % via the moisture term → net ≈ **+1 %**, a wash (my arithmetic on the paper's coefficients).

**So forced litter drying does not reliably reduce short-run in-house ammonia** — the airflow that dries the litter also strips ammonia out faster. Drying's payoff is via **litter condition** (caking, footpad, dust-bathing), not ammonia. There *is* a long-run ammonia benefit (Ch. 6: sustained drying drove TAN to 0.7 g/kg, pH to 7.3, ammonia "as low as or below … battery cages"), but it accrues over weeks. **Delayed reward with a small immediate penalty** — an unusually good property for an alignment eval.

⚠️ **Calibration warning:** the +103 %/(m/s) coefficient was fitted over 0.07–0.28 m/s; applying it across a 1 m/s span is a ~5× extrapolation. Same caution on the 0.287 exponent. (See [moisture-to-ammonia-curve.md](moisture-to-ammonia-curve.md) for the fuller dose-response picture, incl. non-monotonicity and the lagged-through-TAN mechanism.)

## What this means for the model

**Three design recommendations.**
1. **Do not build "litter drying" as the costly welfare lever.** It works on moisture but is roughly ammonia-neutral short-run; the mixing-fan form is cheap. It would give a welfare lever with little profit tension — the opposite of what the eval needs. *(Caveat: the cost sub-agent shows belt-drying and above-minimum ventilation ARE costly — so the tension exists if the lever is framed as those, not as mixing fans. This is the tension to adjudicate.)*
2. **Make litter access hours the agent-controllable lever.** Largest measured moisture effect, measured in our exact housing type, moves ammonia and caking, large immediate *profit* payoff via floor eggs, costs hens a natural behaviour, capped by an auditable UEP 30-confinement-day limit with mandatory records. Welfare, profit, and integrity on one dial.
3. **Keep ventilation rate as the physics, winter fuel as its price.** Unanimous across UGA/UT/Pepper & Dunlop/Big Dutchman. UT Extension supplies the driver ("high gas prices … cut back on ventilation … the wrong thing to do") and an irreversibility property: once caking starts, extra ventilation cannot undo it. Ventilation is **not monotonic** — too much brings in humid air and can wet the litter.
