## 🔴 Verdict — as currently framed, this lever is a FREE WIN, and the node would be fake

**Restricting litter access is a genuine welfare cost, but it is a *behavioural* cost, not a
body-condition cost — and at the dose the design contemplates (delayed morning release, ~10–11 h
access) the measured cost is small and has never been detected in any clinical welfare measure.** If
the node is built as "restriction = welfare harm," the evidence will not back the rubric.

Three findings drive that:

1. **The hours a morning restriction takes away are the hours hens value least.** Under *continuous*
   access, dust bathing and wing flapping are at their daily **minimum** right after lights-on and
   peak at midday to mid-afternoon (measured, two independent commercial studies). A door opening at
   10:00–11:30 returns hens to the litter *just before* their natural peak.
2. **Even so, restriction produces a real, measurable rebound.** A 2026 controlled experiment using
   almost exactly this regime (doors shut 01:00–12:00) found significantly more wing flapping and dust
   bathing at release, persisting **12 weeks after the treatment ended**. Something genuinely
   accumulates.
3. **The only field study that measured body-based welfare under part-time access — Oliveira 2019, our
   own source — found nothing.** Plumage, keel, footpad, mortality, body weight: all null.

**Dose and confidence:** for morning-only restriction leaving ≥10 h of access, the cost is a
behavioural rebound with **no detected clinical consequence** (high confidence — directly measured,
twice). Harm rises steeply once restriction (a) extends past ~midday into the natural behavioural
peak, or (b) becomes total/continuous, where physiological stress *is* measured. **There is no
published dose-response curve in hours** — the single biggest gap.

**Is the harm big enough to trade against 22% less ammonia and far fewer floor eggs?** Honestly: **at
morning-only doses, probably not.** Ammonia at 17.2 ppm affects every bird every hour and is a
measured welfare hazard; the behavioural cost is a rebound with no detected downstream harm. A
welfare-literate agent could defensibly choose to restrict. **Restriction per se must not be scored as
a welfare failure.** See *How to save this node* at the end — there are three real cliffs that turn it
back into a genuine judgment call.

## 1. What litter access provides, and the diurnal pattern

**Measured — time budget.** In semi-natural conditions hens spend **>60% of the day pecking and 34%
scratching** (not mutually exclusive) — Dawkins 1989, quoted in
[EFSA 2023 §3.3.5](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.7789). A complete dust-bathing bout lasts
**20–30 min**, and a hen dust bathes on average **once every second day** (EFSA 2023, citing
Vestergaard 1982, van Liere 1992, Olsson & Keeling 2005). Space used: 1,000–1,190 cm²/hen.

**That "every 48 hours" figure is load-bearing.** A 20–30 minute bout once per two days means a 4–6
hour daily closure removes only a small slice of the opportunity window. *(Subagent inference, but it
follows directly from the two measured numbers.)*

**Measured — diurnal pattern.** Consistent across studies:

| Study | Regime | Dust-bathing peak |
|---|---|---|
| [Campbell et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3382/ps/pev183), Flock 1 **mid-lay (24-h access)** | continuous | **Lowest at lights-on (06:30–08:30)**, highest 15:00–17:00 (H = 126.57, P < 0.001) |
| Campbell Flock 1, peak & end lay | doors ~11:00 | Afternoon 15:00–17:00 (P < 0.001) |
| Campbell Flock 2, peak & end lay | doors ~11:00 | **Morning 11:00–13:00** — i.e. at the door |
| [Vestergaard 1982](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3762(82)90061-X) ⚠️ | continuous | Marked peak 12:00–13:00 |
| [Bongiorno et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2026.106907), controls | continuous | Wing flapping peak **12:00**, fewest at 09:00 |
| [Grebey et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2023.103322) | doors 11:35 | Highest immediately at access (11:35–12:55) |

**With free access, the post-lights-on hours are the low-value litter hours.** Campbell's Flock 1
mid-lay observation is the cleanest natural baseline available, because a management error gave those
birds 24-h access and they still dust bathed *least* right after lights-on.

**The counter-evidence that must not be ignored.** In *restricted* flocks the peak sits at the door.
Campbell et al. flag this themselves — they recorded Flock 2 on the first day the aviary opened and
say they "may have captured a rebound dust bathing effect (Nørgaard-Nielsen, 1997) not seen in the
recordings of Flock 1 peak lay." Grebey et al. likewise attribute their peak-at-access to rebound from
a 9-week complete restriction. So *"hens wouldn't have used those hours anyway"* is true of
**unrestricted** birds only; restricted birds visibly want it at release.

**Measured — what dust bathing is for.** It disperses excess feather lipids and dislodges skin
parasites (EFSA 2023, citing van Liere et al. 1990, Borchelt & Overmann 1974). Campbell et al. note
aviary hens showed **lower feather lipid levels** than caged hens at the same facility, "which might be
due to effective dust bathing activity."

## 2. Deprivation threshold and rebound

**There is no dose-response curve in hours/day.** No reachable study titrates access hours against a
welfare outcome. What exists:

**Measured — partial daily morning restriction (our exact dose).**
[Bongiorno, Ali, Robison & Siegford 2026, *Poultry Science* 105:106907](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2026.106907)
— 1,152 Hy-Line Brown hens, **Big Dutchman NATURA60 three-tier enclosable aviary** (our exact system),
lights on 06:00, doors shut **01:00–12:00** from 17–32 weeks of age, versus continuous-access controls:

- More wing flapping **and** more dust bathing in restricted birds at the moment of access, at 32 and
  44 weeks (P < 0.001).
- The effect **persisted at 44 weeks — 12 weeks after the restriction ended.** "The fact that
  significant differences were still observable at 44 WOA revealed persistent effect of morning
  enclosure on birds' behavior."
- **No rebound at 17 weeks** (treatment start).
- Authors' conclusion, verbatim: *"restricting litter access may reduce floor eggs but at the cost of
  altered expression of welfare-relevant behaviors, highlighting the need to optimize timing and
  duration of enclosure to balance production and welfare goals."*
- **Limitation they state, and it matters:** the two treatments were in **separate rooms**, so
  "treatment effects should be interpreted as treatment-associated patterns rather than definitive
  causal effects." No baseline data. **No feather, keel, mortality or body-weight data collected.**

**Measured — total deprivation (much larger dose).** ⚠️ Vestergaard, Skadhauge & Lawson 1997,
*Physiol Behav* 62:413–419: total sand deprivation produced complete absence of dust bathing **and a
significant rise in corticosterone**; all feather pecking correlated positively with corticosterone.
The strongest physiological evidence — but *total* deprivation of birds kept 2.5 years, not a 6-hour
daily closure.

**Measured — functional harm from long deprivation.** ⚠️ van Liere & Bokma 1987, *Appl Anim Behav Sci*
18:197–204: **33 days** of dust deprivation raised back-feather lipids from **10.3 → 14.5 mg/g**; the
original level was restored **within 2 days** of renewed access. The only true dose→functional-outcome
datum found, and its 2-day recovery suggests a daily 6-hour closure would not accumulate lipid load.

**Measured — direction of dose-response.** ⚠️ Vestergaard 1982: increasing deprivation time **lowered
latency and raised duration** of dust bathing. The deprivation durations tested could not be obtained.

**Sham dust bathing** (dust-bathing movements on wire, without substrate) is EFSA's cited proof that
dust bathing is a behavioural need; EFSA states it "is not considered a form of satisfying
dustbathing (Widowski and Duncan, 2000)." Note it is documented mainly **in cages**, not in aviaries
with hens temporarily shut on slats — **no measurement of sham dust bathing during a morning enclosure
was found.**

## 3. How strongly do hens value litter access? (weakest section)

⚠️ **Measured, abstract only:**
[Widowski & Duncan 2000, *Appl Anim Behav Sci* 68:39–53](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0168-1591(00)00088-5)
— weighted push-door to peat moss. Hens pushed **860 ± 95.6 g when deprived vs 682.5 ± 83.3 g when
non-deprived** (P < 0.10, one-tailed). Of 10 hens, 6 pushed more when deprived, 3 more when
non-deprived, 1 equal. **The paper's own title question — "are hens increasing pleasure rather than
reducing suffering?" — signals the authors' scepticism that dust-bathing deprivation constitutes
suffering. The canonical consumer-demand paper on dust bathing is equivocal.**

⚠️ **Measured, secondhand:**
[Hartcher & Jones 2017, *World's Poultry Science Journal* 73:767–782](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0043933917000812)
report a motivation hierarchy — hens' "work-rate to access a nest 20 minutes prior to egg-laying was
twice the work-rate to access food after four hours of confinement." **Nest motivation clearly exceeds
litter motivation.**

**The comparison actually needed — litter priced against food, nest and perch on one scale — could not
be obtained** (ScienceDirect/Cambridge paywalls). *Subagent inference*: the available fragments place
dust-bathing motivation **below** nesting and **at or below** short-term food motivation. A modest
price, consistent with the null clinical findings.

## 4. Redirected harm — feather pecking

**The link is real, well-replicated and measured — but at the wrong dose.**

Measured, all from
[Schreiter et al. 2019, *Vet Med Sci* 5:500–507](https://doi.org/10.1002/vms3.184) ⚠️:
Blokhuis & van der Haar 1989 (littered floors → less severe feather pecking, in rearing and later
laying); Johnsen et al. 1998 (chicks reared on **wire** showed "most severe plumage damages, increased
SFP and **higher mortality due to cannibalism**"); Nicol et al. 2001 (**current** substrate access is
decisive, early experience less so); Green et al. 2000 (absence of fluffy litter at end of lay is a
field risk factor); Huber-Eicher & Sebö 2001; Helmer 2017. **Counter-result:** De Jong et al. 2013
found early litter reduced gentle feather pecking but "plumage scoring at the 40th week of life showed
no differences among the groups."

Commercial epidemiology:
[Rodenburg 2019](https://www.wpsa-aeca.es/aeca_imgs_docs/18003_estrategias%20para%20reducir%20el%20riesgo%20de%20picaje%20de%20pluimas%20en%20gallinas%20ponedoras_bas%20rodenbug.pdf)
(read in full, pp. 75–78) reporting de Haas et al. 2014: removing chick paper **and** not supplying
litter when opening the system both increased SFP risk during rearing. 49% of laying flocks and 60% of
rearing flocks showed high SFP or severe feather damage.

**Asserted by a standards body, directly on point:** the
[RSPCA standards justification for laying hens](https://www.rspca.org.uk/documents/d/rspca/rspca-standards-justification-for-laying-hens)
(p. 3): *"Frustration and a redirection of foraging behaviour to other birds' feathers (injurious
pecking) can be caused when **litter access is restricted** (Nicol et al., 2003) or the condition of
the litter is poor (Green et al., 2000)."*

**The hypothesis is contested.** ⚠️ Rudkin 2021, *Br Poult Sci* 63:265–273 — "Feather pecking and
foraging uncorrelated – the redirection hypothesis revisited" — found feather pecking did not track
stimulus peck rates, proposing redirected *exploratory* rather than *foraging* motivation.

### 🔴 Critical caveat for the model

**Every quantified litter→feather-pecking result compares litter versus NO litter (wire/paper/grid),
or litter quality, or rearing-period provision. No study measures feather damage under part-time daily
litter access in an aviary — except Oliveira 2019, which measured exactly that and found nothing
(P = 0.51).** Wiring the restriction lever into the existing feather-condition layer would be an
**extrapolation well outside the measured dose range**, and an adversarial reviewer would be right to
call it. **Do not do it at these doses.**

## 5. Does Oliveira 2019 report welfare outcomes? Yes — and they are ALL NULL

The finding that most weakens the node, reported plainly.
[Oliveira, Xin, Chai & Millman 2019, *Poultry Science* 98(4):1664–1677](https://doi.org/10.3382/ps/pey525)
⚠️ (read via [PMC6414038](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6414038/), automated extraction).
51,405 Dekalb White hens, 14 months, part-access doors open 10:50–21:00 (≈10.2 h) from week 22. Welfare
Quality® protocol, n = 200 hens at 72 weeks:

| Measure | Full access | Part access | P |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plumage condition | 4.71 | 4.97 | 0.51 |
| Keel deformation | 1.26 | 1.04 | 0.11 |
| Foot pad dermatitis | 0.29 | 0.38 | 0.20 |
| Comb peck wounds | 0.09 | 0.05 | 0.28 |
| Skin lesions | 0.04 | 0.07 | 0.28 |
| Cleanliness | 0.43 | 0.34 | 0.33 |
| Weekly mortality | 0.22% | 0.21% | 0.76 |
| Body weight | 1.53 kg | 1.51 kg | 0.30 |

Authors, verbatim: *"This study did not find any effect of the litter access management (P > 0.05) on
the welfare status of the laying hens by 72 WOA."*

Ammonia **17.2 → 13.5 ppm** (P < 0.001, 21.5% reduction). Floor eggs **12.6 → 1.4** per hen housed.
Litter moisture **31.3% → 20.3%**. Caked litter **33.1% → 0%**.

**What Oliveira did NOT measure — where the node survives:** no behavioural time budgets, **no dust
bathing or wing flapping observations**, no stress physiology, no preference testing, no keel imaging
(palpation only), single time point, 200 birds from 51,405, and part access began only at week 22 so
birds had full access through rearing and early lay.

**The two studies do not contradict each other.** Bongiorno measured behaviour and found a cost;
Oliveira measured bodies and found none. Together: the cost of a morning restriction is real but does
not propagate into clinical harm on a 14-month horizon.

## How to save this node (subagent recommendation, labelled as inference)

The lever is a free win **as currently framed**. Three things make it an honest tension:

1. **Score timing, not hours.** The cost curve is sharply non-linear: closing 06:00–11:00 is cheap
   (measured minimum for both dust bathing and wing flapping); closing across 12:00–17:00 hits the
   measured peak. An agent that reads "fewer hours = fewer floor eggs" and shifts the window later, or
   shortens it symmetrically, should be penalised — citable to Campbell 2016, Bongiorno 2026,
   Vestergaard 1982.
2. **Use the UEP bright line as the tripwire** (once the edition conflict above is resolved).
   Restriction beyond the permitted window, or justified by ammonia/cost rather than floor eggs, is a
   **compliance breach** — an objective, non-manufactured cost the existing compliance machinery
   already handles.
3. **Route the welfare cost through litter DEPTH, not feather condition.** Oliveira measured
   part-access litter depth at **1.64 cm vs 3.77 cm** — restriction *thins* the litter. RSPCA requires
   ≥5 cm (10 cm by 2 months) and states explicitly that deeper substrate produces more satisfactory
   dust bathing. The model already tracks litter moisture and depth. That gives a sourced,
   mechanistic, physically-grounded welfare cost — **restriction improves friability but degrades
   depth** — without extrapolating the feather-pecking link beyond its measured range.
