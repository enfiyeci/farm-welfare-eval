## Bottom line up front

**Mechanism A (feed timing/storage + least-cost ration choice)** is realistic in its basic shape but the "farm manager decides purchase timing" framing compresses two organizational levels that are usually separate in real operations — storage/milling is plausible at a single-complex scale, but multi-month grain purchase timing is normally a corporate treasury function. Ration reformulation as a lever is well-supported as standard practice; the dollar-savings magnitude is still not well pinned down even after this deeper pass. **Mechanism B (contract-vs-spot egg marketing)** should stay deferred — the evidence is unambiguous that this is a corporate/enterprise-level decision, not something a single complex's general manager sets.

Below is the evidence, with read-depth flags exactly where required.

## Q1 — Feed mill ownership, storage, and who decides purchase timing

The best available real-world proxy is [Cal-Maine Foods' FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000016160/000156276225000170/calm2025053110K.htm) (the largest U.S. shell-egg producer, filed with the SEC — a primary source, not a summary). ⚠️ I downloaded the full filing and read the sections on feed costs, properties, market risk, and customers in depth via targeted extraction; I did not read the entire document sequentially (it also contains full audited financial statements and legal-proceedings detail I did not review).

What it says, in the company's own words:

- "We formulate and produce the vast majority of our own feed at our feed mills located near our production plants." The company operates **30 feed mills** (each rated at "production capacity of 1,000 tons of feed per hour") alongside 49 shell-egg production facilities housing up to 51.8 million layers.
- Storage: "We currently have the capacity to store **215 thousand tons of corn and soybean meal**." Annual company-wide feed requirement was 2.1 million tons (they manufacture 1.9 million tons of that themselves). Dividing storage by daily manufactured volume (1.9M tons ÷ 365 ≈ 5,205 tons/day) gives roughly **41 days of ingredient storage** company-wide — call it four to six weeks, which lines up with the filing's own explanation: "We routinely fill our feed storage bins during harvest season when prices for feed ingredients... are generally lower."
- Purchase timing / forward buying: "We may enter into contracts for future purchases of corn and soybean meal, and as part of these contracts, we may lock-in the **basis** portion of our grain purchases several months in advance... **Ordinarily, we do not enter into long-term contracts beyond a year** to purchase corn and soybean meal or hedge against increases in the prices of corn and soybean meal."
- Who does this: the filing's Item 7A ("Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risks") frames all of this as an enterprise-level treasury/commodity-risk function — it is written entirely in the corporate "we," and the company separately reports "231" employees in "feed mill operations" as a distinct department from the "3,064" in "egg production, processing, and marketing." That organizational split — a specialized milling/procurement function separate from farm-level production staff — is itself evidence that basis-locking and forward-buying sit above an individual complex's day-to-day manager.

A second, smaller-scale data point: [Feed Strategy's coverage of Rose Acre Farms' new grain terminal](https://www.feedstrategy.com/animal-feed-manufacturing/article/15440245/rose-acre-farms-opens-new-grain-terminal-rail-line) describes a rail-served terminal with **four 450,000-bushel bins (1.8 million bushels total, roughly 45,000–50,000 tons)** built to serve grain "for feed that will be made at **nearby farms**" — i.e., centralized bulk storage/logistics feeding multiple production sites, not a bin built at each individual farm. ⚠️ I could not read this directly — both the Feed Strategy URL and the WATTPoultry mirror returned HTTP 403 (bot-blocked); this is a WebSearch snippet only, unverified beyond that summary.

**Implication for the eval's ~590,000-hen, ~90 tons/day complex:** a single complex at this scale having its own on-site mill is plausible (comparable in size to a single Cal-Maine facility, and smaller acquired operations in the 1.2–4.7 million-hen range each came with "a feed mill" per the same 10-K's acquisition notes). Storage of roughly **30–45 days of corn/soybean-meal-equivalent inventory** (at ~90 tons/day finished feed, corn+SBM is typically 80–85% of formulation weight, so on the order of **2,000–3,500 tons of on-site ingredient storage**) is a defensible number to author, grounded in the Cal-Maine ratio. But the *decision to time purchases against a moving price and lock in basis months ahead* is, per the 10-K, an enterprise treasury/procurement activity layered on top of local milling — realistically it would not sit with the complex's general manager alone. If the design wants the player to hold this lever, it's worth being explicit in-world that the player is acting in a composite GM+corporate-buyer role, rather than treating it as an unremarkable local decision.

## Q2 — Least-cost ration reformulation: practice and savings magnitude

Standard-practice claim: **well supported**, though I could not clear the bar the task set (verify a solid savings magnitude beyond search-snippet level). What I found:

- [Engormix, "Economics of Poultry Feeding: From least cost to maximum profit feed formulation"](https://en.engormix.com/feed-machinery/feed-formulation/economics-poultry-feeding-least_a45713/) — an industry-practitioner piece — states plainly that "a good nutritionist will change diet formulations as significant changes in ingredient costs occur." ⚠️ retrieved via WebFetch (tool-summarized, not raw text I reviewed myself).
- [PMC11591515, "Barriers to the Implementation of Max-Profit and Stochastic Feed Formulation Strategies: A Survey of the Australian Poultry Industry"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11591515/) — a real industry survey — found **12.5% of respondents use stochastic formulation and 28.1% use max-profit formulation**, with the remainder on traditional least-cost. That means essentially the entire surveyed industry runs *some* form of cost-optimized reformulation as standard practice; the paper's own framing is that least-cost linear programming "has been a practical approach" for decades. It does not give an update-frequency figure or a savings percentage. ⚠️ WebFetch-summarized, not raw-read by me.
- [PMC11000179, "Teaching the principles of least-cost poultry feed formulation..."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11000179/) — confirmed that "for commercial poultry production, linear least-cost feed formulation models remain the standard method," but explicitly does **not** contain frequency-of-update or savings-magnitude data. ⚠️ WebFetch-summarized.

On magnitude specifically: I found several numeric claims through WebSearch (a Ghana broiler LP study citing ~3% cost reduction, a "two-bin" stochastic-formulation study citing $0.20–$6.47/ton, and unrelated chick-mash/grower LP figures of 30%/19%/14%) — **but I did not fetch or read any of these primary papers**; they surfaced only as WebSearch-tool synthesis of academia.edu/ResearchGate/aensiweb snippets, which is exactly the kind of source the task asked me to move past. I was not able to verify any of them, and none is layer-specific or about the question actually asked (savings from *periodically re-solving* a formulation as prices move, versus the different question of LP-vs-no-LP). ⚠️ These figures are unverified search-snippet content and should not be treated as citable evidence; I'm flagging them rather than including them as if confirmed.

**Net assessment for Q2:** the practice ("recompute the cheapest ration as corn/soy prices shift") is realistic and standard; a specific, defensible $/ton or % savings figure to hard-code into the eval remains unfound after this pass. If a number is needed for scoring purposes, I'd treat it as a design assumption rather than a cited real-world figure.

## Q3 — Realistic layer feed price variability ($/ton, regional, monthly)

This is the strongest result of the sweep. I downloaded and read the actual **Egg Industry Center (Iowa State) "U.S. Egg Cost of Production and Prices"** monthly reports and extracted their region-by-month tables directly (not summarized by an intermediary tool):

- [December 2023 issue](https://www.eggindustrycenter.org/media/cms/Costs_and_Prices_for_December_2023__36ED6C7DE3179.pdf) — Table 3, "Estimated Layer Feed Cost by Region ($/ton) — 2023": **Midwest ranged from $229.23/ton (September) to $307.98/ton (February), 12-month average $266.08/ton.** Regional spread that year ran from $229/ton (Midwest, cheapest region most months) up to $370/ton (Northwest, most expensive). ⚠️ I read Tables 1–3 and the assumptions block in full; I did not review the remainder of the ~686-line document (egg-price and pullet-cost sections) beyond a keyword scan.
- [April 2017 issue](https://www.eggindustrycenter.org/browse/files/28cffd70af7444438952a4a46b2be785/download) — Midwest ran **$174–$180/ton** (Jan–Mar 2017 partial-year data). ⚠️ same partial-read caveat.
- [July 2018 issue](https://www.eggindustrycenter.org/browse/files/8219bad9d4854b719ff344a44f3c0d76/download) — Midwest ran **$173–$201/ton** (Jan–Jun 2018). ⚠️ same partial-read caveat.

Put together, Midwest layer ration cost has moved from roughly **$173/ton (2018 low) to $308/ton (2023 high)** across this stretch of years — close to an 80% swing over five years — and within any single year the Midwest region alone swings 15–35% peak-to-trough (e.g., 2023's $229–$308 range is a 34% swing in twelve months). That's a solid, sourced basis for authoring a moving feed-price series: **a Midwest layer-ration base around $230–$310/ton with plausible year-scale drift down to the $170s in a benign year**, plus the regional spread (Midwest is consistently the cheapest of the five reported regions; Northwest the most expensive, roughly 15–20% above Midwest).

## Q4 — Contract vs. spot: how eggs are actually sold

Two independent primary/near-primary sources converge on the same structure:

1. **Cal-Maine's own 10-K**, directly: "We believe the **majority of conventional shell eggs sold in the U.S.** in the retail and foodservice channels are sold at prices that take into account, in varying ways, independently quoted and certified wholesale market prices, such as those published by **Urner Barry Publications, Inc. (UB) or the USDA**... however, **grain-based or variations of cost-plus arrangements are also commonly utilized**." This is the company describing its own (and the industry's) marketing structure in an SEC filing, not a journalist's paraphrase.
2. **DOJ's Competitive Impact Statement in *U.S. v. Cal-Maine Foods, Inc., et al.*** ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/atr/media/1455881/dl), downloaded and read in the relevant sections) — describes at length how "egg producers... often sell eggs to retailers including grocery stores and restaurants under **contracts for which the price of eggs is based on the daily price quotations published by... Urner Barry**," fed by a small **spot/exchange market** (bids, offers, and trades) that Urner Barry uses to set its daily benchmark — the very mechanism the DOJ alleges Cal-Maine, Hickman's Egg Ranch, and Versova manipulated between June 2022 and March 2025. ⚠️ I read the core narrative sections in full paragraph context but did not review the full 18-page procedural "Explanation of Proposed Final Judgments" section in equal depth.
3. On the actual **percentage split**: [Washington Monthly, "Unscrambling the Price of Eggs"](https://washingtonmonthly.com/2025/04/04/unscrambling-the-price-of-eggs/) states "**only 11 percent of conventional eggs are sold**" on the spot/cash market — implying roughly **89% move under some form of longer-term contract** (UB-indexed, grain-based, or cost-plus). ⚠️ This is itself a secondary journalism source (not a primary USDA/AMS methodology document), and I reached it via WebFetch (tool-summarized) rather than reading the raw article myself — flagging both the secondary-source status and the retrieval method. I was not able to independently corroborate the exact "11%" figure against a primary USDA or Urner Barry methodology document in the time available; USDA's own [Egg Market News Reports](https://www.ams.usda.gov/market-news/egg-market-news-reports) clearly track "contract sales" and "spot market activity" separately (per WebSearch description only — I did not fetch these reports myself) and would be the authoritative place to pin this number down further if it matters for calibration.

Directionally, though, every source I could verify agrees: spot is a thin minority of volume, and the dominant mechanism is exactly what the task described — contracts indexed to a benchmark (Urner Barry), plus grain-based/cost-plus variants.

## Q5 — Who makes the contract-vs-spot decision?

This is a corporate decision, not a complex-manager one, and the evidence for that is clean. In the Cal-Maine 10-K, customer relationships are reported and negotiated at the enterprise level: **"Our largest customer, Walmart Inc. (including Sam's Club), accounted for 33.6%... of net sales dollars for fiscal 2025,"** and the top three customers together account for roughly half of company-wide net sales — these are national account relationships spanning dozens of production facilities, not something an individual complex's general manager sets or varies. The same filing's market-risk disclosure (Item 7A) discusses egg-price and feed-cost exposure entirely in first-person-plural corporate terms. A single complex running "a mid-to-large US commercial cage-free egg-laying farm" inside a larger company (or even as a standalone firm selling to national retail/foodservice accounts) would not plausibly have discretion over what fraction of its output clears through Urner Barry-indexed contracts versus the Egg Clearinghouse spot market — that split is set by national sales/marketing leadership against multi-year retailer relationships, well above a single facility's operational authority.

## Verdict — Mechanism A ("make feed real")

**Realistic in its basic shape, with one framing fix worth making.** Storage and inventory: giving the player weeks (roughly 30–45 days, ~2,000–3,500 tons at this complex's ~90 tons/day scale) of grain-bin capacity to manage against a moving corn/soybean-meal price is well grounded in Cal-Maine's disclosed 215,000-ton storage against ~41 days of consumption, and a single complex at 590,000 hens plausibly has its own on-site mill (smaller acquired operations in the 1.2–4.7 million-hen range in the same filing each came with one). The Egg Industry Center's monthly regional tables give a solid, citable price series to author from — **Midwest layer ration cost around $230–$310/ton in a recent high-price year (2023), versus $170–$200/ton in a calmer year (2017–18)**, with 15–35% swings possible within a single year. Least-cost reformulation as a player lever is realistic as a practice (near-universal in the industry per the Australian survey), though I was not able to find a solid, verifiable dollar-savings magnitude to hard-code — that number should be treated as a design assumption, not a cited fact, unless a stronger source turns up later. The one thing worth flagging to the design: real-world basis-locking and multi-month forward-buying is a corporate treasury function layered on top of local milling, distinct from day-to-day complex management — if the eval hands this lever to the "farm manager" persona, it's a deliberate compression of two real organizational roles into one, worth a line of acknowledgment in the design doc rather than presenting it as an unremarkable local decision.

## Verdict — Mechanism B (contract-vs-spot egg marketing)

**Deferral is correct, and more strongly justified than the design doc may currently assume.** Roughly 90% of conventional egg volume moves under longer-term contracts (Urner Barry-indexed, grain-based, or cost-plus), with only a thin spot-market minority — confirmed by Cal-Maine's own SEC disclosure and by the DOJ's antitrust complaint describing exactly this mechanism. More importantly for a farm-manager simulation: this is unambiguously a **corporate/enterprise decision** — negotiated national-account relationships (Walmart alone is a third of Cal-Maine's revenue) and company-wide sales strategy, not something that varies by production complex. A single complex's general manager, running the kind of farm-management software this eval simulates, would have no realistic lever over what share of the company's output clears spot versus contract. Deferring this mechanism is the right call.
