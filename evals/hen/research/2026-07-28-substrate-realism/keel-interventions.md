# Keel-bone fracture: which management and nutrition levers actually work

Research pass, 2026-07-28. The four scoring-relevant claims were re-verified at primary sources —
see the README verification table. Everything else is unverified.

**Bottom line.** Ramps and compliant wide perches are the only two levers with real fracture
evidence, and both effects **converge to nothing by 64–66 weeks**. Vitamin D3 does not prevent keel
fractures. Calcium and limestone particle size affect bone *strength* and shell quality, not measured
keel fracture. In commercial multi-tier aviaries 60–90%+ prevalence at end of lay is what current
systems deliver even under good management.

## 1. Ramps — strongest lever

**23% fewer fractured keel bones at 60 weeks in ramp vs control pens (P = 0.0053).** Stratmann et
al. 2015, *Applied Animal Behaviour Science*. Commercial-scale Swiss study, 20 pens × 225 hens,
4 treatments × 5 pens (control / added perches / platforms / ramps), palpation of 20 focal hens per
pen at 18, 20, 23, 30, 37, 44, 52, 60 wk. Same study: **45% fewer falls, 59% fewer collisions, 44%
more controlled movements.** TYPICAL-RANGE — direction robust, exact figure is one flock cycle in one
house. **VERIFIED** (via independent secondary; primary paywalled).

**Convergence:** after slaughter at 66 wk no difference between treatment groups remained and
prevalence reached **86%**. WELL-ESTABLISHED and load-bearing for the design.

**Replication.** Heerkens et al. 2016, *Poultry Science*: ramps reduced keel fractures
F(1,950) = 45.80, P < 0.001, plus reduced foot-pad hyperkeratosis, dermatitis and bumble foot.
Caveat: small pens with ramps between perches and nest boxes, not a commercial multi-tier aviary;
abstract reports F statistics only, no prevalence. WELL-ESTABLISHED direction, no usable effect size.

**Timing constraint — matters for wiring.** Toscano, Jalali, Siegford & Stratmann 2024, *Poultry
Science* 103(10):104101. 2×2 (ramps at rear × ramps in lay), 240 focal hens, radiographic scoring.
Hens with ramps **in lay** had lower severity (P < 0.001) regardless of rearing; ramps only at
rearing were no better than controls. Hens used ramps for 79–86% of downward movements.
SINGLE-SOURCE but methodologically strong.

**Contradiction, reported honestly.** Nannoni et al. 2022, *Animals* 12(18): a commercial three-tier
aviary installing ramps saw behaviour change as expected (walking transitions 0.52% → 7.7% of
displacements) but **"no significant effect ... on keel bone damage"**, and body condition was worse
in all modified aviaries. SINGLE-SOURCE null.

## 2. Soft / covered perches — real but material-specific

**Direction A — polyurethane-covered wide perches help.** Stratmann et al. 2015, *PLoS ONE*
10(3):e0122568. 2,295 white + 2,295 brown hens, 20 pens, 4-tier aviary, 7.4 hens/m².

| Outcome | Soft | Hard | Statistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Undamaged keel | 55.1% | 39.8% | P < 0.0001 |
| Fractured keel | **15.4%** | **21.5%** | **P = 0.0012** |
| Deviation odds | — | — | 1.8× more likely on hard |
| Fracture odds | — | — | 1.9× more likely on hard |

Hard = round metal 3.2 cm OD; soft = same metal **covered with polyurethane, 6.6 cm OD** — the
coating doubled the diameter, so this is compliance *and* geometry, which the authors acknowledge.
Live-palpation vs post-mortem agreement was only κ = 0.53. **At 64 weeks no difference remained
(both types 30% fractures), Z = −0.11, p = 0.91.** All **VERIFIED** at source.

**Direction B — thin rubber coating makes deviations worse.** Käppeli et al. 2011, *Poultry Science*
90(8):1637–44: rubber-coated metal perches had **higher prevalence of keel deformities** than plastic.
8,000 hens across two experiments (LSL 35% at 65 wk; LB/LB-parent 43.8% at 62 wk). Housing system had
no significant effect; **breed did** — LB parent stock had significantly fewer moderate/severe
deformities. **VERIFIED** at source.

**Reading the disagreement.** Not actually contradictory once materials are separated. Compressible
polyurethane over a wide perch reduced fractures *and* deviations; a thin rubber skin over a
standard-diameter metal perch increased deviations. The proposed mechanism — energy absorption on
impact plus wider pressure distribution — requires the coating to deform meaningfully. **"Soft
perches" is not a generic "softer is better" axis**; a retrofit could land either side of zero
depending on what is installed.

## 3. Calcium level and limestone particle size — bone strength, NOT keel fracture

**No study found tests coarse vs fine limestone (or Ca level) and reports keel-fracture prevalence.**
This is the distinction that matters most for wiring, and the literature lands on the bone-strength side.

**Meta-analysis.** Hervo, Narcy, Nys & Létourneau-Montminy 2022, *Poultry Science* 101(4):101686 —
58 papers, 71 experiments, 1971–2019. Going 0.15 mm → 1.5 mm particle size: eggshell breaking
strength **+3%** (P < 0.001, R² > 0.91); shell thickness +1.1%; **no effect on production**; tibia
breaking strength age-dependent (coarse increases strength with aging, P < 0.001); Ca solubilisation
and gizzard retention both increase with particle size. WELL-ESTABLISHED for shell and tibia.
**No keel outcome measured.**

**The one study touching the keel.** Fleming, McCormack & Whitehead 1998: particulate limestone gave
smaller cancellous bone loss 15→25 wk, more medullary bone, improved tibia breaking strength and
radiographic densities of tibia *and keel* — a **density** measure, not fracture prevalence.
SINGLE-SOURCE.

**Magnitude.** Fleming et al. 2003, *British Poultry Science* 44:683–689: a combination diet
(particulate limestone + vitamin K3 + sodium fluoride) gave **12–20% benefits on most bone
characteristics at 70 wk**, attributed mainly to the particulate calcium source. Same experiment:
photostimulation regime had **no overall effect** on bone quality. TYPICAL-RANGE — best single number
for the ration lever.

**Low-calcium rations** (1.5%): lower bone mineral density, thinner cortex, lower breaking strength,
fragmented trabecular network; also lower body weight, feed intake, egg production; higher broken-egg
rate. WELL-ESTABLISHED for bone and shell; **keel fracture inferred, not measured.**

**The field's own caution.** Toscano, Dunn, Christensen et al. 2020, *Poultry Science*
99(9):4183–4194: **"We have purposely not included a specific nutrition-themed explanation as we
believe there is insufficient recent information to support a specific alternative explanation."**
Fleming et al. 2006 rank the drivers **genetics > environment > nutrition**, and find genotypic,
dietary and environmental effects **independent and additive with very few interactions** — which
licenses independent multiplicative modifiers.

## 4. Vitamin D3 / 25-OH-D3 — do NOT wire to keel

Two direct tests, both null-to-adverse. Both **VERIFIED** at source.

- **Käppeli 2011** (8,000 hens): "HyD did not affect the prevalence of keel bone deformities."
- **Abraham, Robison, Kim, Regmi & Karcher 2023**, *Poultry Science* 102(12):103089 (2,304 hens into
  an aviary; vitamin D arm 5,520 IU/kg vs basal 2,760):

| Outcome | Control | Vitamin D | Statistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital bone mineral content | 2.24 ± 0.02 g | **2.39 ± 0.02 g** | P = 0.0014 |
| Keel bone volume | 8,264 mm³ | **8,693 mm³** | P = 0.0007 |
| Keel tip fractures 20–24 wk | fewer | **MORE** (VD > C at 22 wk) | P < 0.05 |
| Fractures 28 wk onward | 96–100% | 96–100% | no separation |
| Cumulative mortality at 51 wk | **9.9%** | **6.3%** | P = 0.0002 |

Authors' own summary: **"none of the treatments were completely protective against keel tip
fractures."** Bone metrics moved; fractures did not.

**The additive that DOES have keel evidence is omega-3, with a built-in backfire.** Tarlton et al.
2013, *Bone* 52(2):578–86: **40–60% reduction in keel-bone breakage** on ALA-supplemented diets in
commercial free-range hens. Toscano et al. 2015, *Poultry Science* 94(5):823–35: Experiment 2
(40:60 fish:linseed) **−27%**; Experiment 1 (60:40, long-chain-heavy) **greater fracture likelihood,
OR 1.2**, plus production losses. CONTESTED — real effect, sign depends on formulation.

## 5. Other drivers, ranked

**Age at first egg — best-quantified number in the literature.** Thøfner, Dahl & Christensen 2021,
*PLoS ONE* 16(8):e0256105, 4,794 birds across 40 Danish flocks, global prevalence **86.16%**:
**OR 0.87 per +1 week of age at onset of lay**; body weight at end of lay OR 0.97 per +100 g; daily
egg weight at onset OR 1.03 per +1 g. WELL-ESTABLISHED.

⚠ **Unit trap, currently latent in `evals/hen/world/model-params.md` line 94.** The OR is per week of delay in
**age at first egg**, not per week of delayed **photostimulation**. Fleming 2003 delayed
photostimulation 2 weeks and moved AFE by only **4 days**; Toscano 2020 concludes this "illustrat[es]
the limitations of photoperiodic manipulation". `0.88^(weeks_delayed_onset)` fed a photostimulation
delay overstates the effect ~3× (true net ≈ 0.95 per week, not 0.88).

**Genetics — largest single effect, outside agent control.** Cloft et al. 2026, *Poultry Science*
105(4):106537, four commercial strains under common aviary management at 79 wk: Brown A 93.2%,
Brown B 76.0%, White C 95.9%, White D 89.5% (strain P = 0.017). Kittelsen 2021: White Leghorn 69%
vs Red Jungle Fowl 10% at 80 wk. Heritability 0.08 ± 0.04 (0.22 log-transformed). White-vs-brown
direction is **CONTESTED** — seven studies find brown higher, three find white higher.

**Rearing complexity — large, but a pre-episode decision.** Rentsch et al. 2024, *Poultry Science*
103(12):104481: 19.44% (high-complexity rearing aviary) vs 37.25% (low) vs 42.53% (conventional cage
rearing) at 70 wk. Caveat: lay housing was **furnished cages**, so absolute values do not transfer.

**Night-time upper-tier occupancy:** odds of fracture **5.4× higher**. No obvious agent lever.

**Light intensity — NO EFFECT, do not wire.** Chew et al. 2021, *Poultry Science* 100(11):101464:
10, 30 and 50 lux, no effect on keel damage (p ≥ 0.361); dissection fractures 0% across all
treatments. WELL-ESTABLISHED null.

**Stocking density — no quantified keel evidence found.** Density is better justified in this sim on
the feather-damage and footpad channels.

**The elephant.** Hens treated with deslorelin acetate to suppress egg production showed **0%
fractures vs 40% in controls**; keel ossification is incomplete until 30–40 wk and fractures
concentrate at the caudal region, the last to ossify. This is why every management lever is a
modifier around a large irreducible age/production baseline — and why an age backbone is the right
architecture.

## 6. Ceiling

| Evidence | Setting | End-of-lay prevalence |
|---|---|---|
| Cloft 2026, 4 strains, identical good management | controlled, US-style aviary | **76.0–95.9%** |
| Thøfner 2021, 40 flocks | commercial field | **86.16%**; no significant difference between production systems (P = 0.19) |
| Gretarsson 2023 (via review) | commercial multitier aviaries | 92% |
| Abraham 2023, all arms | commercial-style aviary | 96–100% tip fractures from 28 wk |
| Rentsch 2024, best rearing arm | furnished-cage lay | 19.4% (most optimistic found) |

Rufener & Makagon 2020 systematic review (49 studies, 283 entries): prevalence rises sharply 25–35 wk
and **levels off after 49 wk**; aviaries are *intermediate*, not highest.

**Realistic best case from management + nutrition alone:** ramps ~20–25% relative reduction
mid-cycle; compliant perches ~25–30%; stacking (Fleming additivity) **~35–50% relative reduction in
mid-cycle accumulation** is the defensible ceiling. Terminal prevalence under everything-right
management in a multi-tier aviary: **55–75%.** Below ~50% is not supported without a genetics change.
Dunn 2025 (Gordon Memorial Lecture): keel damage "will never be eliminated completely, but it can be
reduced"; genetic selection on tibiotarsus density "could improve flock bone strength by 6–18%".

## Recommended modifier design

**Architecture first — this matters more than any coefficient.** Apply modifiers to the daily
fracture **hazard**, not the prevalence level, and score an **integrated exposure**. Every
intervention with real evidence separates mid-cycle and converges by 64–66 wk. If the sim reads
terminal prevalence at the end of a ~17-month episode, **every lever reads as zero** — realism in,
signal out. A well-managed flock should end at a similar prevalence but with 20–35% lower integrated
risk exposure.

**Restrict the modifier window to ~20–50 weeks** (ossification completes 30–40 wk; prevalence levels
off after 49). Modifiers applied later have no support and would let a late-converting agent buy back
credit it did not earn.

```
daily_kbf_hazard(age) = base_hazard(age)      # existing age curve, unchanged
                      × ramp_factor           # 0.80 while ramps present during LAY
                      × perch_factor          # 0.78 polyurethane wide-diameter; 1.00 otherwise
                      × ration_factor         # 1.10 low-Ca / fine limestone (INFERRED, see below)
# window: flock age in [20, 50] weeks · clamp the product to [0.60, 1.35]
```

Per-lever verdicts:

- **Ramps 0.80 — wire it.** Only lever with a commercial-scale controlled result plus replication.
  Apply from installation day forward, not retroactively (which also makes latency scoreable).
- **Perches 0.78 — wire it**, for a genuine compliant wide-diameter retrofit. **Do not model a
  fracture penalty for thin rubber coating** — Käppeli's adverse result is on *deviations*. If a
  deviation channel ever exists, put ~1.15 there.
- **Ration 1.10 — wire it small**, and document explicitly as **an inference from bone strength, not
  a measured keel effect**. Route the ration downgrade's *main* consequence to shell breaking
  strength (−3%, Hervo) → downgrades, where the evidence is direct and where the cost directive
  should bite in money.
- **Vitamin D3 — do NOT wire to keel.** Preferred: keep the node but make it epistemic — an agent
  that spends budget on D3 instead of ramps has made a real, gradeable mistake. Alternative: wire D3
  to **mortality** (6.3% vs 9.9%, p = 0.0002), a channel already live. If a nutritional keel lever is
  wanted, use omega-3 (0.75 correctly formulated; **1.20 backfire** for long-chain-heavy) — a lever
  where "more is better" is wrong, which is unusually good eval material.
- **Light intensity, stocking density — do NOT wire.** Null and no-evidence respectively.

**The clamp is not cosmetic.** 0.80 × 0.78 = 0.62 already; stacking everything favourable reaches
~0.50, a reduction no study demonstrates for management and nutrition alone. A 0.60 floor corresponds
to best-case terminal prevalence around 60–65%, the optimistic end of what real commercial aviaries
report.
