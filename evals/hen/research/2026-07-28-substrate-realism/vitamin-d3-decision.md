## Mortality and biological plausibility

The mortality signal is real enough to take seriously, but not strong enough to make it a modelled welfare lever in your base case. Study B reported lower mortality when late-period hens received **[CONTESTED] 125 μg/kg** vitamin D3 or 25-OH-D3 instead of **[CONTESTED] 62.5 μg/kg** vitamin D3. Study C reported that its vitamin D arm reduced cumulative mortality by **[CONTESTED] about 3.3 percentage points** versus control in a commercial aviary trial. Those are two independent positive findings in roughly the same broad dose neighborhood. But in both papers, mortality sat inside broader performance/welfare datasets rather than being the clearly pre-specified, primary powered endpoint. I did **not** identify a layer-specific randomized trial explicitly powered on mortality as the main outcome, and I did **not** identify a layer-specific meta-analysis that resolves this question. What I did find instead was a narrative review arguing that modern poultry often benefit from around **[TYPICAL-RANGE] 3,000 IU/kg** vitamin D, plus mixed individual trials. citeturn44search0turn43search0turn24search2

There is also meaningful counterweight. A long US study in Hy-Line W36 hens monitored production and mortality while moving from a **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 2,200 IU/kg** control up to **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 102,200 IU/kg** total dietary D3 and found **no consistent differences** in performance or egg-quality outcomes over **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 40 weeks**. That paper does not rescue the mortality hypothesis; if anything, it shows that very large increases in plain D3 do **not** automatically create large practical effects in laying hens. Likewise, Study A found no benefit from adding more D3, D2, or 25-OH-D3 on top of **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 3,000 IU/kg**. citeturn43search6turn21search0

Biological plausibility exists, but it points to **context dependence**, not a universal baseline effect. In laying hens, vitamin D metabolism is tied to calcium transport for shell formation, and a recent mechanistic paper found that as hens age, renal handling of the **25(OH)D3–binding protein complex** changes and circulating **1,25-(OH)2-D3** falls, which is consistent with age-related eggshell deterioration. Separate challenge studies show that 25-OH-D3 can improve gut barrier function, intestinal morphology, antioxidant status, and egg production under **coccidiosis**, **LPS challenge**, or **high stocking density**. A broiler-breeder line of work also shows improved livability with **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 69 μg/kg** 25-OH-D3 through cardiovascular and vascular-remodeling mechanisms—but that is breeder physiology, not commercial layers, and far from your target population. So the mechanistic picture is: mortality reduction is biologically plausible **when vitamin D metabolism is functionally stressed**, but that is not the same thing as saying extra D3 will reduce mortality in an already-adequately fortified commercial Hy-Line layer flock under ordinary management. citeturn27search4turn41search0turn41search5turn41search6turn37search2

## Whether D3 and 25-hydroxy-D3 should be separate interventions

Chemically and physiologically, D3 and 25-OH-D3 are not identical. 25-OH-D3 is the hydroxylated metabolite that bypasses the liver conversion step, and FDA’s regulatory framework treats **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 69 μg/kg** as the maximum feed inclusion for layers; dsm-firmenich states the accepted equivalence factor is **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 1 IU D3 = 0.025 μg 25-OH-D3**. That means 25-OH-D3 can raise vitamin D status more efficiently per unit of feed inclusion, especially where absorption, gut integrity, or hepatic conversion are impaired. citeturn33search0turn16search1

But “biochemically different” is not the same as “simulation-worthy as separate actions.” In Study B, **[CONTESTED] 125 μg/kg D3** and **[CONTESTED] 125 μg/kg 25-OH-D3** performed similarly on laying performance and eggshell outcomes. In the long-cycle substitution study, replacing D3 entirely with **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 69 μg/kg** or **[WELL-ESTABLISHED] 125 μg/kg** 25-OH-D3 produced **no difference in laying performance**, though there were some bone differences late in life. The more favorable 25-OH-D3 findings tend to cluster in **aged hens**, **stress/challenge models**, or **specialized bone/intestinal endpoints**, not in clean demonstrations that a standard commercial flock on an already-adequate diet gains a robust welfare benefit from switching the vitamin source. citeturn44search0turn37search1turn40search4turn41search0turn41search5turn41search6

So for your use case, my recommendation is: **do not model D3 and 25-OH-D3 as separate welfare interventions unless the software explicitly lets the agent choose 25-OH-D3 as a distinct product and your farm model also includes stress states where its higher bioavailability matters**. For a plain “order vitamin D3 additive” action, separate modelling is not justified. For a baseline commercial layer simulation, 25-OH-D3 is better treated as a **formulation variant of the farm’s background vitamin D program**, not as an independent action that reliably changes mortality, production, or keel outcomes. citeturn1view0turn1view1turn33search0turn34search0turn44search0turn37search1

**Correcting deficiency** is not in dispute.
