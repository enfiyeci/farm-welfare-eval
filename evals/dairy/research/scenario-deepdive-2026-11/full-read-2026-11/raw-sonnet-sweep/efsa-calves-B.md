## 3. New facts for the scenarios

Calf-contact and separation (C1)
- C1: Separation is easier the older and less milk-reliant the calf is; at 3 to 4 months, gradual weaning off milk or separating the cow from the calf at a different time from weaning (fence-line or nose flaps) reduced stress in calf and cow (Wenker et al. 2022; Loberg et al. 2007). Lines 2727-2730.
- C1: Hazards named for separation stress: separating and weaning at the same time, young weaning age, and changing the calf's social or physical environment at the same moment (move to new pen, loss of peers). Lines 2721-2724.
- C1: Lever: keeping the calf peer group intact after separation gives a social buffer (Rault 2012). If calves are separated before milk weaning, fence-line separation reduced the stress response (Johnsen et al. 2015). Lines 2730-2735.
- C1: Lever: stress was lower when permanent separation and weaning off milk were disconnected (Johnsen et al. 2015, 2018). Lines 2706-2707.
- C1: Measures of separation stress in CCC are vocalisations, heart rate and restlessness; all sensitive, all low in specificity (also rise with hunger, isolation or heat). Lines 2713-2717.
- C1: Harms/costs on the contact side: aggression from other cows towards calves in a shared group (worse with high stocking density, tight space, no calf creep area); calf-calf competition if too many calves per foster cow; cows may kick alien calves trying to suckle (Waiblinger et al. 2020; Wieczorreck & Hillmann 2022). EFSA found no study showing group stress in CCC calves so far. Lines 2741-2749, 2768-2773.
- C1: Levers for CCC design: free calf creep area, high space allowance, avoid dead ends; set calves per foster cow to cow milk yield and suckling length; monitor calf behaviour and weight gain; make sure the foster cow bonds (Perez et al. 1985). Lines 2777-2784.
- C1: Handling: short-time CCC (calves moved to meet cows twice daily) needs regular handling; dam-reared calves with little human contact may be more fearful of humans than artificially reared calves (Waiblinger et al. 2020; Webb et al. 2022); gentle contact in the first 5 days (e.g. bottle-feeding colostrum, assisting the calf to suckle) is especially effective (Krohn et al. 2001, 2003). Lines 2790-2802, 2830-2836.
- C1/C2: In full-time dam contact studies cross-sucking was not observed at all (Fröberg & Lidfors 2009; Roth et al. 2009b) and was rare in restricted suckling (Fröberg et al. 2008); calves suckling an udder cross-sucked less than calves on an AMF. Lines 2406-2411.
- C1/C4: Diarrhoea in CCC is no clearly lower or higher than artificial rearing: in a study of 39 farms, artificially reared calves had more diarrhoea in summer, autumn and winter, CCC more in spring, but farmers' one-year records showed no difference (Hillmann et al. 2019). Lines 2858-2862.
- C1/C4: Lever for CCC: confirm adequate suckling to be sure colostrum was taken (Godden et al. 2019; Lorenz 2021); keep maternity pens clean. Lines 2876-2878.

Milk feeding, hunger and sucking (C2)
- C2: EFSA recommendation (own, 90-100% certainty): about 20% of body weight in milk per day until at least 4 weeks; at least 3 meals a day, by teat; wean gradually over at least 1 week, preferably per calf according to solid feed intake; permanent access to open-surface drinking water because milk does not cover water need. Lines 3538-3541, 3551-3560.
- C2: Milk allowance is a hazard for hunger in AMF systems together with calves per feeder and mixed ages. Lines 2639-2640.
- C2: AMF stocking: with 12 calves per feeder, calves were disturbed 10% of the time in the feeder, with 24 calves per feeder 50% (Jensen 2004); the teat should stay available about 20 minutes after the milk (Lidfors 1993); closable (protected) feeders cut displacement and cross-sucking (Weber & Wechsler 2001). Sharing one teat raises respiratory problems (Curtis et al. 2016) and poor teat hygiene and poor-quality milk replacer are gastroenteric hazards. Lines 2548-2559, 2575-2582, 2618-2619, 2658-2659.
- C2: Cross-sucking levers: teat feeding, a teat barrier between calves' heads, more than one teat per calf on a teat bar (von Keyserlingk et al. 2004), gradual weaning with good solid feed, ad libitum roughage (Keil & Langhans 2001), and confining calves about 20 to 30 minutes after milk (only if a teat is available). Lines 2385-2404.
- C2: Calves fed high milk volumes eat little solid feed, so weaning them is hard; pair or group housing helps through social facilitation (more concentrate eaten when both calves could feed at once; Miller-Cushon et al. 2014). Pair housing raised concentrate intake at 9 L/day for the first 4 weeks but not at 6 L/day (Jensen et al. 2015). Lines 3706-3712, 3697-3700.
- C2: Wider facts: individually housed calves are "not recommended" in EFSA's recommendation unless the calf has dam contact; groups of 2 to 7 from week 1 (Section 3.16 recommendation). Lines 3919-3922. (See group housing below.)

Group housing of calves (not tied to a single scenario; relevant context for C1, C2, C4, C6)
- Recommendation (EFSA own): unless in contact with the dam, calves should be kept in pairs or small groups of 2 to 7 within the first week of life, not in large groups (more than 7) in week 1. Lines 3919-3922; reasoning 3761-3764.
- US figures cited: respiratory disease 16% in pens of 8 or more, 9% in pens of 2 to 7, 7% in individual pens, 100 US dairy farms (Karle et al. 2019); in an earlier US study groups of more than 7 calves had higher mortality, no difference between individual and groups of 6 or fewer (Losinger & Heinrichs 1997). Lines 3846-3851.
- Other group-size data: 14.1% large group, 8.6% small group, 5.4% individual, 122 Swedish dairy herds, heifer calves 0 to 90 days (Svensson et al. 2003); odds ratio 0.69 small vs large groups in 892 calves, 9 Swedish farms (Svensson & Liberg 2006). Lines 3840-3845.
- EKE (EFSA's own, for 6-week-old veal calves): median respiratory disorder prevalence 12% individual, 12% in groups of 2-3, 13% in groups of 4-7, 30% in 12-18, 35% in 30-40; pairs or small groups from week 1 raise infection risk by no more than about 1%. Lines 3861-3866, 3901-3903.
- Immune gap: passive immunity starts fading at 2 to 3 weeks and active immunity is not yet developed, so the gap is about 3 to 7 weeks (Hulbert & Moisá 2016). Lines 3600-3606, 3755-3756.
- Social housing benefits: pair-housed calves less fearful, better reversal learning (Meagher et al. 2015; Gaillard et al. 2014), more positive judgement bias (Bučková et al. 2019), and less stress at weaning and regrouping; individually housed calves with only visual and tactile contact are more fearful and more aggressive after regrouping (Jensen & Larsen 2013, 2014). Lines 3618-3662.
- Space for calves (EFSA's own EKE): at the current minimum of 1.8 m2 per calf in groups, calves show about 10% of full locomotor play; EFSA recommends at least 3 m2 per calf in groups (15% of full play, more relaxed lying) and doubling the individual-pen minimum; a space allowance under 1.8 m2 per calf was linked to higher odds of respiratory disease (Calderón-Amor & Gallo 2020). Lines 4141-4178, 4090-4092, 4110.

Transport and early sale (C6, C4)
- C6: In EFSA's veal model, calves leave the dairy farm at about 2 weeks old; transport at 14 to 21 days coincides with the lowest immunity (2 to 4 weeks) and raises disease risk; commingling adds pathogens and stress. Lines 3790, 3904-3910.
- C6: EFSA recommends avoiding long journeys and not sending calves through auction markets, and fattening at the farm of origin or nearby (EFSA's own). Lines 3556-3558.
- C6/C4: Calves fed whole milk and little iron-rich roughage risk iron-deficiency anaemia (Reif et al. 2019); whole milk has about 0.5 mg iron/kg, hay 250-525 mg/kg DM (INRA 2018); anaemia is linked to weaker immunity, more diarrhoea and respiratory disease, and lower gain below 4.5 mmol/L haemoglobin. Lines 4268-4277, 4384-4385. (Relevant for any holding of bull calves on milk-only diets.)

Heat, ration and staffing (A3, B2, E3)
- A3: EFSA cites an upper critical temperature of 25 degrees C for cattle (EFSA AHAW 2022b); heat load depends on humidity (THI), air movement, cloud, rain, shade and cold drinking water; panting raises risk of dehydration and alkalosis (Sparke et al. 2001). Lines 3393-3407.
- A3: Indicators in calves: respiration rate (high sensitivity, low specificity), 5-grade panting score (Mader et al. 2006), body temperature above 39.5 degrees C. Lines 3412-3424.
- A3/B2: Metabolic heat rises with feed intake, so animals on the highest rations are usually more sensitive to heat; dark coats absorb more heat. Lines 3398-3400.
- A3: Levers: shade, fresh clean cold water at several troughs with enough flow, troughs not in direct sun in hot seasons (calves in outdoor pens). Lines 3435-3437.
- E3: Handling-stress hazards: poor human-animal relationship, lack of stockperson training, large number of animals per stockperson, time pressure and high workload. Lever: training stockpeople improves attitudes and behaviour (Hemsworth & Coleman 2011); quiet, gentle handling and handling facilities without loud noise, sharp turns or bottlenecks. Lines 2818-2828, 2836-2840.
- A2 (heifer analogue only): disturbances in heifer lying behaviour at 1.8 m2 lying area per animal versus 2.7 or 3.6 m2 (Nielsen et al. 1997); aggressive interactions fell at 3.6 m2 compared with 1.8 and 2.7 (Wechsler 2011); at least one cubicle per animal allows synchronous lying, and "more calves than cubicles" is a listed resting-problem hazard. Lines 2942-2944, 3047-3049, 3248-3254. These are weaned calves and heifers, not lactating cows.
- A4.5 (calf analogue only): regrouping is a group-stress source; calves in stable groups gained more and had less disease than in dynamic regrouped groups (Pedersen et al. 2009). Lines 3041-3046, 3757-3759.
