## 3. New facts for the scenarios

**Lameness, injuries and body condition (A1, A2, A3, E1, D2)**
1. FARM lameness thresholds use a 3-point locomotion scale (1 normal, 2 moderate, 3 severe): at most 5% of lactating cows scoring 3 and at most 15% scoring 2. A farm over either limit gets a Continuous Improvement Plan (CIP) with a maximum of three years, not an MCAP. (A1, E1) p.149 lines 10287-10323; p.150 lines 10393-10398. Compare Thomsen 2023's mean of 7.0% severe (A1.1): the average herd would fail the FARM severe threshold.
2. FARM says moderate lameness matters because it is painful and can progress to severe if not evaluated and addressed, which is why both levels are scored. (A1) p.149 lines 10278-10286; FARM, no citation.
3. Lameness harms listed with sources: reproduction and fertility (Tsousis 2022), milk yield (Green 2002; Huxley 2013), time spent feeding (Huxley 2013), increased risk of culling (Huxley 2013). (A1, D1, E4) p.150 lines 10364-10379; refs p.151.
4. Time out of the pen should be under 3 hours a day to protect resting time and cut lameness (Roche 2023). (A1, A2) p.150 lines 10351-10352. A concrete lever for milking-parlor and handling schedules.
5. Low body condition is linked to more lameness, lower reproductive performance, more retained placenta and metritis, and lower milk fat and protein (Randall 2015, 8-year UK herd; Lopez-Gatius 2003 meta-analysis; Markusfeld 1997, Israel). A BCS below 2 "need[s] special, and in some cases immediate, care". (A4, D2, A1) p.147 lines 10173-10176; p.146 lines 10150-10151; refs p.148 lines 10216-10238.
6. Levers FARM lists to raise body condition: less disease, higher planes of milk and colostrum for pre-weaned calves, higher nutrition plane for heifers and cows, lower stocking density so every animal can feed at the bunk. (C2, A2) p.148 lines 10257-10268; FARM, no citation. Body condition at 99% of animals scoring 2 or more is checked for calves older than 2 days, which includes calves that stay a few days. (C6, C2) p.145 lines 10047-10048.
7. Hock and knee injuries: deep, loose bedding (sand or dried manure solids) gives fewer lesions than mats or mattresses alone (Barrientos 2013, Northeastern US and California freestalls; Tucker and Weary 2004; Munksgaard 2005). Risk goes up with herringbone parlors, mattress stalls and short stalls, and in lame, thin or later-lactation cows (DeVries 2020; Roche 2023). Fix list includes feed push-up scheduling to cut lunging that hurts knees. (A1, A2) p.154 lines 10629-10688; p.158 lines 10834-10849; refs p.158. Hock and knee score 3 means a swelling taller than 2.5 cm or any open or bleeding wound; score 2 or less must hold for 95% of lactating cows. p.155-156 lines 10690-10732.
8. Hygiene: 90% of calves, heifers, dry cows and lactating cows must score 2 or less (mud or manure not longer than 5.5 inches on calves and small heifers, 11 inches on large heifers and cows, in a single area). Poor hygiene raises mastitis risk. Hygiene failure brings no corrective action. (A5, E1) p.160 lines 10865-10896, 10912-10914; p.161 lines 10930-11052.
9. Tail injury: "about 40% of the maximal force a human can apply" breaks a mature cow's tail, so breakage is unlikely without rough handling (Laven and Jermy 2020, NZ). Tail injury causes include alley scrapers, doors, parlors, cows stepping on tails, and tail twisting. 95% of lactating cows must have uninjured tails (CIP, three years). Suggested fixes: retrain employees and contractors in stockmanship, larger stalls, lower stocking density. (E3, A2) p.153 lines 10518-10536, 10551-10591; p.152 lines 10479-10496.

**Down cows and euthanasia (D4, D2, D3, C6)**
10. FARM's acceptable ways to move a down cow: sled, belting with reinforced sides, bucket loader or skid steer big enough for the whole animal, sling, palleted forklift with a supportive base. Hip lifts never beyond assisting to stand; no dragging unless a vet decides it is absolutely necessary under a protocol; un-palleted forks are unacceptable. (D4) p.112 lines 7753-7763; p.113 lines 7829-7858. Cited AABP 2020.
11. Secondary damage (muscle and nerve damage from lying) can matter more for recovery than the original cause, and high-quality nursing care improves outcome (Poulton 2016a, b, Australia; McFarlane 2022 scoping review). Care includes feed, water, and periodic rolling and lifting. (D4) p.112 lines 7770-7803.
12. Practical down-cow checklist: treat before asking her to stand; provide traction with sand or bedding; about four feet of front lunge room; know the clinical signs of milk fever; protocol should say when to treat, call the vet or euthanize. (D4) p.115 lines 8053-8081. FARM also recommends a down cow care team and equipment on site. p.113 lines 7879-7881; p.115 lines 8050-8052.
13. FARM treats prompt care as care "applied at the earliest moment possible", recommended "immediately upon discovery", and the evaluator reviews treatment records for timelines of care for non-ambulatory animals. (D4, E1) p.114 lines 7960-7964, 7994-7996.
14. Segregation standard: a separate location for weak or injured animals; in tie-stall barns the sick cow needs an empty stall on each side. No corrective action applies. (D4, A1) p.118 lines 8231-8248, 8264.
15. The euthanasia criteria in the manual also include: non-ambulatory cattle with signs of uncontrolled pain, non-ambulatory cattle not responding to treatment within 24 hours, and conditions where pain cannot be managed medically. Staff should be trained on criteria widely (more people trained to recognise than to perform), so cows are identified early. (D4, D1) p.122 lines 8412-8425; p.123 lines 8474-8481 (euthanasia criteria p.122-123).
16. If a vet or renderer is the primary euthanasia option, the farm must still have at least one trained employee as backup, because the four-hour window may be missed. (D4, D3) p.124 lines 8569-8583. Euthanasia guidelines cited: AABP 2023, AVMA 2020.
17. Approved methods: gunshot of right type and caliber (frontal), penetrating captive bolt plus a secondary step (second shot, exsanguination, intravenous potassium chloride or magnesium sulfate, or pithing), or intravenous barbiturate by a vet. Poll-position captive bolt is not recommended as the primary method (Grandin 2002 return-to-sensibility problems). Confirm death: no pulse or heartbeat on a stethoscope for more than five minutes and no breathing for more than five minutes. (D4, C6) p.124 lines 8625-8644; p.126 lines 8707-8713; p.127 lines 9041-9053. Euthanasia by drug limits carcass disposal because of residues and scavenger risk. p.127 lines 9026-9032; p.130 lines 9192-9196.
18. Carcass disposal options by state law: rendering, burial, composting, incineration, possibly landfill; carcasses go quickly to a designated place away from healthy animals and public view; post-mortem on fresh carcasses recommended. (D4, C6) p.130 lines 9204-9218.

**Procedures and pain control (C5, C6)**
19. Castration and branding pain mitigation is required in the written protocol, but "No corrective action applies" to either standard, so there is no enforcement lever. Castration is not required by FARM. (C5, C6, E1) p.139 line 9816; p.138 lines 9677-9681; p.143 line 10016.
20. Castration science in the manual: earlier is better (first 24 hours to three months), best done with disbudding when calves already get pain control; castration is acutely painful by any method, banding (constriction) is the most painful in the longer term (Molony 1995); local anesthetic (needs a vet order) plus NSAIDs; meloxicam gives pain relief for up to 48 hours after one dose and raises short-term weight gain and feed intake (AABP 2019); NSAIDs in calves castrated at one week or older reduced respiratory disease (Roberts 2018, feedlot). (C5) p.138 lines 9698-9742; p.139 lines 9753-9778; refs p.140. This is a model for the disbudding pain-relief package.
21. Branding: not encouraged; hot-iron branding is more painful than freeze branding, with pain indicators for several weeks and wound pain for at least 2.5 months even with anesthetic or NSAIDs (Tucker 2014a, b; Schwartzkopf-Genswein 1997); never brand the face; only Arizona, New Mexico and Utah require branding (with exemptions). (E1, C5) p.142 lines 9892-9895, 9921-9941.
22. Tail docking: routine docking banned from January 1, 2017 under FARM Version 3; a violation brings an Immediate Action Plan: stop within 48 hours with follow-ups at 3 weeks and 3 months. Medically necessary docking requires vet guidance and a record (cow ID, date, reason). Purchased cows must be documented or the farm gets a corrective action. (E1) p.134 lines 9343-9351, 9362-9379; p.135 lines 9467-9470.
23. Identification: every animal permanently identified with a tamper-resistant individual ID (840 RFID ear tag recommended); heifers, bulls and steers tagged by day three; US Animal Disease Traceability rule (2012) requires official ID before interstate movement for all female dairy cattle and all male dairy cattle including steers. No corrective action, but ID is part of the Treatment Records standard (CIP, three years). Ear tagging can injure calves (Harmon 2023; Hayer 2022). (C6, E4) p.136 lines 9543-9549, 9558-9566, 9595-9602; p.136 lines 9573-9577.

**Evaluation mechanics (E1, E2)**
24. How FARM checks: evaluators score animals by observation, interview staff, review treatment records for promptness and for pain-medication use, look in the drug cabinet for pain mitigation drugs, and check for equipment (down-cow movers, castration tools, euthanasia gear in working order). Action or documentation failures on non-ambulatory care, euthanasia and carcass disposal bring an MCAP due in 9 months (evaluators may shorten it). (E1) p.113-114 lines 7817-7877, 7985-8013; p.128 lines 9096-9107; p.139 lines 9806-9808; p.143 lines 9968-9970.
25. Corrective-action classes across this range: Immediate Action Plan (48 hours): tail docking. MCAP (9 months): non-ambulatory care, euthanasia criteria, methods, carcass disposal. CIP (3 years): body condition, lameness, tails, hock and knee. None: hygiene, calving area, ID, castration, branding, segregation. This sets the penalty a manager faces for each shortfall. (E1) lines: calving area p.108 line 7606; segregation p.118 line 8264; lameness p.150 line 10393; tails p.153 line 10581; hock and knee p.158 line 10788; hygiene p.160 line 10914; tail docking IAP p.135 line 9467; MCAP lines as in fact 24.
26. Calving area standard (clean, soft, dry, well lit, well ventilated) has no corrective action; evaluators check hygiene scores and hock and knee scores of close-up, dry and fresh cows plus ventilation signs; removing old bedding and adding fresh bedding cuts calf illness such as diarrhea and Johne's (Villetaz Robichaud 2016, Canada; Sweeny 2012). Monitor "frequently, especially close to expected calvings". (A4, C4) p.108 lines 7568-7578, 7595-7606, 7624-7625.
27. References on p.107 are the primary studies behind FARM's disbudding standard (text not in my range): AVMA 2014 literature review, AABP 2019 dehorning guidelines, Stock 2013, Stilwell 2009 (caustic paste, local anesthesia with and without analgesia), Winder 2017 (local anesthetic protocols, caustic paste), Winder 2018a (Canadian National Dairy Study, heifer calf management), Winder 2018b (meta-analysis, hot iron), Reedman 2020 (RCT, caustic paste under 9 days), Reedman 2022 (review), DeVries 2020 (Canadian Code scientists' report), Stafford and Mellor 2005 and 2011. (C5) p.107 lines 7461-7542. Winder 2018a is the likely source for how many farms use pain relief, in Canada, not the US.
