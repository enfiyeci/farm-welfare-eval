## 3. New facts for the scenarios

Scenario C1 (separation of calf and cow)
- C1: EFSA's own recommendation: keep the calf with the dam for a minimum of about 24 hours, then house it with another calf; contact should "increasingly" be extended, in future across the whole pre-weaning period; foster-cow rearing is the "second-best alternative". Lines 5532-5538.
- C1: Benefits of contact grow with duration (EFSA conclusions, certainty 66-100%): vitality after some hours (Lidfors 1996; Edwards and Broom 1982; Metz and Metz 1986); weight gain after 4 days; less diarrhoea after about 2 weeks; social competence after 12 weeks. Evidence gap for contact lengths of 3-8 weeks and 4-10 weeks. Lines 5502-5507, 5470, 5491-5492.
- C1: Diarrhoea figures: calves separated at 6 h were treated for diarrhoea 5.5 +/- 1.7 days in the first 4 weeks vs 4 +/- 0.7 days at 1 day and 2 +/- 1.2 days at 4 days (Weary and Chua 2000, Canada). Lines 5463, 5469.
- C1: Disease-risk side of the argument: a systematic review (Beaver et al. 2019) found no evidence that immediate separation controls Johne's disease; hygiene of the calving pen matters more. Yet EFSA expects CCC to facilitate transmission (Vass-Bognár 2022) and notes higher odds of testing positive for MAP on farms using foster cows (Nielsen 2008). Immediate separation can reduce Salmonella transmission because of heavy faecal shedding in the first 24 h after calving (Anderson 2001; House 2001; Holschbach and Peek 2018). Lines 5162-5178, 5462.
- C1: Separation-stress levers: two-step weaning (contact kept but no suckling, via nose flap or fence line) or gradual reduction of contact time; never remove the dam and the milk at the same time; keep the calf peer group intact after separation; separate at an older age (3-4 months) with gradual habituation. Nose flaps can injure the nasal septum if left in too long (Lambertz 2015). Mixed evidence: Enríquez 2010 found no overall reduction in stress, only a different timing (beef calves). Lines 5301-5326, 6173-6177.
- C1: Effect sizes for two-step weaning: beef suckler calves fence-line separated at 6.5 months vocalised about half as much as abruptly weaned calves (Price 2003); dairy calves with fence-line dam contact at 8 weeks vocalised less than behind a solid wall (Johnsen 2015). Lines 5306-5319.
- C1: Dam side: cows have strong motivation to reach their calf even after separation shortly after birth (Wenker 2020); cows separated at birth show lower separation stress than cows that fostered their calf for days or weeks (Stěhulová 2008; Flower and Weary 2001). Few studies; EFSA calls for more research. Lines 5338-5349, 5493-5495.
- C1: Practical CCC variants: daily contact can be full-time, half-day or short periods; foster cows rear 2-3 (up to 4) calves and are usually not milked; foster calves may get less maternal care. Lines 5193-5205. Group stress hazards in CCC: too many calves per foster cow, cow aggression, lack of a calf creep area; measures are matching calf number to cow yield, monitoring calf weight gain, free access creep area. Lines 6177-6182.

Scenario C2 (milk allowance)
- C2: Cross-sucking cumulative prevalence (EKE medians, calves to 12 weeks): full-time dam contact with ad libitum udder milk 1% (90% CI 0-3%); twice-a-day dam contact 14% (4-30%); separated within 24 h, ad libitum teat milk 30% (12-53%); separated within 24 h, restricted teat milk 64% (38-95%). Conclusions text quotes 65% for the last category (inconsistent with 64%; EFSA's own). Full-time contact of at least 6 weeks (95% CI 4-8) keeps prevalence within 10 points of 10 weeks of contact. Lines 5442-5453, 5517-5520.
- C2: Roughly two-thirds of Austrian farmers reported cross-sucking on their farms (Gugatschka 2008; Graca 2016). Lines 5364-5366.
- C2: Levers EFSA lists against cross-sucking in dairy calves (EFSA's own, Table D.5 and D.3): offer milk by teat rather than open bucket; leave the teat in place about 20 to 30 minutes after the milk is drunk; raise milk amount and feeding frequency; stepwise weaning based on solid-feed intake; dam or foster-cow rearing; breed choice. Lines 5984-5987, 6053-6060, 6119-6124.
- C2: Other cross-sucking risk factors (Table 42, hypothesised by the Panel, effect not fully determined): lower fibre in solid feed, insufficient solid feed, fewer teats per calf, larger groups, mixed ages, indoor housing, higher-lactose milk replacer, first-parity dam, no closing gate on automatic milk feeders, some breeds. Lines 5414-5427.
- C2: Prolonged-hunger measures to monitor (EFSA's own): body condition score, number of vocalisations, restlessness (more activity, less lying), attempts to reach inaccessible feed. Lines 5992, 6113-6114, 6146-6147.
- C2: Routine twice-daily monitoring of calves is the listed measure for catching gastroenteric disease early. Lines 6002, 6129, 6154, 6186.
- C2: Feeding poor-quality milk (for example milk from medicated cows) is "not good agricultural practice" but occurs. Lines 5157-5159.

Scenario C4 (colostrum and newborn care)
- C4: EU rule: colostrum within the first six hours of life (Directive 2008/119/EC). Lines 5147-5148. EFSA's measure: 10-12% of body weight, up to 6 h post partum, of high-quality colostrum. Lines 6001, 6128, 6155, 6185, 6190.
- C4: In CCC systems EFSA adds "confirmation of adequate sucking to ensure colostrum intake", because a calf left with its dam may not suckle enough. Lines 6184, 6189.
- C4: Lower failure-of-transfer risk is part of the hazards chain for both diarrhoea and respiratory disease (see C4.3 above).

Scenario C6 (surplus bull calves)
- C6: Weak link only: slaughter-line indicators for white veal calves aged 5-7 months, selected by EFSA's expert scoring (Table 38): body condition (score 3.55), carcass condemnations (3.55), carcass colour (3.00), abomasal lesions (2.95), lung lesions (2.85), bursa swelling (2.70). Abomasal lesions exceed 70% of veal calf abomasa in reviewed papers (Bus 2019). Bursitis reached 53% of calves at the end of fattening (Brščić 2009); risk factors: concrete or wooden slatted floors, space allowance at or below 1.8 m2/calf, worn or slippery floors; bedding protects (Brščić 2011). Lines 4701-4720, 4856, 5053-5058. This describes veal fattening in the EU and does not bear on the days-old bull calf sale.
- C6: Slaughter data understate on-farm problems because calves that died or were treated on farm are missing; EFSA recommends complementing them with on-farm behaviour measures and mortality data. Lines 5096-5098, 5139-5141.
- C6: In French data, calf carcass condemnation (6 million calves, 2016-2020) was mainly peritonitis, pleuritis, emaciation/cachexia, abscesses and haemorrhagic infiltration, and varied substantially by region and slaughterhouse after adjusting for age, sex and breed (Collineau 2022). Lines 4796-4798, 4815-4818.

Scenario C5 (disbudding), peripheral
- C5: Appendix A names de-horning among intentional mutilations that cause soft-tissue lesions (pain, discomfort, distress), and Tables D.1, D.5, D.9, D.11 list "mutilations" among stress factors to reduce when preventing calf respiratory disease. No pain-relief figures. Lines 5681, 5702, 6009, 6132, 6230, 6272.

Scenario E3 (rough handling), peripheral
- E3: For calves in CCC systems EFSA lists handling-stress hazards: injurious events during handling including "being hit", lack of training and knowledge of stockpeople, high animal-to-stockperson ratio and workload, too little human contact in the first week. Measures: appropriate behaviour during handling, gentle human contact in the first 5 days (for example bottle-feeding colostrum and helping the calf suckle), adapted environment, stockperson training. Avoidance distance is the ABM. Lines 6193-6199. Calves, not cows, and no US/FARM rule.

Scenario A3 (heat), peripheral
- A3: For weaned calves in outdoor feedlots, heat stress ABMs are respiration rate or panting, body temperature, open-mouth breathing, sweating. Hazards are high THI, high solar radiation, low wind, lack of shade, lack of cold water or too few water points. Measures: shade or covered pen areas and enough water points. EFSA's own table, no threshold values, calves not cows. Lines 6275-6280.

Other
- General: Cold stress, heat stress, thirst, resting problems and umbilical disorders are rated medium relevance in CCC systems, while locomotion disorders, bone lesions, eye disorders, metabolic disorders are low (EFSA's consensus ranking). Lines 5938-5945.
- General: EFSA's F2F EKE model takes the unexposed population (for example full cow-calf contact) as the reference for "natural" welfare, so its 1% cross-sucking figure rests on an assumption that the unexposed state is optimal. Lines 5760-5771.
