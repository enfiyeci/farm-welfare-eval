## 3. New facts for the scenarios

Corrective-action map (matters for E1, A2, A3, A1)
- FARM has three outcome types: Mandatory Corrective Action Plan (MCAP, maximum nine months, evaluators may shorten), Continuous Improvement Plan (CIP, maximum three years), or no corrective action. [E1, all] lines 570-574 (PDF 12); 1578-1584 (PDF 25).
- MCAP applies to: Cow Care Agreement (570-574), continuing education/training (798-802), VCPR (1276-1280), Herd Health Plan annual review (1387-1391), water access (1857-1861), feed access with BCS benchmark (2050-2054). CIP applies to treatment records (1580-1584).
- NO corrective action is attached to: heat and cold protection (2493), housing characteristics (2701), resting areas (2912), flooring (3044), daily exercise (2267), electrical currents (3195), lighting (3309), ventilation (3489), emergency contacts (1695), antibiotic stewardship residue standards (961-962), and treatment of common diseases protocol (3730). So an operator can leave cows under-cooled, overstocked or poorly bedded without an MCAP; only animal-based measures with a benchmark (BCS) bite. [E1, A2, A3, A1.7]
- Evaluation method for protocols: Protocol Review (written protocol), Action Review (evidence on farm), Matching (do actions match the protocol); evidence includes animals, records, staff interviews, tools. lines 246-271 (PDF 6). [E1]
- Guidelines are reviewed every three years by FARM governance committees. 213-218 (PDF 5). [E1]
- Evaluators are "second-party" (co-op or processor) with third-party verifiers sampling farms; annual items are "not just on the year the facility is being evaluated", so farms work to an evaluation year, but no notice period or announced date is stated in this range. 172-178 (PDF 5); 576-581, 680-687 (PDF 12-13). [E1.2: still no source for advance notice of date]

Heat, cold and cooling [A3]
- Protection standard applies to all age classes: facilities plus mitigation tools (misters, fans, calf coats, shade cloth); shade, fans and soakers are "typically needed for all age classes" in warm and hot conditions. 2361-2368, 2393-2396 (PDF 38). Directly relevant to the "cool every group" tension, although no corrective action is attached (2493).
- Thermoneutral zone: newborn calves 50 to 78 F, adult cattle 32 to 73 F; young calves need protection below 50 F. 2420-2423, 2388 (PDF 38). No primary named beyond the Kadzere and Wagner references at lines 2559, 2537.
- Heat-stress behaviour: aggression or fighting for shade and water (Schutz 2010, 2014); increased respiratory rate, panting, drooling, drop in feed intake (West 2003). 2428-2449 (PDF 38).
- Cold: calf jackets "supplement, but not replace" deep bedding; curtains, windbreaks, barns; take care with newborn wet calves (Nordlund and Halbach 2019; Van Os 2022). 2510-2515 (PDF 39).
- Water on hot days: cows predicted to drink 20.7 gallons a day, 0.7 gallons for every 0.26 gallons of milk, from a review of 55 studies (Appuhamy 2016); requirement rises with temperature (Kadzere 2002). Best practice is continuous access; where freezing prevents it, water to satiation at least twice a day; provide water in return alleys from the parlour. 1798-1808, 1783-1787 (PDF 30); 1937-1939 (PDF 31). Water need not be available at all times under FARM (1762-1767). [A3]
- Ventilation: minimum 4 air exchanges per hour in winter, 40 to 60 per hour in the heat of summer (Dairyland Initiative, no primary study); calf draught defined as air above 60 feet per minute. 3386-3389 (PDF 52); 3498-3501 (PDF 53). [A3]
- Emergency Action/Crisis Plan must exist for potential emergencies including temperature extremes and power failure. 3566-3571 (PDF 55). [A3]

Housing, bunk and resting levers [A1, A2, A4]
- Cattle prioritise lying over feeding when time budgets are tight, show rebound rest after prolonged standing, and lie less on wet bedding or mud (Tucker 2021). 2854-2858, 2870-2872 (PDF 44). [A2, A3]
- Mattresses or mats are associated with higher hock injury than deep bedding (Roche 2023 narrative review); sufficient, clean, dry bedding is the most critical practice for fewer hock injuries (Barrientos 2013, northeastern US and California freestalls). Deep loose bedding is ideal, especially sand for older classes. 2884-2890, 2927-2928 (PDF 44-45). [A1.7, A2]
- Stall-design trade-off: neck rails increase lameness but improve udder and stall hygiene (Bernardi 2009, "stall-design paradox"). Ref at 2783-2787 (PDF 43). [A1.7]
- Flooring: high-traction rubber flooring is desirable in holding areas and transfer lanes; cows walk faster on it than on concrete (Armstrong 2020; Shearer 2007; Rushen 2017). All maternity areas "essential" to have high-traction flooring given more frequent rising and lying in labour (Armstrong 2020; Cook 2004). 2999-3015 (PDF 46). [A4, A1]
- Feeding space levers for competition: feed twice daily rather than once, frequent push-ups, more bunk space (DeVries 2005; Sova 2013), and head-lock barriers reduce effect of aggression for subordinate cows (Huzzey 2006). 2001-2016 (PDF 32). [A2.1]
- Social contact: visual contact with other cattle is a standard; isolation should be minimised, with exceptions when calves are newborn (Whalin 2021), when cows approach calving (Proudfoot 2014), and when animals are ill. 2668-2678 (PDF 41). [C1, A4]
- Nutritional management is key to prevention of transition diseases (milk fever, displaced abomasum, ketosis) in dry and fresh cows. 1962-1965 (PDF 32). [A4]
- Common diseases by farmer report in the US (order given): mastitis, lameness, metritis, ketosis, diarrhoea, milk fever, respiratory disease, displaced abomasum (reference 1 is on a page outside my range; ⚠️ not seen). 3669-3672 (PDF 56). [A4.3 context, no incidence numbers]
- Herd Health Plan must include written protocols for: treatment of common diseases (diarrhoea, DA, ketosis, mastitis, milk fever, metritis, pneumonia, others vet-identified), lameness prevention and treatment, fitness to transport, milking procedure, pest/fly/parasite control, biosecurity, vaccination, plus an Emergency Action/Crisis Plan; protocols translated into staff languages. 3526-3634 (PDF 55). [A1, A4, A5, D2]

Calves [C2]
- Offering water from the first day increases solid feed or starter intake and tends to improve growth (Wickramasinghe 2019); calves drank 0.79 qt of water a day in the first 16 days at a 6.3 qt/day milk allowance in one study, and 1.1 to 2.6 qt/day at milk allowances of 4.2 to 6.3 qt/day across studies (Jensen and Vestergaard 2021 review). Warm water increases winter intake. 1818-1835 (PDF 30). [C2]
- Milk contaminates calf drinking water for the cleanliness test (soiled or contaminated water fails the "clean" standard). 1772-1773 (PDF 30). [C2]
- Calf housing standard: pre-weaned calves must be able to turn 360 degrees both ways without touching the enclosure. 2199-2203 (PDF 36); 2615-2619 (PDF 41). [C1, C2]
- Space for calves raises play behaviour (Jensen 1998, 2000), hygiene and immunity (Calvo-Lorenzo 2016). 2659-2667 (PDF 41). [C2]

Treatment, drugs, records [A5, B1, D3, E2, E4]
- Treatment records must hold eight items (date, animal ID, treatment, condition, dose, route, duration, milk and meat withdrawal times), kept at least 2 years; best practice adds the name of the person treating. Miss it and the farm gets a CIP (3 years). 1415-1460, 1555-1558, 1580-1584 (PDF 24-25). [E4, E2]
- FARM's definition of "drug" excludes vaccines and hormones, so hormone treatments (such as rbST) need not be recorded in FARM treatment records (best practice is to record them). 858-862, 1471-1484 (PDF 16, 24). [B1]
- Residue causes include milking a treated cow into the bulk tank and not diverting milk; sulfonamides other than sulfadimethoxine are prohibited in lactating dairy cattle (FDA); reduced liver/kidney function can lengthen withhold; extra-label use must be extended; keep records at least 2 years (FDA). 876-917, 1048-1050 (PDF 16, 18). [E2, D3, A5]
- Leg bands or other visual markers are suggested to identify treated cows. 988-990 (PDF 18). [E2, D3]
- Medically important antibiotics must not be used where the principal intent is to improve performance (AVMA, AABP, AVC guidance). 1058-1062 (PDF 18). [B2, A5]
- Where euthanasia is difficult for staff, involve the veterinarian "to ensure the procedure is performed in a humane and timely manner". 1226-1231 (PDF 20). [D4]
- Electric prods: "should not be used as standard practice; if needed, reserved for emergency situations"; train all staff in low-stress handling so shocks are not needed. Cattle find shock aversive (Grumett and Butterworth 2022; Pajor 2000) and it causes pain (Lefcourt 1985, 1986). 3140-3142, 3204-3205, 3145-3147 (PDF 48). [E3]
- Disbudding pain medication is mentioned only as an example of a protocol change that should trigger staff retraining ("if there was a change in pain medication for disbudding"). 825-830 (PDF 15). [C5.3 context; no mandatory corrective action stated in this range]

Training requirements [E3, D2, D4]
- Annual documented training (within 12 months, named signatures, date, topic) is required for stockmanship (all age classes), pre-weaned calf protocol, fitness to transport, non-ambulatory animals, and euthanasia, for staff with those duties. Missing it gives an MCAP. 431-471 (PDF 11); 624-664, 740-754 (PDF 13-14); 798-802. [E3, D2, D4]
