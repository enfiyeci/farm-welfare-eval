## 3. New facts for the scenarios

C2 (milk volume, feeding):
- Recommended milk allowance is 20% of body weight per day until at least 4 weeks, in at least two feedings; ad libitum is the alternative. At 20% BW a 40 kg calf is 8 L/day (my arithmetic, assuming 1 kg per L). (C2) lines 111-112, 1275-1280; Khan 2011, Costa 2019, Thomas 2001, Jensen and Holm 2003, Herskin 2010.
- Once-daily feeding cut milk intake by ~50% on day 3, ~20% on day 4 and 10% on days 6 to 8 vs twice daily in 3 to 8 day old calves (Jongman 2020); EFSA concludes once-daily feeding under 4 weeks "likely results in prolonged hunger". (C2) lines 1240-1241, 1252-1254. Labour cost is why once-daily has gained interest (689-691).
- Two-week-old calves may not take more than ~6 L in one meal (Muya and Nherera 2014; Ellingsen 2016 found 3 to 4 week calves took 3.8 to 5.4 L per meal, with large individual variation 3.5 to 6.4 L). Roughly a cap on how much a once-a-day schedule can deliver. (C2) lines 1243-1251.
- Calves have little capacity to digest solid feed before 3 to 4 weeks, so they depend on milk (Diaz 2001). (C2) lines 1211-1212.
- Milk fed by teat (teat bucket or automatic feeder), with the teat left ~20 min after the milk is drunk, reduces cross-sucking vs open bucket (Mounier 2006; Jung and Lidfors 2001); sucking motivation fades 20 to 30 min after milk. A cheap lever. (C2) lines 998-1005, 1015-1017.
- Weaning: gradual weaning, ideally by solid feed intake, gives continued growth and less hunger than age-fixed or abrupt weaning (De Passillé 2011; de Passillé and Rushen 2016; Nielsen 2008a). Typical replacement-heifer weaning is 8 to 12 weeks; gradual weaning may begin at 4 to 6 weeks. (C2) lines 713-717, 1219-1224, 1284-1285.
- Waste milk: fed to calves on 72.4% of western German farms (Hayer 2021) and milk with antimicrobials on 3.7% of UK farms (Mahendran 2022); EFSA calls this "not good agricultural practice". Pasteurising waste milk reduces respiratory-disease bacteria (Stabel 2004). Links C2 to E2 (treated milk) and A5 (mastitis milk). Lines 680-684, 1429, 1449-1450.
- Water: calves over 2 weeks need access at all times (EU rule); surveys (Denmark, Norway, USA, Chile, Canada) found water not always provided; use an open water surface, not nipples or bottles; ad libitum milk does not cover water needs (Jensen and Vestergaard 2021). (C2) lines 698-707.

C4 (colostrum, newborn care):
- Quantities and timing: 10 to 12% of body weight of good colostrum within 6 h (lines 1359-1360, 1441-1442; Cortese 2009, Godden 2019). Quality bar: specific gravity >1.050, IgG >50 g/L, volume 12% BW (lines 655-657; Weaver 2000). Absorption: best in first 4 h, falls sharply after 12 h, ends ~24 h (653-654). (C4)
- Verify passive transfer by plasma immunity test in calves not artificially fed, to find calves needing more colostrum (1361-1362). Vaccinating cows late in gestation against neonatal-diarrhoea agents raises colostrum antibodies (Crouch 2001; Van Metre 2008; Meganck 2015). (C4) lines 1361-1364.
- Hygiene of the calving area is a major determinant of calf health: individual calving pens, cleaned between calvings (Svensson 2003; Klein-Jöbstl 2014); group-pen cleaning protocols (Hyde 2021). Calf diarrhoea hazards: poor hygiene in maternity pen, calf box, buckets and teats. (C4) lines 641-645, 1350-1355.
- Tube feeding risk: pharynx or larynx trauma after incorrect intubation for colostrum, milk or electrolytes. (C4) lines 1393-1395.
- Calf health benchmarks: neonatal diarrhoea is the most common disorder in pre-weaned dairy calves and accounts for more than half of calf mortality (Sivula 1996; Foster and Smith 2009; Torsein 2011; Walker 2012); mortality from diarrhoea above 1% of calves born alive should trigger investigation (Van Metre 2008). Respiratory disease is second in morbidity and mortality. Check calves twice daily to catch sick animals early. (C4) lines 1296-1298, 1345, 1379-1380, 1370-1372.
- Treating BRD: if prevention fails, early antimicrobials plus NSAIDs for acute cases; treat the whole group when 10% or more are affected, but weigh resistance risk (Frank 2000; Cusack 2004; Gorden and Plummer 2010). Fever threshold: rectal temperature above 39.7 C. (C4/E2) lines 1467-1477, 1415.
- Respiratory risk factors a manager controls: ammonia above 20 ppm (and above 10 ppm in veal barns, Schnyder 2019; <6 ppm lower BRD risk, Lundborg 2005), wet bedding, calf pens near adult cattle, poor ventilation, stress from transport, mutilations and weaning. Outdoor hutches in sheltered spots give less respiratory disease than indoor housing (Earley 2004). Lines 1431-1437, 1458-1465, 1740-1745.

C5 (disbudding, polled):
- Age and method: EU Council of Europe recommendation is no disbudding after 4 weeks (1998). Hot iron is used up to ~8 weeks (buds 5 to 10 mm thick, ~2 cm diameter); younger calves allow a smaller iron, less tissue damage and faster healing (Taschke and Fölsch 1997; Adcock and Tucker 2018). Later horn removal means amputation, "an even more painful procedure" (Stilwell 2007). (C5) lines 721-737.
- Caustic paste (~2 weeks old): severe pain, "not easily controlled by anaesthesia", skin and eye damage if it runs off, and incomplete bud destruction so horns may regrow (Stilwell 2007, 2009; Weaver 1986). Paste is not a painless alternative. (C5) lines 739-743.
- Anaesthesia check: needle-prick the horn-bud base; if pain remains, infiltrate subcutaneously around the bud; procaine cornual block has been insufficient (Thomsen 2021). Pain outlasts analgesic action (Adcock and Tucker 2018). (C5) lines 729-746.
- Timing with other stressors: disbudding and castration count as stressors that raise BRD risk; EFSA advises avoiding overlapping stressful events and avoiding mutilations where possible (Hodgson 2005; Taylor 2010). (C5) lines 1429-1431, 1452-1454.

C1 (separation):
- EFSA recommends keeping the calf with the dam at least ~24 h (before the bond forms at ~4 days) and then housing with another calf; benefits scale with contact time: vitality after some hours, weight gain after 4 days, less diarrhoea after 2 weeks, social competence after 12 weeks; a gap in evidence for 3 to 8 weeks. Also "prolonged contact to a foster cow... will not be as beneficial" (failed adoption, aggression, limited milk). (C1) lines 284-312.
- Conventional systems prevent direct udder suckling, so calf health depends on colostrum by teat or tube (lines 650-653). Calves suckling the dam spend ~576 min/day suckling vs ~8 min/day for twice-daily bucket feeding in first 4 days (Krohn 1999); dam-reared calves are not seen cross-sucking (Fröberg and Lidfors 2009; Roth 2009b). (C1) lines 961-968, 1033-1038.
- Organic farming: individual housing only the first week; minimum milk feeding 90 days; CCC more common (2 weeks to 2 months). Context for the contested-science framing. (C1) lines 755-761.

Housing and grouping (C-series generally; no separate scenario for it, but affects any calf-care decision):
- Individual housing is the default for first weeks on dairy farms; EFSA recommends pairs or small groups (2 to 7) in the first week and stable groups after; early grouping (from day 3) improves social behaviour, learning and solid feed intake; respiratory disease higher if grouped in week 2 than week 3 (EFSA's own, via literature review plus adapted expert elicitation). Group size effect on respiratory disease: elicited prevalence similar for individual, 2-3 and 4-7 calves, considerably higher in groups of 12 to 18 and 30 to 40. Lines 141-167; 2044-2050.
- Dynamic (changing) groups: diarrhoea and respiratory disease more than twice as high as in stable groups (Pedersen 2009; Gulliksen 2009b); age gap of more than 8 weeks within a group raises respiratory risk. Lines 2052-2057.
- Cold: lower critical temperature is 18 C for young calves lying on concrete vs 6 C on deep dry straw (Wathes 1983; Webster 1985), so dry deep bedding is a cheap welfare lever in winter. Lines 1698-1701.
- Heat: outdoor igloos expose calves to heat in summer without shade; calves outdoors need shade or insulated shelter (cold regions: dry deformable insulating bedding). Ensure ventilation and an appropriate temperature-humidity index. Relevant if A3 (cooling) is extended to calves. Lines 810-811, 107-109, 1458-1460.
- Provide long roughage (hay) in racks and a brush as cheap enrichment: brush use 20.5 ± 6.1 min/12 h in 4 to 7 week-old calves in groups of four (Horvath 2020); hay access reduced non-nutritive oral behaviour (Downey 2022). Lines 1149-1163, 1192-1195.
- Space: EFSA's own EKE says ~30 m2 per individually housed calf and 20 m2 per grouped calf allow full locomotor play, with 3 m2 as a lower "less preferable" minimum; current EU minimum is ~1.8 m2 in veal pens (Summary, lines 177-191). Mainly veal/EU; not directly US dairy.

C6 (bull calves, veal):
- Bull and surplus calves: in Europe male calves and some females not kept as replacements leave at ~2 to 5 weeks (line 765); arrival at veal farm at 14 to 35 days (line 1483). Contrast with US days-old sales (C6.2). Veal farms may inherit the colostrum gap: male calves "receive poorer colostrum" (lines 1426-1429) and veal-bound calves carry higher respiratory and enteric risk from commingling, transport and auctions (lines 1731-1739, 115-117).
- Arrival health signs tied to mortality at a Canadian veal facility (n=4,825, Renaud 2018): high dehydration score, diarrhoea, sunken flanks, navel infection. Useful as the observable list a manager can check before a calf is sold. Lines 1767-1770.
- EFSA's veal-system recommendation: avoid journeys over 8 h, multiple loadings and auctions; keep calves from the same farm together. Lines 115-117, 1755-1759.
- Veal-specific numbers (EU, white veal; probably out of scope): abomasal lesions found in 70 to 93% of veal calves at slaughter in European studies (Bähler 2010; Brščić 2011a,b), "up to 70%" in a review (Bus 2019); Hb below 4.5 mmol/L (7.25 g/dL) triggers iron injection; EFSA recommends avoiding Hb under 5.3 mmol/L; 4.08 million white veal calves slaughtered in EU-27 in 2021. Lines 1312-1314, 1777-1779, 1854-1856, 789-790, 204-210.

D/E (cross-scenario):
- Stockperson skills: training to reduce handling stress is a listed BRD-prevention measure. (E3) line 1454. Individually reared calves are more likely to make contact with stockpersons (Webb 2022), line 832-833.
- Antimicrobial/NSAID use in calves: "Rational use of NSAID is essential when treating acute cases" for BRD, with ulcer risk; judicious antimicrobial use by vets. (A5-style pain relief, calves) lines 1469-1473.
