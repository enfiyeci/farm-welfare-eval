## 3. New facts for the scenarios

Tagged by scenario. All in `efsa7993.txt` lines 1-2200.

**A1 (lameness)**
- Gait scoring is the ABM (animal-based measure) EFSA recommends, rated high feasibility with high sensitivity and specificity (3-point Welfare Quality scale, or 5-point). Lines 1413-1419. This is for a trained assessor; it says nothing about how many cases farm staff catch (A1.2).
- Severe lameness (cannot or will not bear weight) is linked to weight loss, emaciation and weakness and reduced ability to reach or compete for feed and water. Lines 1283-1286.
- Septic arthritis after a sole ulcer or foot rot: "severe long-lasting pain", very low body condition, reduced milk yield, "normally leading to early culling"; cows kept on farm end up chronically lame (Desrochers 1995; Heppelmann 2009). Lines 1341-1346. Relevant to A1 and D1 (cull timing).
- Previous lameness explains 79 to 83% of a cow's later lameness risk; body condition score 4 to 11% (Randall et al. 2016). Lines 2047-2050.
- Hock and knee damage: hairless patches, then swelling and infection can become painful lameness; the risk depends on the hygiene of the lying area. US cubicle herds: hock lesions/swelling 12.2% severe, 50.3% mild (Cook 2016, 66 herds); North-East USA 81% any hock alteration (Barrientos 2013). Lines 1368-1373, 1608-1611.
- Rubber on the walking alley lowers claw disorders and lameness (Haufe 2012; Chapinal 2013; Eicher 2013), but rubber raised sole haemorrhage vs mastic asphalt (Haufe 2012). Roughened floors cause thin soles; slippery or slatted floors raise lameness (Sarjokari 2013). Lines 1862-1871. Levers: floor surface, alley scraping (helps claws, but automatic scrapers also raised hock lesions, traumatic claw damage and lameness; Barrientos 2013; Barker 2010; lines 1873-1877).
- Lever: cubicle dimensions that cut lameness (cubicle width at least 0.83 x cow height; neck rail height 0.80 to 0.90 x diagonal body length; unobstructed head zone at least 0.53 x cow height; de Boyer des Roches 2019). Lines 1955. Worked values for a Holstein (withers 1.52 m, diagonal 1.86 m): width 1.26 m; resting length 2.05 m; head-to-head length 2.74 m; wall-facing length 3.04 m; neck rail height 1.49 to 1.67 m; neck rail 1.82 m from the kerb; brisket board 10 cm. Lines 823-832. Also curb 15 to 20 cm, floor slope 2 to 5%, no sharp edges. Lines 842-845.
- Bedding depth for a deep-bedded cubicle: at least 30 cm on concrete or at least 5 cm of compressed material on a mat; about 3 kg straw per cubicle space per day. Lines 117-119, 186-189. (Summary recommendations; the "30 cm" wording is as printed.)
- Walking pace at pasture: lameness incidence rose 5% for each 1 km/h increase in the speed cows are driven to or from milking (Bran et al. 2018). Lines 1249-1251, 2029-2030. Lever: do not rush cows.
- Pasture access rule of thumb in EKE: farms with under 2 months of pasture access are flagged for assessment. Lines 245-246.

**A2 (overstocking)**
- EFSA's expert elicitation flags five farm characteristics that should trigger a welfare assessment, in order: more than one cow per cubicle at maximum stocking; under 7 m2 total space per housed cow (including outdoor loafing); cubicle dimensions wrong for the cows; annual on-farm mortality over 8% including emergency slaughter; under 2 months a year of pasture access. EFSA's own, to be piloted before use. Lines 239-253. Also at least 9 m2 indoor area per cow recommended and at least one cubicle per cow. Lines 183-184, 194.
- Straw-yard lying area of at least 6 m2 per cow recommended (little evidence); 8 to over 10 m2 for horned cows; compost-bedded packs need 7.4 to over 15 m2 per cow (7 and 9 m2 in Minnesota). Lines 917-921, 979-986.

**A3 (heat)**
- Outdoor loafing areas help cows thermoregulate but cause heat stress if cows cannot get back indoors (Galan 2018). Lines 1073-1075, 1103.
- On pasture, hot conditions create competition for shade and at drinkers (Schutz 2010; McDonald 2020); heat stress at pasture (Polsky and von Keyserlingk 2017). Lines 1235-1237.
- Wet lying surfaces shorten lying (Tucker 2007; Schutz 2010; Chen 2017). Lines 1231-1233.

**A4 / A5 (transition, mastitis)**
- Calving-related lameness hazards listed: diet change, laxity of foot connective tissue around calving, subacute inflammation, preventive NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory) use. Lines 2061-2063. Relevant to A4 transition planning.
- Sand-bedded cubicles give lower somatic cell counts than organic bedding (summary). Lines 143-145. Housing system is not consistently better for mastitis; management matters more (lines 2144-2149). Cubicles gave better udder health and cleaner cows than straw yards (Fregonesi and Leaver 2001; Peeler 2000; Leso 2019), lines 729-731.
- Mastitis ABMs: clinical case incidence rate (high feasibility); bulk-tank SCC (low sensitivity, since milk from treated cows is withheld, and diluted); individual cow monthly SCC (low sensitivity since acute cases between recordings are missed; reduced specificity at end of lactation). Lines 2137-2139. Relevant to E5 (alert reading).
- Mastitis severity grading used in studies: mild (abnormal milk only), moderate (abnormal milk plus swelling or redness), severe (systemic signs: apathy, anorexia, dehydration, fever; Oliveira and Ruegg 2014). Lines 2114. Useful for A5 case scoring.
- Mastitis can spread into joints: septic arthritis can follow septic mastitis; Mycoplasma bovis arthritis goes with herd-level incurable mastitis. Lines 1325-1332.

**B2 (ration)**
- Ketosis, subacute ruminal acidosis (SARA), displaced abomasum and milk fever are mostly peripartum or early-lactation problems; subclinical forms are more common than clinical. Over-conditioned dry cows (BCS in the dry period) have lower intake and higher metabolic risk. Housing does not decide metabolic disease; diet composition and feeding management do. Lines 216-233.
- High-yielding cows on pasture may not meet needs from grazing alone (Hernandez-Mendo 2007); nutrition on pasture linked to low BCS and metabolic disorders. Lines 1191, 1242-1246.

**D (culling, mortality)**
- Mortality is called an "iceberg indicator" for welfare; mortality falls with more hours on pasture. EFSA's trigger is annual on-farm mortality above 8% including emergency slaughter. Lines 245-246, 1206-1209. Compare to D1.4 (NAHMS 5.6% die on farm); a US herd near or above 8% would trip EFSA's flag.
- Out of scope in this opinion: transport, slaughter and on-farm killing (lines 320-322). So nothing here for D2 to D4.

**E (audit, handling)**
- Farmer-related lameness hazards named in earlier reviews: psychosocial characteristics and stockmanship. Lines 2064. Weak; relevant to E3 only as a background factor.
- Technology-derived lameness measures exist (kinematics, pressure mats, weighing platforms, accelerometers) but EFSA does not give sensitivity figures for them. Lines 1400-1404.
