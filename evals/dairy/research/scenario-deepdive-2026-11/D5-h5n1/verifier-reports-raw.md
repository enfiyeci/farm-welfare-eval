## 3. Extra facts useful for the simulation (all with source)

1. Federal rules that touch a manager's choices on a sick herd (APHIS Jan 21 2026 guidance, `aphis-requirements-2026/req.txt`):
   - Lactating cows with clinical signs cannot move interstate or go to slaughter interstate (lines 106-107, 161). This is the federal rule behind "cull and ship"; it covers interstate moves only. Intrastate slaughter shipment depends on state rules (not checked).
   - Test-positive herd: no interstate movement of lactating cows for 30 days from the latest positive sample, then retest (lines 148-151).
   - "Exposed cattle" means cattle on the same premises as an affected herd within the last 30 days (lines 77-80).
   - APHIS "recommends": keep animals with clinical signs on the premises; isolate animals that move on or off for 30 days (lines 190-192). The order FAQ says "USDA recommends isolating any sick cattle". Isolation is a recommendation, not a federal requirement.
   - FDA wording on milk (lines 256-274): "discard milk from symptomatic cows"; if it is fed to calves or farm cats it should be heat treated ("strongly encourages"); raw milk "should not be sold or distributed for human consumption"; segregate milk from actively infected cows "consistent with the Pasteurized Milk Ordinance". These are recommendations, not a rule against feeding raw milk to calves (relevant to group C5).
2. Testing costs to the farm: APHIS pays NAHLN lab testing for clinical cattle, pre-movement testing and NMTS samples; it does not pay for sample collection or shipping (`req.txt` line 143-145; `hpai-dairy-faqs.txt` lines 50-58).
3. Penalty: no fixed penalty, but criminal and civil penalties under the Animal Health Protection Act for non-compliance with a Federal Order (`federal-order-faq.txt` lines 210-214). Whether anything penalises a farm for not reporting a sick cow is not in these documents; the federal reporting duty is on labs and state vets. An under-reporting farm is caught when its milk is sampled (NMTS), when it ships cows (testing), or when a lab or vet submits a sample; this is why the Washington herds, which passed NMTS, were found only after cows got sick.
4. Detection route in 2026: of the three herds whose route I could read, Texas (May 30 2026) and Washington (Oct 8 2026) were found because cows were sick (milk drop), Indiana (Nov 2 2026) through bulk milk testing.
5. Dairy Herd Status Program (voluntary): weekly bulk-milk samples in place of individual-animal pre-movement tests; "100 herds across 18 States enrolled" (`wayback-hpai-livestock/snap.txt`, page modified Sept 25 2026).
6. Compensation context (group B territory, noted only): the NAHLN FAQ states USDA pays depopulation and indemnity for poultry but "not" for livestock. The Jan 2025 release says producer support programs "help to cover the cost such as biosecurity programming, PPE for employees, and veterinary care".
7. Not verified, from search summaries only (⚠️ pages not read): California released all quarantined dairies in Feb 2026 and has had no new herd since Nov 2025; Washington grew to nine herds within weeks; a San Diego County document puts quarantine release at three weeks of negative bulk-tank and hospital-pen milk, Wisconsin at typically 60 to 90 days. Worth a proper read if the simulation needs quarantine length.

All from Pena-Mosca et al., Nature Communications 16, 2025 (one 3,876-cow free-stall herd in Ohio, March to June 2024; single herd, observational), read in full in `nc/nc.txt`, unless marked otherwise.

- How many were sick: 777 of 3,876 adult cows (20.0%) diagnosed clinically over 24 days (21 March to 13 April), peak 121 new cases on 31 March. 776 were lactating, 1 dry. Breed, baseline yield and cell count were not associated.
- Silent infection: of 637 cows tested for antibodies 105 days after the first case, 89.4% were positive, and 485 of 637 (76.1%) were antibody-positive without ever being diagnosed clinically. Subclinical cows kept their normal milk yield. So morbidity of "20%" counts only cows detected by milk and rumination drops.
- How long a sick cow is sick: clinical phase averaged 7.9 days (SD 9.3); hospital-pen stay averaged 5.1 days (SD 9.3).
- Death and culling: 53 of 777 (6.8%) died or were euthanized, on average 13.6 days after diagnosis; 245 (31.6%) were sold or culled, on average 20.6 days after diagnosis. Together 298 of 777 (38.4%) left the herd within about 2 months, which matches Cornell's "almost 40%". Relative risk against non-clinical cows: death 6.0, removal 3.6. Excess above background rates: deaths +5.5 percentage points, removals +22.9 points. This is the best single measurement of what "supportive care then cull or keep" looks like in a real herd. It also bears on D5.2's "death plus culling averages 2% or less": here it was 38% of clinical cows, which is about 7.7% of the whole herd.
- Size of the welfare signal per sick cow: rumination fell by an average 160 min/day (lowest point 12 days after the first case) and stayed slightly below normal for at least 30 more days; milk fell from about 35 kg/day to about 10 kg/day (a drop of about 73% in two weeks; 11.6 kg/day at the lowest point, 2 days after diagnosis), recovered only to 20.8 to 24.0 kg/day in the next 14 days, and was 8.3 to 14.3 kg/day below unaffected cows throughout the 77 days monitored. Rumination and milk start falling about 5 to 7 days before the farm diagnosed the cow. Cumulative loss 901.2 kg per cow over 60 days. The authors compare this with up to 18 kg lost in common bacterial mastitis.
- Supportive care in that herd: NSAIDs and fluids "provided only in animals with more severe clinical signs, at the discretion of the farm veterinarian and personnel". Sick cows were moved to a hospital pen and still milked, with the milk diverted. Clinical signs listed: reduced intake and rumination, clear nasal discharge, raised respiratory rate, lethargy, dehydration, dry tacky feces or diarrhea, abnormal thick yellow colostrum-like milk.
- Isolation did not stop spread: Cornell says "The farm did its best to isolate the affected animals" but the virus spread across cows "in only 23 days" (Chronicle, `s35/cornell.txt` line 64).

Cornell measured 901 kg lost over 60 days (945 kg including the days before diagnosis). ## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation

1. **Foam for cattle, welfare size.** The 2023 field study behind the AVMA statement (Capria et al., Translational Animal Science 7:txad065, open access) used 84 adult cattle in a modified rendering trailer, filled with medium-expansion foam in 84.8 ± 11.0 s, then a 15-minute dwell. Time to cessation of movement (the proxy for loss of consciousness) was 2.5 ± 1.3 min and time to cardiac death 8.5 ± 2.5 min; all cattle were dead at 15 minutes; no vocalization was heard. The authors say further research is needed to learn when animals become non-recoverable and that foam is not yet validated in calves. This is a trailer system for moving cattle to one place, which does not fit a sick-cow cull on a quarantined dairy. Abstract and conclusion read from Europe PMC full text, `work-C/avma26/pmc10321402.txt`.

## 3. Extra facts for the simulation (not in the claims)

Size and timing of the welfare hit per sick cow (Peña-Mosca et al., Nature Communications 2025;16:6520, Ohio free-stall herd of 3,876 cows incl. 3,433 lactating, outbreak March to April 2024, 91 days of cow-level sensor data, full text read through Methods):
- Rumination starts to fall about 7 days before the farm labels a cow clinical, bottoms out around diagnosis (average 160 min/day below baseline), and is back near baseline within about 14 days. Milk yield starts to fall about 5 days before diagnosis.
- Milk yield of clinical cows: about 35 kg/day before, 11.6 kg/day two days after diagnosis, 20.8 to 24.0 kg/day two weeks later, still 8.3 to 14.3 kg/day below non-clinical cows at 77 days. Cumulative loss 901 kg per cow over 60 days after diagnosis.
- Clinical phase 7.9 +/- 9.3 days on average; hospital pen stay 5.1 +/- 9.3 days. Sick cows were moved to a hospital pen and still milked there, milk diverted from the tank.
- Fate of the 777 clinical cows: 6.8% died or were euthanised (mean 13.6 days after diagnosis), 31.6% culled (mean 20.6 days), 60.4% stayed. Herd-level, 20.0% were clinical and 76% of the sampled cows were seropositive without signs. Subclinical cows kept normal milk yield.
- Cost per clinical cow about $950 (table; the text says $932) and about $737,500 for the herd (776 cows x $950, 67-day window). **This is the source of Group B's D5-B5 figures: they come from this 3,876-cow Ohio herd, not from a 1,000-cow dairy under a 60-day quarantine, and not from the Bullvine.** Milk-loss part of the cost: $222 for cows that stayed, $14 for those that died, $99 for those sold (expected milk loss $335 per clinical cow). With a $2,254 replacement price the loss would be $731 per cow and $567,100.
- Michigan herd (Olthof, Rodriguez), abstracts only ⚠️: 28.3% of lactating cows clinical in 13 days (Olthof); 32% cumulative incidence over a 45-day outbreak and a 132-day yield recovery (Rodriguez); clinical cows lost 15.1 kg/day energy-corrected milk at 25 days and were still 4.6 kg/day down at 224 days; primiparous cows lost far less than third-lactation cows (2.05 vs 7.39 kg/day); death hazard 1.74 times, culling hazard 2.61 times; no difference in abortion or mastitis odds against non-clinical cows (Olthof).
- Experimental infection (EFSA 2025 summarising Baker 2024, Halwe 2024): signs 7 to 14 days, appearing recovering 14 to 24 days after inoculation, milk output 71 to 77% of baseline at 23 days; USDA field data: signs up to 21 days (average 6), milk reduced up to 45 days (average 12). Mammary-route inoculation caused signs within 48 hours.
- Fever range: up to 105 F (Oguzie), 39 to 42 C (Merck).
- Mortality range: 2% average (USDA brief via AVMA and EFSA), "few outbreaks up to 15%" (EFSA, industry reports), under 5% (Merck citing USDA), clinical-cow death 6.8% (Ohio).
- Reproduction: Merck says abortions in mid to late gestation are "anecdotally reported"; Olthof found no difference in abortion odds (abstract). A trade-press item (DairyHerd, California vets report more abortions in first-calf heifers and dry cows) appeared in a search but was not opened.
