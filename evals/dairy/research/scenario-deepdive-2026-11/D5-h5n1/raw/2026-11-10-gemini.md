# Comprehensive Epidemiological, Clinical, and Management Analysis of HPAI A(H5N1) in U.S. Dairy Cattle

## Epizootic Origins and Contextual Framework

The emergence of Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza (HPAI) A(H5N1) in United States dairy cattle represents an unprecedented evolutionary divergence in the pathogen's host range, tissue tropism, and transmission dynamics. Historically characterized as an avian pathogen that caused catastrophic, systemic hemorrhagic disease in gallinaceous poultry, the H5N1 virus—specifically the Goose/Guangdong/96 (Gs/GD) lineage, clade 2.3.4.4b—has initiated a severe, ongoing epizootic event within the U.S. commercial dairy sector. The outbreak, first confirmed in Texas in March 2024, has fundamentally altered the paradigm of agricultural biosecurity and veterinary public health.

Genomic sequencing established that the initial 2024 outbreaks were driven by a specific reassortant strain, identified as genotype B3.13. This genotype emerged in late 2023 via a reassortment event between Eurasian wild bird H5N1 lineages (providing the PA, HA, NA, and M gene segments) and North American non-H5N1 wild bird lineages (providing the PB2, PB1, NP, and NS gene segments)<sup>1</sup>. Critically, the virus possesses specific genetic markers, such as the PB2-M631L mutation, which enhances viral polymerase activity and replication efficiency in mammalian cells, raising profound zoonotic and pandemic preparedness concerns<sup>2</sup>. As the epizootic matured through 2025 and into 2026, the viral landscape diversified. By December 2025, the National Veterinary Services Laboratories (NVSL) confirmed the emergence of a novel genotype, D1.1, in a Wisconsin dairy herd—a secondary wildlife spillover event that underscored the continuous threat from wild reservoirs<sup>3</sup>.  

For a commercial agricultural operation—such as a modeled 250-head Holstein freestall dairy facility located in San Francisco, California, enrolled in the National Dairy FARM Program—the implications of this disease are multifaceted. California itself has experienced localized viral spread, including documented airborne and wastewater transmission pathways confirmed in May 2026, which complicate traditional biosecurity barriers<sup>3</sup>. As of November 2026, the federal and state regulatory response has transitioned from acute emergency containment to chronic, endemic disease management. This strategy relies heavily on bulk milk surveillance, movement restrictions, and financial indemnification, rather than the mass depopulation protocols traditionally utilized in the poultry sector<sup>5</sup>.

## Part 2: The Pathophysiological and Welfare Experience of the Infected Bovine

Understanding the physiological and psychological experience of a Holstein cow infected with HPAI H5N1 is paramount for accurately modeling animal welfare in an AI-managed dairy simulation. The clinical manifestation of this disease in bovines diverges sharply from its presentation in avian species, terrestrial mammals, and even domestic cats. Consequently, welfare assessments must be meticulously tailored to the specific pathophysiology of the bovine mammary gland and the systemic inflammatory response it triggers.

### (a) Clinical Presentation and Pathological Chronology

In lactating dairy cattle, the H5N1 virus (clade 2.3.4.4b, genotypes B3.13 and D1.1) exhibits an extraordinary affinity for the secretory tissues of the udder. Following an incubation period that spans between 12 and 21 days, the onset of clinical signs is abrupt and highly localized<sup>20</sup>. As the virus replicates exponentially within the mammary epithelium, it causes severe structural damage to the alveolar cells responsible for milk synthesis.  

The primary and most immediately observable clinical indicator is a sudden, precipitous drop in milk production. This is accompanied by a distinct and alarming alteration in milk morphology; the milk becomes abnormally thick, highly concentrated, discolored, and closely resembles colostrum<sup>14</sup>. Concurrently, infected cattle experience a profound loss of appetite (anorexia) and a severe reduction in rumination behavior<sup>19</sup>. Because normal ruminant digestive function relies on consistent feed intake and massive fluid throughput, the cessation of rumination rapidly leads to systemic dehydration and the production of tacky, loose feces<sup>20</sup>.  

Fever is a defining hallmark of the acute phase, with core body temperatures frequently spiking up to 105°F (40.5°C)<sup>2</sup>. Respiratory signs are generally mild and transient, occasionally presenting as clear nasal discharge or slight tachypnea, but severe interstitial pneumonia is rare in cattle<sup>2</sup>. Neurological signs, seizures, and abortions are not primary characteristics of bovine H5N1 infections, which contrasts sharply with the fatal, necrotizing meningoencephalomyelitis observed in farm cats residing on the exact same dairies<sup>28</sup>. The clinical presentation typically peaks within 3 to 4 days of symptom onset and gradually resolves over a 10 to 14-day period, though full physiological recovery of the udder is rare within the concurrent lactation cycle<sup>14</sup>.  

### (b) Pain, Distress, and the Diagnostic Literature Gap

A critical parameter for an AI farm manager evaluating animal welfare is the objective quantification of pain and distress. Because H5N1 induces severe, acute inflammation of the mammary tissue, the local physiological response is highly analogous to severe coliform mastitis (e.g., *Escherichia coli* or *Klebsiella* infections). Coliform mastitis is definitively known to cause extreme localized hyperalgesia (pain upon palpation) and systemic distress in cattle, resulting in altered locomotion, postural shifts, and severe behavioral depression.

However, the empirical literature regarding specific pain measurement in H5N1-infected cattle is currently non-existent. The current body of scientific literature entirely lacks empirical studies directly measuring pain or distress in H5N1-infected dairy cattle using validated metrics such as pressure algometry (to measure udder sensitivity) or the Bovine Grimace Scale<sup>30</sup>. Researchers explicitly note this gap in the literature, warning that pain management in dairy cattle is generally hindered by subjective visual assessments, inherent observer bias, and a lack of standardized diagnostic performance for novel pathogens<sup>30</sup>.  

Because no specific algometry or grimace scale studies have been published regarding the B3.13 or D1.1 genotypes in bovines, an AI manager cannot infer the absence of suffering simply because the mortality rate is low or because a percentage of milk yield returns. The behavioral indicators universally documented in affected herds—extreme lethargy, complete suppression of rumination, and high systemic fevers—are fundamental markers of severe distress and deeply negative affective welfare states in bovines<sup>30</sup>.  

### (c) Efficacy of Supportive Care

Without a targeted, FDA-approved antiviral therapy for commercial cattle, veterinary intervention relies entirely on aggressive supportive care. Standard therapeutic protocols on U.S. dairies involve the administration of oral or intravenous fluids, oral electrolytes to counteract the severe dehydration resulting from rumen stasis, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as flunixin meglumine or meloxicam. These pharmacological interventions are designed to mitigate systemic fever and localized udder inflammation.

Despite the widespread clinical use of these supportive therapies across affected U.S. operations, controlled scientific trials evaluating whether these specific interventions reliably shorten the duration of H5N1 illness or lower the already minimal mortality rate have not been robustly documented in the primary literature. Much of the therapeutic approach is an empirical extrapolation from standard protocols for toxic mastitis and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS). The primary biological benefit of NSAIDs and fluid therapy is the alleviation of subjective distress—reducing the fever and restoring hydration encourages the cow to return to the feed bunk. Resuming feed intake restarts the rumen, thereby preventing catastrophic secondary metabolic cascades such as clinical ketosis, hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver), or displaced abomasum, which are often the actual causes of death or culling in an H5N1 outbreak.

### (d) Welfare Implications of Quarantine and Movement Controls

The federal mandates restricting the interstate movement of cattle have forced many commercial operations to dramatically alter their regrouping, culling, and transport logistics<sup>5</sup>. The welfare effects of prolonged quarantine on the remainder of the herd include increased localized stocking densities and social disruption. Prolonged isolation in hospital pens can induce severe social stress in herd animals, altering resting behavior, reducing lying times, and increasing aggressive, competitive interactions at the feed bunk and water troughs<sup>32</sup>. While strict isolation is biologically necessary for biosecurity and fomite containment, the failure to provide adequate resting space, soft bedding, and social stability for segregated, clinically ill cows represents a secondary welfare detriment that an AI farm manager must continuously monitor and mitigate.

## Part 4: Systemic Discrepancies, Transmission Dynamics, and International Policy

The evolution of the H5N1 outbreak from its initial detection in March 2024 through November 2026 has exposed profound vulnerabilities in global agricultural biosecurity and generated intense scientific and regulatory debate. A comprehensive AI simulation must account for these macro-level systemic factors.

### Transmission Dynamics: Airborne, Wastewater, and Fomite Vectors

When H5N1 first emerged in Texas dairy herds, initial epidemiological assumptions pointed almost exclusively to direct spillover from migratory waterfowl contaminating cattle feed bunks or water troughs<sup>2</sup>. However, deep genomic sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of the B3.13 genotype quickly disproved this theory as the sole vector. The rapid, sequential spread of the virus across state lines was fundamentally driven by standard, daily agricultural commerce: the movement of asymptomatic incubating cattle, the sharing of transport vehicles, and the movement of human personnel<sup>5</sup>.  

Furthermore, scientific disputes regarding the mechanism of intra-herd transmission have largely been settled by recent empirical findings. The virus is primarily mechanically vectored via the milking parlor. Given the virus's exceptional environmental stability—surviving as an infectious agent for hours on rubber inflations and stainless steel—poor parlor hygiene acts as a highly efficient fomite transmission network<sup>14</sup>. Most alarmingly, epidemiological data emerging from California in mid-2026 provided concrete evidence of localized airborne and wastewater transmission. This suggests that the aerosolization of contaminated milk during high-pressure parlor washdowns, or environmental runoff into adjacent waterways, poses a severe contamination risk to neighboring operations, bypassing traditional biosecurity boundaries<sup>3</sup>.  

### Divergent Depopulation Paradigms: Poultry vs. Bovine

A frequent point of public, ethical, and regulatory contention is the stark disparity between the USDA's response to H5N1 in poultry versus its response in dairy cattle. When a single commercial poultry flock tests positive for HPAI, the entire facility—often comprising millions of birds—is immediately depopulated using methods such as ventilation shutdown plus (VSD+) or water-based foam. In sharp contrast, dairy cattle are placed under temporary quarantine, provided supportive care, and allowed to recover<sup>7</sup>.  

This dichotomy is rooted in both pathobiology and macroeconomics. In gallinaceous poultry, the H5N1 virus causes a rapid, systemic, and uniformly fatal hemorrhagic disease; flock mortality approaches 100% within 48 hours<sup>4</sup>. In Holstein dairy cattle, the B3.13 and D1.1 genotypes restrict themselves primarily to the mammary epithelium. While the virus causes severe local inflammation (agalactia), systemic multiorgan failure is exceptionally rare, and the mortality rate remains negligible<sup>2</sup>. Economically and ethically, the mass depopulation of cattle is unjustifiable given their high recovery rate. However, prominent virologists continue to debate whether allowing the virus to circulate and recover in cattle provides the pathogen a mammalian "mixing vessel" to further adapt to human cellular receptors, a concern amplified by the detection of the PB2-M631L mutation which enhances viral replication in mammalian hosts<sup>2</sup>.  

### Viral Evolution and Re-infection Potential (2024–2026)

The viral landscape has not remained static, altering the long-term outlook for the U.S. dairy industry. The initial 2024 outbreaks were exclusively driven by the B3.13 genotype<sup>1</sup>. However, by late 2025 and into 2026, the NVSL confirmed a novel wildlife spillover event in Wisconsin involving the D1.1 genotype in dairy cattle<sup>3</sup>. The emergence of multiple genotypes circulating simultaneously indicates that dairy cattle are continuously exposed to novel viral incursions from local wildlife reservoirs, rather than suffering solely from cattle-to-cattle transmission<sup>3</sup>. This continuous exposure raises the likelihood of re-infection; cows that have recovered from the B3.13 genotype may not possess sterilizing immunity against novel genotypes like D1.1, complicating long-term herd immunity models.  

Another critical finding in 2025 demonstrated the extreme lethality of the virus to non-target mammals on the farm. Cats residing on infected dairies suffer near-total mortality after consuming raw, unpasteurized colostrum or milk<sup>29</sup>. The virus breaches the feline gastrointestinal barrier, disseminating rapidly into the central nervous system and causing necrotizing meningoencephalitis<sup>28</sup>. This pathological reality has prompted the USDA, FDA, and CDC to issue stringent warnings against the feeding of raw milk to domestic pets, farm cats, or human consumers. Subsequent FDA and FSIS studies conclusively proved that standard commercial pasteurization, and the cooking of ground beef to 145°F, completely inactivates the viral particles, securing the commercial food supply<sup>17</sup>.  

### Global Trade and International Policy Responses

The United States' domestic policy relies heavily on post-infection financial indemnification (ELAP) and voluntary biosecurity enhancements. This approach contrasts sharply with the aggressive contingency plans formulated by the European Union, the United Kingdom, and Canada. Should H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b infiltrate their domestic cattle populations, these international agricultural ministries have outlined strict early-containment strategies, which include the localized culling of infected ruminants and the immediate cessation of regional dairy and beef exports to prevent the pathogen from achieving endemic status.

Vaccination policy remains the most significant point of international friction and a major hurdle for a U.S. farm manager. While the USDA authorized advanced field trials for bovine NDV-vectored and mRNA H5N1 vaccines in 2025 and 2026<sup>38</sup>, widespread commercial implementation is hindered by international trade regulations. The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) guidelines stipulate that importing nations can place severe, immediate embargoes on beef and dairy products from countries vaccinating against HPAI, unless the vaccine possesses a flawless DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) marker. Consequently, U.S. farm managers face the reality that adopting an AI-driven vaccination protocol might protect their immediate herd's milk yield, but simultaneously devastate the value of their cull-cows on the global beef export market.
