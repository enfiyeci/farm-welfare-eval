## Part 2. Effects on treated cows’ welfare

The **negative health impacts noted above imply animal welfare effects** (mastitis and lameness are painful, etc.), but direct measurements of pain or behavior in rbST-treated cows are scarce.  The meta-analyses found treated cows have **more frequent clinical mastitis (≈25% increase)**【75†L79-L88】 and **lameness (~50% more)**【76†L96-L100】. These conditions (mastitis, hock lesions, lameness, ketosis) are known to cause pain and suffering in dairy cows.  Thus rbST- treated cows can be expected to experience more episodes of painful disease. For example, a cow with clinical mastitis endures udder inflammation, fever and milk clots; a cow with hock swelling or laminitis experiences joint/joint pain and difficulty walking. However, **no study has directly measured pain severity, duration, or animal behavior during those episodes** under rbST treatment. The literature reports only incidence/frequency, not symptom severity. 

Injection-site reactions are another welfare issue. The FDA label acknowledges **redness and swelling at the injection site**: typically a 3–5 cm swelling lasting ~3 days, but in ~10% of cows a 5–10 cm swelling persisting up to 6 weeks (sometimes permanent scar)【12†L288-L296】. These local reactions presumably cause some discomfort, but I found no behavioral study (e.g. observing pain-related changes) after injection. 

Body condition and stress: Treated cows have slightly **lower BCS (poorer condition)**【27†L38-L42】 and, as Health Canada noted, increased metabolic demand (higher appetite)【39†L69-L73】. A lower BCS suggests increased mobilization of body reserves, which can be stressful especially if feed is limited. Studies of energy balance in rbST cows are limited, though one meta noted a small mean BCS drop. We did not find any published work on feeding or lying behavior differences (e.g. increased feed intake, reduced resting), nor on markers of discomfort.  Heat stress might be worse under rbST (higher production raises heat load), but again no direct data in cattle. 

**In summary, treated cows experience more disease episodes (mastitis, lameness) and injection-site reactions; all of these involve pain or discomfort.** No direct behavioral/pain measures are reported in the literature. Authors and regulators note this knowledge gap. As one USDA fact sheet states: producers worry about “animal health” with rbST, and indeed minimal data were collected on cow comfort【47†L45-L50】. We conclude that welfare concerns stem from the higher incidence and longer duration of health problems in rbST-treated cows, even though specific pain scores or distress measurements are lacking.

## Part 4. Additional issues

- **Regulatory/research disputes:** FDA’s 1993 approval and Canada’s 1999 refusal were based on the same or similar data but different judgments.  FDA’s stance was that rbST is safe when used per label【12†L270-L277】; Health Canada saw the adverse health signals (mastitis, lameness, fertility) as unacceptably risky【39†L39-L47】. An independent CVMA panel (Canada) performed meta-analysis and reported substantial increases in disease risk【75†L79-L88】【76†L96-L100】, whereas the FDA’s own JECFA/WHO review focused on human safety and largely deferred to producers to manage cow health. In the EU, a 1999 scientific assessment (CVMP) reportedly found no new human risk, but the EU instead cited **animal welfare** as justification for the ban【100†L91-L99】【101†L35-L43】. 

- **Labeling and honesty:** The FDA’s 1994 guidance (see Part 1 C7) holds that terms like “rbST-free” must be accompanied by “No significant difference has been shown…” and be substantiated by records【110†L150-L158】【110†L163-L166】. Some states (e.g. Vermont) have since mandated clear labeling of rbST use, but this is voluntary at the federal level. Legal challenges (e.g. Monsanto vs. some states) have affirmed the right to use “rbST-free” if qualified. If a farm under contract falsely claims rbST-free, it would breach its marketing agreement; enforcement would rely on contract law and audits (FDA or others could deem the label misleading under misbranding statutes). 

- **FARM Program and record-keeping:** The National Dairy FARM Program (animal welfare audit standard) requires documentation of all drug treatments, including rbST, in a herd health records system. Producers must list rbST use in veterinary records and drug inventories (although the FARM manual does not single out rbST, it covers all hormones). 

- **Manufacturers (2026):** Posilac (sometribove zinc) is now marketed by Elanco (the former Lilly animal health division). It was originally by Monsanto (sold to Lilly in 2008) and Elanco continues distribution. No other rbST products are sold in the US. Elanco’s public materials describe it as improving “cow performance” by boosting milk yield. 

- **Recent reviews (2015–2026):** The scientific consensus has not shifted dramatically. The 2014 meta-analysis【27†L32-L40】 remains a key source for pooled effects. More recent studies focus on specific aspects (e.g. milk fat content, fatty acids【42†L168-L177】) but confirm basic outcomes (higher yield, minor composition changes). Some newer work examines nutritional or breeding alternatives. Notably, no new technique to detect rbST use has emerged – analytical chemistry for rbST in milk remains impractical, leaving reliance on documentation【39†L74-L79】【110†L150-L158】.
