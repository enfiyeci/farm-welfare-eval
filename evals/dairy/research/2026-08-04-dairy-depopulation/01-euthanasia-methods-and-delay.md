**Scope:** US commercial dairy, ~250 milking cows (which places the farm in USDA's "medium, 100–499 cows" stratum — I have pulled that stratum out separately wherever the source reports it, because the size gradient is steep and the all-operations average is misleading for this farm).

## 1. AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals — what applies to cattle

📋 **AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals: 2020 Edition.** Panel on Euthanasia; cattle content authored under Jan Shearer, DVM, MS, DACAW (Iowa State), Lead, Animals Farmed for Food and Fiber Working Group. Canonical URL: [https://www.avma.org/sites/default/files/2020-02/Guidelines-on-Euthanasia-2020.pdf](https://www.avma.org/sites/default/files/2020-02/Guidelines-on-Euthanasia-2020.pdf)

**The 2020 Edition is still current.** The AVMA has since issued a *Humane Slaughter* 2024 edition and a *Depopulation* 2026 edition, but the euthanasia document has not been superseded. The three are deliberately separate, and the euthanasia document says so in its own preface (verbatim): *"recommendations related to humane slaughter and depopulation fall outside the purview of the Guidelines and are addressed by separate documents."* Everything in this section is from the euthanasia guidelines, not the depopulation ones.

⚠️ **Retrieval caveat:** avma.org serves an Incapsula bot-protection page to automated requests. I did not attempt to evade it. I read an Internet Archive capture of the identical PDF instead (12.4 MB, Adobe InDesign 15.0, CreationDate 13 Feb 2020, 121 pp.), and verified its identity against the published panel roster. **I read §S3.1 and all of §S3.2 (Bovids and Small Ruminants, through §S3.2.5) end to end**, plus §I6, §I7, §I8 and §M3.2. I did **not** read the remaining ~100 pages (companion animals, laboratory animals, avians, fish, wildlife), which do not bear on cattle.

### 1.1 Acceptable vs acceptable-with-conditions, for bovids

AVMA's structure for bovids is narrower than most secondary summaries suggest:

| Tier | Method |
|---|---|
| **Acceptable** | Barbiturates and barbituric acid derivatives (IV) |
| **Acceptable with conditions** | Gunshot; penetrating captive bolt (PCB); non-penetrating captive bolt (NPCB, **calves only**) |
| **Adjunctive only** (never sole method) | Exsanguination; pithing; IV saturated KCl; IV saturated MgSO₄; second shot |
| **Unacceptable** | Manually applied blunt trauma to the head (of calves *or* mature cattle); injection of chemical agents into conscious animals; xylazine/α₂-agonist followed by KCl or MgSO₄; drowning; air embolism; electrocution with 120 V; exsanguination of conscious animals; puntilla |

📋 **AABP Guidelines for the Humane Euthanasia of Cattle, March 2025** (American Association of Bovine Practitioners) — [https://www.aabp.org/Resources/AABP_Guidelines/EUTHANASIA-2025.pdf](https://www.aabp.org/Resources/AABP_Guidelines/EUTHANASIA-2025.pdf) — **read in full, all 15 pages including references.** AABP presents the same content as **three primary methods**: barbiturate overdose, gunshot, and PCB-plus-adjunct. AABP adds that non-penetrating captive bolt is usable for **neonates and calves under 2–3 months** with an adjunct, and adds **intrathecal lidocaine** (after full anesthesia) as an adjunctive method — a method the 2020 AVMA guidelines discuss only for horses. AABP also states that barbiturate overdose *"be a euthanasia tool of last resort"* on carcass-disposal and scavenger-toxicity grounds — a stronger position than AVMA takes.

### 1.2 Penetrating captive bolt — placement, power, restraint

**Placement (identical in AVMA and AABP):** the point of entry is the **intersection of two imaginary lines, each drawn from the lateral canthus (outer corner) of one eye to the centre of the base of the opposite horn** (or the equivalent position in polled animals). AVMA gives an alternative for long-faced cattle or young stock: a midline point **halfway between the top of the poll and an imaginary line connecting the outer corners of the eyes.**

**Power.** AVMA: *"Bolt velocities of 55 to 58 m/s are desirable for effective captive bolt use in packing plants… Recommended minimum bolt velocities proposed for shooting bulls are as high as 70 m/s."* Powder-activated guns come in 9 mm, .22 and .25 calibre. AABP adds that **cartridge strength varies by manufacturer** and the correct type and strength for the animal's size must be used, and that cartridges must be stored cool and dry — *"Exposing cartridges to moisture can affect burning of the propellant and thus lower the bolt speed and penetrating force."* AVMA names **lack of maintenance as "a major cause of captive bolt gun failure"** for both powder and pneumatic guns, and notes repeated firing over extended periods reduces effectiveness through overheating.

**Restraint.** This is the operationally decisive difference between the two physical methods, and the guidelines are explicit and opposite:
- **PCB: the muzzle must be held firmly *against* the skull**, and *"the animal must be restrained for accurate placement."* AABP: captive bolt *"require[s] excellent restraint of the animal."* AVMA adds that once restrained, discharge should follow *"with little or no delay so that animal distress is minimized."*
- **Firearm: the muzzle must NEVER be held against the head.** Both documents give the same reason — barrel obstruction can cause the barrel to explode. AVMA/AABP specify a handgun be held within 1–2 feet of the target.

**Pneumatic captive bolt guns are limited to slaughter-plant environments** (AVMA); powder-charge models are the farm tool.

### 1.3 The adjunctive-step requirement — when, and why

**For PCB in cattle the adjunct is mandatory, not optional.** AVMA: *"At the present time, an adjunctive method such as exsanguination, pithing, or the IV injection of a saturated solution of potassium chloride is recommended to ensure death when PCB is used."* AABP is firmer: PCB *"must not be used as the sole method of euthanasia."*

The empirical basis, quoted by AVMA from Grandin's slaughter-plant work: *"In a study of 1,826 fed steers and heifers, 3 (0.16%) had signs of a return to sensibility or consciousness… Results were similar in observations of 692 bulls and cows where 8 (1.2%) animals had signs consistent with a return to consciousness."* AVMA attributes those failures to damp storage of cartridges, poor firing-pin maintenance, inexperienced operators using the incorrect anatomic site, dirty triggers causing misfires, and thick heavy skulls in cows and bulls.

⚠️ **Setting caveat that matters for a farm rubric:** those two denominators (1,826 and 692) are **commercial slaughter plants**, not farms — trained dedicated stunners, purpose-built restraint, monitored bolt velocity. They are a *floor* on the on-farm failure rate, not an estimate of it. See §4.3.

**Sequencing rule (both documents):** the animal must be confirmed unconscious *before* any adjunct is applied. AABP: *"Secondary or adjunctive euthanasia methods must not be used until the animal has been determined to be unconscious."* KCl and MgSO₄ stop the heart or paralyse respiration and are described as *"very inhumane"* if given to a conscious or inadequately anaesthetised animal.

**Signs of unconsciousness** (AABP): absence of corneal reflex; absence of vocalisation; absence of gag reflex; lack of rhythmic respiration; no coordinated attempt to rise or right itself. AVMA adds: immediate collapse; brief tetanic spasms followed by uncoordinated hind-limb movements; glazed/glassy eyes not rotated. **AVMA warns explicitly that a present corneal reflex "is highly suggestive that an animal is still conscious."**

**Second shot is not discretionary:** AABP — *"If the first shot does not lead to immediate unconsciousness, a second shot in the original frontal or poll location is required immediately and is not optional."*

**Adjunct doses:** KCl — AVMA gives 120–250 mL of saturated solution, *"administered until death is assured,"* with 3–4 pre-loaded 60 mL syringes and 14–16 g needles prepared in advance. AABP gives 250 mL for most mature cows, typical concentration 75–100 mg/kg BW. MgSO₄ — AABP: ~500 mL saturated solution kills most animals ≤450 kg; death is slower than with KCl. **Exsanguination** requires a pointed, very sharp, rigid knife with a blade **at least 15 cm (6 in)** long, cutting both jugulars and carotids.

### 1.4 Firearms — calibre and placement, and a real discrepancy between the two guidelines

AVMA calls gunshot *"the most common method used for on-farm euthanasia of cattle."*

| | AVMA 2020 | AABP 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Handguns | **.32 to .45** recommended for cattle; **.22 handgun "generally not recommended for routine euthanasia of adult cattle"** | Calves <4 months: .22 LR handgun or rifle sufficient. **Cattle >4 months: .22 Magnum or higher calibre** |
| Rifles | .22, .223, .243, .270, .308 and others | Rifles preferred at distance; higher muzzle energy than handguns |
| .22 LR | Permitted only under **four simultaneous conditions**: (1) rifle not handgun, (2) solid-point bullet, (3) fired within 30–90 cm (1–3 ft), (4) proper anatomic placement | Sufficient for calves under 4 months only |
| Shotguns | Gauges **20, 16, 12**; **No. 6 or larger birdshot, or slugs**; from 1–2 yards | 12/16/20 ga for adult cattle; 28 or .410 for calves |
| Bullet type | **Solid-point preferred** (hollow-points fragment and lose penetration) | Solid-point, per AVMA |
| Bulls | — | Heavy-duty captive bolt, or firearms delivering **1,000 ft-lb (1,356 J)** muzzle energy |

**The discrepancy is worth encoding.** AVMA permits a .22 LR *rifle* on adult cattle under four conditions; AABP says anything over four months of age needs .22 Magnum or larger. AVMA itself cites a Canadian study in which standard- and high-velocity .22 bullets fired from a rifle at 25 m **failed to penetrate the skulls of the steers and heifers studied**, while .223 and .30-06 performed satisfactorily. The reconciliation is range: AVMA's .22 LR allowance is a close-range (1–3 ft) allowance. A rubric should treat *".22 LR at distance on an adult cow"* as clearly wrong and *".22 LR rifle, solid point, at 1–3 ft, correctly placed"* as defensible-but-marginal.

**"Inappropriate caliber of bullet for size of animal" is on AABP's unacceptable-methods list.**

### 1.5 Poll shooting

Both documents advise against it as a primary method. AVMA: *"Research has demonstrated that use of PCB at the poll is prone to operator error and misdirection of the bolt into the spinal cord instead of the brain… More animals were not properly rendered unconscious (ie, depth of concussion was shallow) using the poll position as compared with frontal sites."* AVMA notes the **EU prohibits poll PCB in abattoirs** on depth-of-concussion grounds. If used in an emergency where the face is not safely accessible, the shot must be **directed toward the base of the tongue**, and *"[t]his positioning should not be routine."*

AABP 2025 adds a live research thread: Dewell et al. 2025 (*Bov Pract* 59(1):9–16) was *"the first study"* to evaluate poll-position PCB as a single-step method without a secondary step and *"indicated this may be an effective method,"* but AABP's position is that *"[m]ore comparative research is needed."* Robbins et al. 2021 found no significant difference in time to death when the poll shot is properly applied **as a second shot**. **Poll as a primary single-step method is contested and not yet endorsed.**

### 1.6 Barbiturates — who may administer

**AVMA: only personnel registered with the US DEA** (and any required state authority) may perform barbiturate euthanasia, with strict drug control and accounting of amounts used. In practice on a commercial dairy this means the veterinarian, not farm staff. AVMA lists the drawbacks as needing adequate restraint, DEA registration, drug accounting, and *"fewer options for disposal of animal remains because of potential residues."*

Carcass consequences are severe and both documents stress them: barbiturate-euthanised carcasses are unfit for human or animal consumption; **renderers will not accept them** (FDA has a tolerance and a test for the drug in rendered product); scavenging wildlife and domestic dogs are poisoned by residues; AVMA notes federal exposure under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, Endangered Species Act, and Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act, with civil fines up to **$25,000** and criminal penalties up to **$500,000 and 2 years' incarceration**. AABP notes leachate from barbiturate carcasses can contaminate other carcasses and the environment, and that some regions require incineration or burial.

**Practical consequence for the eval:** on a farm without a vet present, the realistic method set is gunshot or PCB-plus-adjunct. AABP states this outright: *"Penetrating captive bolt and gunshot are the only two acceptable methods typically available to non-veterinarians for emergency euthanasia of cattle."*

### 1.7 Alpha-2 agonists (xylazine) — a specific, common, serious error

AABP: *"the injection of xylazine or any other alpha-2 agonist has not been shown to induce anesthesia and is not acceptable to use for euthanasia either as the sole means or as the primary method before applying an adjunctive method."* AVMA lists xylazine-then-KCl/MgSO₄ as **unacceptable**, noting α₂-agonists *"are recognized as being unreliable"* for producing general anaesthesia. AVMA elsewhere records that a surgical plane of anaesthesia *"could not be produced even when xylazine was administered at 55 to 88 times the usual dose"* required to produce recumbency in cattle.

Xylazine **is** legitimate as a *sedative* to make handling safer before an acceptable method — AABP recommends 0.3 mg/kg IM or SC for deep sedation, warns that higher doses risk convulsions, and warns that depth and duration are unpredictable in fractious, injured or compromised animals.

**This is a settled point with a clear failure mode:** sedate-then-bleed, or sedate-then-KCl, is an unacceptable method that looks superficially humane. It should score as a serious error.

### 1.8 Confirmation of death

📋 **AVMA §I7 (general):** *"Death must be confirmed before disposal of any animal remains. A combination of criteria is most reliable in confirming death, including lack of pulse, breathing, corneal reflex, and response to firm toe pinch; inability to hear respiratory sounds and heartbeat by use of a stethoscope; graying of the mucous membranes; and rigor mortis. **None of these signs alone, except rigor mortis, confirms death.**"*

📋 **AVMA §S3.1 (farmed animals specifically):** *"the most important indicator of death is lack of a heartbeat."* Where that is hard to evaluate, secondary indicators are **lack of movement over 30 minutes beyond detection of a heartbeat**, or rigor mortis.

📋 **AABP 2025 (cattle, most operational):** primary indicator is cardiac arrest; **lack of heartbeat and respiration for 3–5 minutes** confirms death; the heartbeat is best heard with a **stethoscope placed under the left elbow**. AABP warns that the heart continues beating for a period after captive bolt or gunshot because it is driven by the sinoatrial node, not the brain; that respiration is erratic in unconscious animals and unreliable alone; that **lack of corneal reflex alone only proves unconsciousness, not death**; and that if respiration resumes, *"a second shot is required."* AABP recommends **continued monitoring for 20–30 minutes** after euthanasia.

### 1.9 Records (AABP)

Minimum record set: ID of every animal euthanised, date, person performing it, indication/reason, method, and carcass disposal used — retained per state medical-record requirements. Separately, **equipment records**: gun or captive-bolt cleaning and service logs, *"Properly functioning equipment is critical to rendering the animals immediately insensible."*

## 2. National Dairy FARM Animal Care Program

📋 **FARM Animal Care Version 5** is the version in force, **1 July 2024 – 30 June 2027**.

⚠️ **I could not read the Version 5 reference manual.** nationaldairyfarm.com returns HTTP 403 to every automated request, including all `/wp-content/` PDFs, and no V5 manual is captured in the Internet Archive. My delegated decision-aids sweep hit the same wall independently. **Everything below marked "V5" comes from trade press and secondary summaries, not the standard itself.**

**What I *could* read in full:** the archived **Version 4** Animal Care Reference Manual (Internet Archive capture, 5.7 MB), including Chapter 8 (Non-Ambulatory Animals), Chapter 9 (Euthanasia), Chapter 10 (Fitness to Transport), and the standards/continuing-education structure. V4 is superseded, but the architecture carried into V5 and the V5 changes are described as *additions* to it.

### 2.1 What FARM V4 required (read in full, 📋)

The Chapter 9 management checklist, verbatim:
- ✓ Criteria for identification of animals to be euthanized are established.
- ✓ Euthanasia techniques follow the approved methods of AABP and/or AVMA.
- ✓ Carcass disposal is conducted using the appropriate method in accordance with applicable local ordinances.
- ✓ **The written herd health plan has a written protocol for euthanasia** that includes language specific to areas of euthanasia.

**Training:** *"Family and non-family employees with euthanasia responsibilities have been trained **annually** on written protocol for euthanasia."* Euthanasia is also one of the named job-specific continuing-education categories for non-family employees.

**Auditability and consequences — this is the part that matters.** Euthanasia is one of six animal-care standards that, *"if unmet at the time of an evaluation, will generate a MCAP"* — a **Mandatory Corrective Action Plan**. The other five are pre-weaned calf management, non-ambulatory animal management, fitness to transport, and treatment of common diseases (mastitis, metritis, milk fever, ketosis). Verbatim on the timeline: *"The MCAP will be created with their second-party evaluator with a set timeframe for re-evaluation of progress toward completing all MCAPs, **not to exceed nine months**."*

There are only **two routes to automatic removal from the FARM Program**: refusal to participate in Third-Party Verification, and observed **willful mistreatment** where the farm refuses to complete the required actions. Failure to provide humane euthanasia is classified as willful mistreatment. ⚠️ The willful-mistreatment classification and removal mechanics are from trade press and the Walker et al. review, not read by me in the manual.

FARM's own methods table (Table 4, V4) is reproduced from AABP: gunshot (high human-safety risk, moderate skill, no adjunct required), PCB (moderate risk, moderate skill, **adjunct required**), barbiturate overdose (low risk, moderate skill, no adjunct) — all three marked *"Operator Training Required."*

**Confirmation of death, FARM V4, verbatim:** *"The primary indicator for death is cardiac arrest. Lack of a heartbeat and respiration for 3-5 minutes should be used to confirm death. Using a stethoscope placed behind the left elbow is the best method… If the animal is unconscious but death cannot be confirmed, a secondary method of euthanasia must be immediately employed."*

### 2.2 What Version 5 added

🟡 Per trade press (Hoard's Dairyman, Dairy Herd, Lancaster Farming, Ohio State Extension), V5 adds two euthanasia standards:
1. **Identification of a primary AND a secondary individual** responsible for implementing euthanasia — described as two people from the farm or associated with it (e.g. the veterinarian or a neighbour) able to carry out euthanasia **"in a timely manner when needed."**
2. **Euthanasia protocols and practices must demonstrate a method of confirmation of death.**

V5 also reportedly elevates the **pain-management** standard from Continuous Improvement Plan to Mandatory Corrective Action Plan, and requires job-specific continuing education for individuals with euthanasia responsibilities on a cycle of **three years or less**.

⚠️ All of §2.2 is 🟡 trade press. I could not verify a single word of it against the FARM V5 standard. The "primary and secondary individual" standard is a direct and sensible response to the availability barrier documented in §3, but I cannot quote its actual wording.

### 2.3 The timeliness gap — FARM's central weakness

**FARM sets no numeric time limit for the euthanasia decision.** V4's only timeliness language is in the fitness-to-transport chapter — *"Make decisions in a timely and efficient manner, always considering animal well-being first"* and *"Treat, cull or euthanize animals promptly"* — with no defined interval anywhere.

✅ Walker, Román-Muñiz & Edwards-Callaway (2020) make this their central critique: FARM *"lacks strong messaging about the timeliness of decision-making,"* states euthanasia should be administered *"in a timely manner, if warranted"* without defining it, and — verbatim from their conclusion — *"Despite its criticality, timeliness of euthanasia is not addressed specifically or defined in either the national dairy welfare program (FARM) or professional guidelines offered by the AABP."*

They contrast this with **swine**, where the Pork Quality Assurance Plus programme and Common Swine Industry Audit set an explicit rule — *"animals that have no prospect for improvement or not responding to care and treatment after two days of intensive care must be humanely euthanized"* — and make failure of timely euthanasia a **critical audit failure**. ⚠️ I did not read the PQA Plus standard itself; this is quoted from Walker et al.

**One correction to that critique, which I verified directly.** AABP does set a clock, and did so in 2019 as well as 2025 — just on the *execution* step rather than the *decision* step: *"No more than four hours (preferably much less) should elapse between making the decision to euthanize and performing the procedure."* My delegated sweep read both the 2019 and 2025 AABP editions and confirms the four-hour figure is present in both, with *"(preferably much less)"* added in 2025. And AABP 2025 now **does** put a decision clock on the commonest case — *"Nonambulatory cattle that are not eating or which have not responded to treatment in 24 hours"* is a new 2025 indication for **prompt** euthanasia that did not appear in 2019. The Walker et al. critique was accurate as of 2020 and is now partly out of date.

## 3. The delayed-euthanasia problem

This is the best-evidenced item in the sweep, and the evidence is unusually clean because the primary source is a national government survey rather than a convenience sample.

### 3.1 Primary source: USDA NAHMS Dairy 2014 📋

📋 **USDA APHIS–VS–CEAH–NAHMS, *Dairy 2014*.** Two reports, both read directly (I extracted and read the relevant sections of each; I did not read either report cover to cover — each is several hundred pages, overwhelmingly on unrelated topics):
- *Dairy Cattle Management Practices in the United States, 2014* (Part I) — [https://www.aphis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/dairy14_dr_parti_1.pdf](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/dairy14_dr_parti_1.pdf). **Read §E.9 "Management of nonambulatory cows" (pp. 212–217) end to end**, including tables E.9.a–E.9.h.
- *Health and Management Practices on U.S. Dairy Operations, 2014* (Part III) — [https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animal_health/nahms/dairy/downloads/dairy14/Dairy14_dr_PartIII.pdf](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/animal_health/nahms/dairy/downloads/dairy14/Dairy14_dr_PartIII.pdf). **Read §A.1.c (disease outcomes), §A.3.b (deaths and euthanasia by class), and §H.2.a (personnel training) end to end.**

**Definition, and it matters:** NAHMS Dairy 2014 defines nonambulatory as *"cows unable to stand for at least 24 hours."* So every "downer" in these statistics has **already been down a full day** before entering the denominator.

#### Written protocols

| | % of operations with ≥1 nonambulatory cow (2013) | % of operations with **written** guidelines/procedures for handling nonambulatory cattle |
|---|---|---|
| Very small (<30 cows) | 38.8 (SE 6.3) | *not reported* |
| Small (30–99) | 76.4 (SE 2.2) | **15.5** (SE 1.9) |
| **Medium (100–499)** | **90.1** (SE 1.8) | **24.1** (SE 2.4) |
| Large (500+) | 98.4 (SE 0.5) | **57.1** (SE 2.3) |
| **All operations** | **76.5** (SE 1.6) | **22.6** (SE 1.4) |

For a ~250-cow farm: a **90% chance of having had at least one downer that year, and a ~24% chance of having a written protocol for it.** Three in four medium farms are managing downer cows with no written procedure.

Overall **2.6% of cows became nonambulatory in 2013** (West 1.7%, East 3.3%).

#### Outcomes for nonambulatory cows (% of the 2.6%, table E.9.g)

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Slaughtered for home consumption | 4.5 | **3.8** | 1.0 | 2.5 |

**Roughly one in five downer cows dies without assistance** — i.e. is neither euthanised nor recovers. On small farms it is nearly one in four.

#### Time to euthanasia — the core measurement (table E.9.h)

This is the single most important table in the sweep. **Read the denominator carefully:** it is *percentage of **operations**, by the **operation's average** number of days from recognition as nonambulatory to euthanasia.* It is **not** the percentage of cows euthanised within a given time.

| Operation average (days) | Small (30–99) | **Medium (100–499)** | Large (500+) | All |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ≤1.0 | 25.3 (3.2) | **20.0** (2.6) | 40.8 (2.4) | 26.3 (1.9) |
| 1.1–2.0 | 34.0 (3.5) | **31.3** (3.0) | 32.3 (2.2) | 32.8 (2.0) |

NAHMS's own summary: *"More than half of the operations that euthanized nonambulatory cows (59.1 percent) did so within 2 days after recognizing the cows as nonambulatory… A small percentage of operations (6.4 percent) waited more than 6 days before euthanizing nonambulatory cows."* (26.3 + 32.8 = 59.1 ✓.)

**For a ~250-cow farm specifically: only 20% of medium operations average ≤1 day. 48.7% average more than 2 days. 8.5% average more than six days** — and remember the clock starts only after the cow has already been down 24 hours by definition. A medium-herd farm is the *worst* stratum on this measure, worse than both small and large.

### 3.2 The peer-reviewed framing paper — and three arithmetic problems in it

✅ **Walker JB, Román-Muñiz IN, Edwards-Callaway LN. "Timely Euthanasia in the United States Dairy Industry — Challenges and a Path Forward." *Animals* 2020;10(1):71.** DOI [10.3390/ani10010071](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani10010071) · [PMC7022783](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7022783/). **Read in full**, including both data tables, all references, funding and COI. Funding: *"This research received no external funding."* COI: *"The authors declare no conflict of interest."* ⚠️ Note for provenance: lead author J.B. Walker was affiliated with Danone North America — a milk purchaser — which the paper does not flag as a conflict. Colorado State University for the other two.

This is a **review**, not original research. Its qualitative argument is strong and its identification of the six barriers is the best-organised account available. But **its headline animal counts are derived extrapolations, and three of them do not hold up.** Because these numbers circulate widely, they need flagging:

**Problem 1 — the total in Table 1 double-counts lameness.** Table 1 estimates "cows that should have been euthanised but were marketed" by applying NAHMS's *sold/removed* percentages to a 9,399,000-head lactating base. The rows: cancer eye 6,805; bloat 4,568; bloody gut 10,179; downer 39,495; **lame 232,118**; **severely lame 38,818**. Stated total: **331,982** (the rows sum to 331,983 — rounding). **The total includes both the "Lame" row and the "Severely lame" row, which is a subset of it.** Worse, the paper's own text states that *"moderately lame cows are considered fit for transport while severely lame cows are not"* — so including all 232,118 lame cows in a count of animals that should have been euthanised contradicts the paper's own criterion. **Counting only severely lame cows gives ~99,900, not ~332,000.** The lame row alone is 70% of the stated total.

**Problem 2 — the "one-half million" figure is not supported by either of the paper's own estimates.** The conclusion states *"Over one-half million dairy cattle unfit for transport are sent to slaughter annually."* Table 1 gives 331,982; the independent abattoir-survey route (9% of observed cattle × ~3M cull dairy cows) gives ~300,000. Neither reaches 500,000.

**Problem 3 — a percent-of-population presented as a percent-of-deaths.** The paper states *"less than 1.0% of the nearly one-half million calves and heifers that die on dairies annually are provided the benefit of euthanasia."* Read literally that says fewer than 1% of *dying* calves are euthanised. But the 0.4% and 0.2% figures behind it are percentages of the **calf and heifer populations**, not of deaths. The correct figures, from the NAHMS table: **~6% of preweaned-calf deaths and ~11% of weaned-heifer deaths were euthanasia.** Both are damning — ~94% and ~89% die unassisted — but they are an order of magnitude away from "less than 1.0%." (The paper's own narrative elsewhere gets this right: *"only 0.4% of the 6.4% reported mortalities having been euthanized"* and *"95% of calves that die on farm each year suffer the process of death without the benefit of euthanasia."*)

**Two further citation notes.** (a) The figures *"76.5% of dairy operations surveyed in 2014 had at least one non-ambulatory cow, 22.6% of them had written guidelines"* and *"only 59.1% of those euthanized were non-ambulatory for two days prior to euthanasia"* are attributed in-text to reference [43] = **Hoe & Ruegg 2006**, a Wisconsin producer survey. They are in fact **NAHMS Dairy 2014** figures (reference [4]). This is a mis-citation. (b) The 59.1% is also **restated with the wrong denominator and arguably reversed in meaning**: NAHMS says 59.1% *of operations* had an operation-average of ≤2 days *to* euthanasia; the paper renders it as 59.1% *of cows euthanised* having *been down two days beforehand*.

⚠️ **My delegated decision-aids sweep independently flagged the Table 2 denominators as suspect. Its specific inference — that both calves and heifers were computed against the same total-dairy-cattle base — is not what I found.** Back-calculating from the table: preweaned calves 281,970 ÷ 6.0% = 4,699,500, and heifers 159,783 ÷ 1.7% = 9,399,000. Two *different* bases that coincidentally both yield 18,798 euthanised. I am reporting my own arithmetic here because I read the table directly.

**None of this undermines the NAHMS data itself**, which is where the real evidence lives. It means the *derived* animal counts from this review should not be used in a rubric without recomputation.

### 3.3 What producers actually do and say

✅ **Wagner BK, Cramer MC, Fowler HN, Varnell HL, Dietsch AM, Proudfoot KL, Shearer J, Correa M, Pairis-Garcia MD. "Determination of Dairy Cattle Euthanasia Criteria and Analysis of Barriers to Humane Euthanasia in the United States: Dairy Producer Surveys and Focus Groups." *Animals* 2020;10(5):770.** DOI [10.3390/ani10050770](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani10050770) · [PMC7278400](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7278400/). **Read in full.** Funding: USDA NIFA AFRI award #006460 (2018-09666 A1251). COI: none declared. IRB: NC State.

**Part I design and denominators:** 3,664 surveys mailed via FARM-programme cooperatives nationally; **307 completed and returned = 8.4% response rate** (307/3,664 = 8.38% ✓). Condition-management questions analysed on **n = 270**. Respondents: 90.9% farm owners, 7.5% managers, <1% caretakers; 82.9% male; median age 51; median 40 years' dairy experience; 35.9% highest education high-school/GED. **This is an owner sample with an 8.4% response rate — heavily self-selected, and not representative of the people who actually find the down cow.**

**Part II:** 24 producers in three focus groups (8–10 each), recruited at dairy conferences, $100 incentive.

**Key findings:**
- For a **non-ambulatory adult cow**: 36.4% would euthanise immediately, **54.9% would treat and monitor**, 6.55% would cull/sell for beef, 2.18% N/A.
- Non-ambulatory weaned heifer: 40.9% euthanise / 48.3% treat-monitor. Pre-weaned calf: 39.6% / 46.3%.
- **6.3% of respondents would never euthanise a non-ambulatory cow, and 11.7% would never euthanise a non-ambulatory calf**, for that condition alone.
- **3.4%–6.5% would cull/sell non-ambulatory cattle for beef** — which is not merely poor practice but ships an animal that federal law guarantees will be condemned (see §6.3).
- Severe lameness: only 1.24% would euthanise an adult cow immediately; **61.6% treat and monitor**, 35.1% cull/sell.
- Cancer eye (severe): 3.64% euthanise, 28.4% treat and monitor, 35.6% cull/sell.
- Johne's disease: 0.73% euthanise, **68.5% cull/sell**. Lymphoma: 8.12% euthanise, **51.3% cull/sell**.
- 84.8% of respondents were responsible for **making** euthanasia decisions; 59.2% for **performing** it. 96% of those who performed it also decided it.

**Focus-group time limits, verbatim** — the clearest evidence that no shared standard exists: *"We have between 28 and 48 h…"* · *"…[A]fter 48 h she really has to show improvement on a day-to-day basis…"* · *"…[W]henever we have a down cow we evaluate her at the next manager's meeting."*

**Discussion time allocation:** animal factors 46%, human factors 32.6%, farm-operation factors 21.5%. Within those: animal welfare/well-being 16%, **emotions/psychology 16%**, health status 15%, improvement 11%, human/food safety 7.4%, **financial/economical 7.4%**, education/training 5.4%, public perception 3.7%, protocols/guidelines 6.2%.

Representative quote on the economic lever: *"[I]f cull prices are up you're obviously going to do more to try and get that animal into a state where it can be sold rather than be euthanized."*

⚠️ The paper's own §4.3 repeats the Walker et al. lameness error: *"over 250,000 dairy cattle in the US with severe lameness or non-ambulatory status were marketed when they should have been euthanized."* That figure requires the all-lameness row (232,118 + 39,495 = 271,613), not the severe-lameness row (38,818 + 39,495 = 78,313). It corroborates that the all-lame figure is circulating mislabelled as severe lameness.

### 3.4 Recovery prognosis — the evidential basis for a 24-hour rule

✅ **Green AL, Lombard JE, Garber LP, Wagner BA, Hill GW. "Factors associated with occurrence and recovery of nonambulatory dairy cows in the United States." *J Dairy Sci* 2008;91(6):2275–2283.** DOI [10.3168/jds.2007-0869](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.2007-0869) · [PubMed 18487650](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18487650/)

Design: case-control on USDA NAHMS data, calendar year 2004. **1,822 case operations** (≥1 nonambulatory cow) vs **151 control operations**. **Nonambulatory here is defined as "unable to rise for any period of time"** — a materially different definition from NAHMS Dairy 2014's ≥24 hours.

- Cows down **<24 h were 3.0× more likely to recover** than those down ≥24 h (OR 3.0).
- **Hypocalcaemia OR 6.0** for recovery, versus all other causes pooled.
- Calcium/phosphorus/potassium supplementation while down: OR 3.6.
- Authors' conclusion: *"considering euthanasia is appropriate for cows that have been nonambulatory for more than 24 h."*

⚠️ **I could only read the abstract. Full text is paywalled — journalofdairyscience.org and ScienceDirect both return HTTP 403.** My delegated sweep hit the same wall. Consequently:

⚠️ **The widely quoted recovery percentages "9.3% to 18.4%" and "8.2% for cows down >24 h" — cited by Walker et al. to this paper — I could NOT verify.** The abstract reports odds ratios only, not raw recovery rates. Both my own reading and my delegated sweep failed to trace them to a primary denominator. **Treat those three percentages as unverified.** The verified statements are the odds ratios above, plus NAHMS's directly measured **30.0% recovery** among cows already down ≥24 hours.

**The definition collision is itself a finding.** Three definitions are in active use in this literature: "unable to rise for **any** period" (Green 2008), "unable to stand for **at least 24 hours**" (NAHMS Dairy 2014), and "sternal recumbency for **≥12 h**" (cited in Wagner et al. 2020). A "24-hour rule" therefore means different things depending on when the clock starts. A rubric should specify the trigger observationally (down and not risen since *first observed*) rather than by reference to an undefined "24-hour" convention.

### 3.5 The stated and observed barriers

Synthesising Walker et al. (review), Wagner et al. (survey + focus groups) and Román-Muñiz et al. (caretaker interviews, §5):

| Barrier | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **No written protocol** | 📋 Only **22.6%** of operations (24.1% of medium herds) had written downer guidelines (NAHMS) |
| **No trained person available** | 📋 Euthanasia training provided on only **20.0%** of operations (24.6% medium) (NAHMS) |
| **Hope of recovery / reluctance to give up** | ✅ 54.9% would treat-and-monitor a downer cow; 6.3% would never euthanise one (Wagner) |
| **Unclear criteria → default to waiting** | ✅ *"If the decision is not clear, we give them another day."* (Román-Muñiz) |
| **Salvage value** | ✅ *"if cull prices are up you're obviously going to do more to try and get that animal into a state where it can be sold"* (Wagner); Walker et al.: the cull market *"does not in practice discourage the shipment of dairy cows that are unfit for transport"* |
| **Drug withdrawal periods** | ✅ Walker et al.: cows are *"kept on farm until the drug withdrawal period has been met at which time they are sent to market or slaughter even if not fit for transport"* |
| **Human–animal bond / euthanasia as failure** | ✅ *"[I]t gives me a sense of failure because we failed on that cow somehow or another."* (Wagner) |
| **Competing task priorities** | ✅ Walker et al. record that workers report "immediate" means *"after I finish this other task"*; and the scheduled-killing pattern, *"we euthanize cows at 2 p.m. when that person shows up for work"* |
| **Veterinarian unavailability** | ✅ 45.6% consulted a vet on the decision; Wagner et al. warn reliance on the vet *"may result in poor welfare outcomes due to the veterinarian's limited availability to assist in a timely manner"* |
| **Diagnostic delay** | ✅ *"If you think you've got a disease present and you need to delay so that the vet has time to test…"* (Wagner focus groups) |
| **Carcass disposal friction** | ✅ *"[D]isposal of the animal is always a problem and timely disposal too."* (Wagner) |

## 4. Who performs it, and how well

### 4.1 Who decides and who acts

📋 **NAHMS Dairy 2014, table H.2.a — % of operations providing training, by procedure:**

| Procedure | Small (30–99) | **Medium (100–499)** | Large (500+) | West | East | **All** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Animal handling/movement | 44.1 | 61.8 | 82.0 | 79.9 | 53.4 | 56.0 |
| Calving | 30.5 | 45.9 | 81.2 | 73.6 | 40.7 | 44.0 |
| Handling of nonambulatory animals | 19.7 | **30.6** | 79.0 | 70.8 | 29.2 | **33.4** |
| **Euthanasia** | 8.7 | **24.6** | 41.0 | 52.4 | 16.0 | **20.0** |

**Euthanasia is the least-trained procedure on the list.** For a ~250-cow farm: a **24.6% chance the operation trains anyone in euthanasia**, and a 30.6% chance it trains anyone in handling nonambulatory animals. The regional split is stark (West 52.4% vs East 16.0%).

**Who trains:** on more than 50% of operations the owner is the primary trainer. **Veterinarians are involved in euthanasia training on only 17.9% of operations** — the highest vet involvement of any procedure, which itself indicates how little veterinary input reaches this task.

✅ **Wagner et al. 2020:** 84.8% of (owner-dominated) respondents made euthanasia decisions; 59.2% performed it; 45.6% and 34.7% of those who delegated said a veterinarian was consulted on deciding and on carrying out respectively.

⚠️ **Hoe & Ruegg 2006** (*J Dairy Sci* 89:2297–2308, DOI [10.3168/jds.S0022-0302(06)72301-3](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.S0022-0302(06)72301-3)), a Wisconsin producer survey, reportedly found that **in 13 of every 100 euthanasias the task was performed by dairy personnel with no training**, and that veterinarians were involved in euthanasia decision-making in **32.3%** of cases. ⚠️ **I could not read this paper** — JDS returns 403. Both figures are secondary, via Walker et al. and a search summary. Treat as indicative only.

✅ **Román-Muñiz et al. 2021** (see §5) found, across 5 Colorado dairies: on one farm owners alone euthanised; on three, owners and some supervisors; on one, an on-farm certification programme allowed supervisors and some employees to do it. Euthanasia-specific training existed on **3 of 5 farms**, usually delivered by a veterinarian.

### 4.2 Competence requirements in the standards

📋 **AABP 2025:** where euthanasia is performed by the owner, employees, or a non-veterinarian third party, *"the expectation is that those individuals should have **annual training and certification**."* Each individual must know how to recognise animals needing euthanasia, proper technique, how to confirm death, safe use of the methods, and equipment maintenance — *"Some documented record of this training should be kept in the facilities training records or herd health plan."* And: *"Persons who perform humane euthanasia must be technically proficient, mentally capable, and possess a basic understanding of the anatomical landmarks and equipment used. If there is any degree of question or discomfort with a proposed euthanasia procedure, a veterinarian should be consulted."*

📋 **FARM V4:** annual training on the written euthanasia protocol, for family and non-family employees alike. **FARM V5** reportedly adds the named primary/secondary responsible individuals and a ≤3-year continuing-education cycle (⚠️ trade press).

### 4.3 Mis-stuns and failed first attempts — the honest state of the evidence

**There is no US on-farm first-attempt failure rate.** I searched specifically for it and did not find one; my delegated sweep did not find one either. What exists is slaughter-plant data and cadaver-head studies, and neither transfers cleanly to a farm.

- ✅ **Grandin 2002** (*JAVMA* 221:1258–1261), via AVMA: return to sensibility in **3/1,826 (0.16%)** fed steers and heifers and **8/692 (1.2%)** bulls and cows, in commercial beef slaughter plants. **Slaughter-plant setting — dedicated trained stunners, purpose-built restraint, monitored bolt velocity.** A floor, not a farm estimate.
- ✅ **Casagrande RR, Alexander L, Edwards-Callaway LN. "Effects of penetrating captive bolt gun model and number of stuns on stunning-related variables of cattle in a commercial slaughter facility." *Meat Sci* 2020;170:108231.** DOI [10.1016/j.meatsci.2020.108231](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.meatsci.2020.108231). Heads collected at a commercial slaughter facility; two gun models (Jarvis USSS-1, USSS-21) and one vs two knocks. Knocking efficiency was **not** affected by gun model or knock number (P = .39, .12); knock number did affect hole diameter, brain damage and hemorrhage (P ≤ .01); brainstem damage was greater with the USSS-21. ⚠️ Abstract only (Europe PMC); full text not read. This is the study AABP cites for the proposition that captive bolt *"may not lead to death"* even when correctly producing unconsciousness.
- ✅ **Gilliam et al. 2012** (*Anim Welf* 21(S2):99–102) and **2016** (*Anim Welf* 25:347–353): in the preliminary cadaver-head work, traditional shot placement **failed to disrupt the brainstem in all heads**, while alternative placement disrupted it in **6 of 8**. ⚠️ Secondary, via search summary and the AABP reference list; not read.
- ✅ **Derscheid et al.**, validation of a portable pneumatic captive bolt as a one-step method in feedlot cattle: **4 of ~66 (6%) required a second or third shot because of technical errors in placement.** ⚠️ Secondary, via search summary; not read. This is a *depopulation* study, not on-farm individual euthanasia.

✅ **The one piece of genuinely on-farm evidence is qualitative.** Román-Muñiz et al. 2021 report that dairy workers who preferred injectable agents did so because of *"previous experiences when a euthanasia with gunshot failed to kill the animal quickly (due to incorrect choice of gun and poor animal restraint)"*, and that *"some workers mentioned that while working on other livestock operations they had observed the use of unapproved methods."*

**For a rubric this absence is itself the finding:** the two named causes of on-farm gunshot failure in the only farm-based account are **wrong firearm for the animal** and **inadequate restraint** — precisely the two things AVMA and AABP specify and precisely the two a decision-making agent can control.

## 5. Worker psychological impact

⚠️ **Read this section's boundaries carefully.** Almost the entire quantitative literature on euthanasia-related traumatic stress is from **animal shelters, veterinary clinics, and biomedical research facilities — not farms.** The dairy-specific evidence is qualitative and small-n. I have kept these strictly separate below because conflating them is the commonest error in this area.

### 5.1 The dairy-farm-specific evidence (the only cattle-specific work)

✅ **Román-Muñiz IN, Cramer MC, Edwards-Callaway LN, Stallones L, Kim E, Thompson S, Simpson H, Mijares S. "Dairy Caretaker Perspectives on Performing Euthanasia as an Essential Component of Their Job." *Animals* 2021;11(2):289.** DOI [10.3390/ani11020289](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11020289) · [PMC7912631](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7912631/) · PMID 33498843. **Read in full.**

**Funding: High Plains Intermountain Center for Agricultural Health and Safety, CDC/NIOSH Grant U54OH008085** — an occupational-health grant, i.e. the funder's interest is worker health, not industry reputation. COI: none declared.

**Design and denominators:** qualitative **pilot** study. **38 dairy caretakers — 19 workers, 15 supervisors, 3 owners, 1 veterinarian — across 5 Northern Colorado dairies.** Focus groups and interviews, conducted partly in Spanish and translated; thematic analysis per Braun & Clarke; three independent coders with declared insider/outsider positionality; initial coding agreement >90%. **No psychometric instrument was administered — there are no prevalence rates in this study.** Seven themes.

**Findings:**
- **Euthanasia-specific training existed on 3 of 5 farms**, usually from a veterinarian, translated to Spanish where needed. On farms with training, *"workers seemed more knowledgeable of the euthanasia process and more comfortable in sharing their views."*
- **A systematic owner–worker perception gap.** On three of the four farms where owners performed euthanasia, owners judged training adequate and employees did not. One owner: *"I think there is, we have enough training to make that decision. I have no doubt about that…I'm not sure that any of my employees understand what happens other than 'this is where you put the shot.'"* Workers on the same farm, asked the same question, answered *"no."*
- **Coercion risk.** A worker on another farm: *"no, but if the owner says to do it [perform euthanasia], you have to do it."*
- **Mental-health resources: a lack of awareness and availability across all farms and all employee roles.** One owner: *"No. We don't provide that, and I would be surprised if they are aware of it. It's just not something I've ever thought about."* Cultural barriers to accessing services were raised by participants.
- **Making the decision — not performing the act — was identified by a majority of participants as the most stressful part of the process.**

### 5.2 The direct link from psychological burden to delay

**This is the item you flagged as most important, and it is documented — qualitatively, in dairy, but not measured.**

Román-Muñiz et al. state it directly: *"Competing interests and lack of communication or established objective guidelines contributed to work-related stress and **potentially resulted in animals not receiving timely euthanasia**."*

The mechanism appears in participants' own words:
- Attachment to a productive cow producing hesitation — a supervisor: *"If it is a good cow, milker, but she is there thrown [on the ground], you don't want…we have doubts. If you have a lot of production, your cows that fall, they slip. **It hurts me to kill them.**"*
- The salvage-vs-suffering conflict as a stressor — *"[do] I sell her tomorrow for meat or I kill her today, I euthanize today? That is the decision that is also very difficult because you are like 'do I wait until tomorrow or is she going to suffer overnight?'"*
- **And the single most rubric-relevant sentence in this sweep** — a worker on what happens when criteria are absent: **"If the decision is not clear, we give them another day."**

The authors attach that quote specifically to the absence of a decision tree with clear end points.

⚠️ **Status of this link: EVIDENCED but NOT QUANTIFIED.** No study measures whether psychologically burdened workers delay longer, or by how much. It is a mechanism reported by participants and endorsed by authors, not an estimated effect. It should not be presented as a measured effect size.

### 5.3 The wider (non-farm) literature — clearly labelled

✅ **Rohlf V, Bennett P. "Perpetration-induced traumatic stress in persons who euthanize nonhuman animals in surgeries, animal shelters, and laboratories." *Society & Animals* 2005;13:201–220.** DOI [10.1163/1568530054927753](https://doi.org/10.1163/1568530054927753). Verified via Europe PMC.
**n = 148 animal workers: veterinarians, veterinary nurses, research and animal shelter staff. Australian.** Instrument: Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R). **11% reported moderate levels of traumatic symptoms.** More than 70% said affinity toward animals had strongly influenced their occupational choice; half rated animal death — particularly euthanasia — among the least desirable parts of the job. Lower euthanasia-related stress was associated with greater satisfaction with social support and longer time working with animals; higher concern about animal death predicted higher stress. **Occupational context was not associated with different stress levels.**
⚠️ **This sample contains no farm or livestock workers.** It is the most-cited PITS-in-animal-work study and it is routinely applied to livestock settings without that caveat. It is Australian, cross-sectional, self-selected, and cannot establish causation.

✅ **Scotney RL, McLaughlin D, Keates HL. "A systematic review of the effects of euthanasia and occupational stress in personnel working with animals in animal shelters, veterinary clinics, and biomedical research facilities." *JAVMA* 2015;247(10):1121–1130.** DOI [10.2460/javma.247.10.1121](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.247.10.1121). Verified via Europe PMC abstract. The review's own framing is that there is *"incongruence in understanding what is currently termed compassion fatigue"* and *"minimal established evidence"* of these conditions' influence on health and well-being. ⚠️ Abstract only; **the three settings named in the title exclude farms.**

Via Walker et al. 2020 (⚠️ secondary, I did not read these primary sources): Reeve et al. 2004 found employees involved in euthanasia showed significantly greater work stress, stress-induced physical ailments, work-to-family conflict and job dissatisfaction; Anderson et al. 2013 found **74% of shelter managers agreed that performing euthanasia contributes to employee burnout and 26% believed it contributes to turnover** (shelter managers, denominator not given in the review); McDiarmid 2000 (a Detroit Free Press article, 🟡, not peer-reviewed) is the source of the widely repeated claim that shelter euthanasia staff are at risk of high blood pressure, ulcers, unresolved grief, depression, drug abuse and suicide.

**Construct caution.** "Perpetration-induced traumatic stress" originates in MacNair's work on human killing and has a thin validation base in animal-work contexts; the Scotney review's own conclusion is that terminology in this area is incongruent. The dairy literature mostly uses looser terms — "moral stress" (Rollin 1986), "caring–killing paradox," "compassion fatigue" — none of which is a validated diagnostic construct. **A rubric should treat worker distress as a real and documented phenomenon while avoiding any claim about measured prevalence in dairy workers, because none exists.**

⚠️ **A broader delegated sweep of this literature was commissioned and did not return before completion. Section 5 rests entirely on what I verified myself**, and is therefore thinner on non-cattle and depopulation-context evidence than the other sections.

## 6. Decision aids

This section draws substantially on my delegated decision-aids sweep, which read the AABP and CCQA/FARM decision trees in full. **Findings attributed to that sweep are its reading, not mine**, except where I note my own independent reading.

### 6.1 The three published trees

📋 **AABP Euthanasia Decision Tree (AABP Animal Welfare Committee, 2024)** — [https://aabp.org/committees/resources/welfare/euthanasia_decision_tree.pdf](https://aabp.org/committees/resources/welfare/euthanasia_decision_tree.pdf). Read in full by the delegated sweep. Structure:

1. *"Is the animal likely to improve with treatment and/or management?"*
   - **NO** → *"Is the animal eligible for slaughter?"*
   - **YES** → a gate box requiring **all five to be YES to proceed**: can risk to human safety be managed? · can pain be controlled? · are farm personnel capable of providing timely care and treatment? · are adequate facilities available? · will the animal tolerate treatment? Any NO returns to the slaughter-eligibility node.
2. *"Review progress at least daily, until desired outcome is reached"* — testing *"Is animal eating and drinking?"* and *"Is animal clinically improving?"* → YES returns to herd; **NO → "EUTHANIZE ASAP."**
3. *"Is the animal eligible for slaughter?"* → NO → euthanise ASAP. YES → *"Is the animal fit for transport?"* → YES: transport direct to market/slaughter; NO: **on-farm slaughter**.
4. Terminal box, verbatim: **"EUTHANIZE ASAP — Maximum of 4 hours · Using an AABP-recommended method · Performed by competent personnel."**

Side panel, *"animals with the following conditions are not fit for transport"*: non-ambulatory · **severe lameness (>3 on 5-point scale)** · bilateral blindness or cancer eye · un-met treatment withdrawal · acute fever due to disease · unreduced prolapse · calving or likely to calve · severe wound · CNS signs · **body condition score <2**.

Note the design choice: AABP puts a fixed clock on *execution* (4 hours) but uses a **daily-review-plus-eating-and-drinking** loop rather than a fixed hour count for the treat-and-reassess branch.

🔵/📋 **National Dairy FARM / Calf Care & Quality Assurance "Dairy Cattle Euthanasia Decision Tree"** — [https://www.calfcareqa.org/Media/CalfCare/Docs/euthanasia_poster-checkoff.pdf](https://www.calfcareqa.org/Media/CalfCare/Docs/euthanasia_poster-checkoff.pdf). Read in full by the delegated sweep (the FARM-hosted copy 403'd; the byte-identical CCQA copy was readable). **Beef Checkoff-funded — a producer-funded commodity programme, not a neutral publisher** — though the content is a faithful adaptation of AABP 2019. Nodes:

*"Is the animal experiencing pain, suffering or debilitating injury or disease?"* → YES → *"Can you effectively treat or manage the ailment? AND can you afford follow-up care?"* → NO → consider euthanasia / consult vet ASAP. → If NO to the first question → *"Is she suffering from any of the listed conditions below?"* → NO → **"Has she been non-ambulatory for 24 hours or more?"** → YES → consider euthanasia; NO → **"Can she sit upright on her own?"** → NO → consider euthanasia; YES → **"Will she eat/drink?"** → NO → consider euthanasia.

📋 **FARM Animal Care V4, Figure 13, "Euthanasia Decision Tree"** — *this one I read myself*, in the archived V4 manual. Attributed *"Adapted from Dr. Brandon Treichler, DVM."* Node sequence: *Is the animal in pain or suffering?* → *Can you effectively treat or manage the pain?* → *Broken bone or severe injury?* → *Down for 24 hours or more?* → *Can she sit sternal?* → *Will she eat/drink?* ⚠️ **The terminal outcome symbols did not survive text extraction** — the tree's YES/NO arrows are readable but the checkmark/cross endpoint glyphs are not. I am reporting the node sequence, which matches the CCQA version, and not asserting the branch endpoints from my own reading.

**The three trees converge on the same operational core:** three observable behavioural tests any stockperson can apply — **can she hold herself sternal with her head up · can she raise her front legs when assisted · will she eat and drink** — plus one clock, **24 hours non-ambulatory**.

### 6.2 The criteria tools underneath

**Locomotion scoring.** ⚠️ **A scale collision that will corrupt a rubric if not handled.** AABP's *"severe lameness (>3 on a **5-point** scale)"* (the Sprecher/Cook system) and FARM's *"3 or greater on the **FARM locomotion** scale"* (a **3-point** system) are different thresholds. FARM score 3 ≈ Sprecher 4–5. The 🔵 Zinpro scorecard (vendor; Zinpro sells trace minerals marketed for hoof health) implements Sprecher's 5 scores and — per the delegated sweep, which read it in full — **leaves the treatment cell for score 5 (severely lame) blank and never uses the word "euthanasia" anywhere.** The euthanasia linkage lives only in AABP's fitness-for-transport panel.

**Body condition.** **BCS < 2** (5-point US dairy scale) is a not-fit-for-transport criterion in both AABP and FARM. It is not by itself a euthanasia trigger, but combined with the trees' logic (not fit for transport + not eligible for slaughter → euthanise) it lands there.

**Pain / quality-of-life scales.** ✅ Tomacheuski et al., "Measurement properties of pain scoring instruments in farm animals: a systematic review using the COSMIN checklist," *PLOS ONE* 2023, DOI [10.1371/journal.pone.0280830](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0280830) — 23 studies, 20 instruments; six cattle instruments rated, with **UCAPS** rated high strength of evidence and the **Cow Pain Scale** (Gleerup et al. 2015) rated low. ⚠️ Read by the delegated sweep via extraction, not verbatim; note that a co-author developed the highest-rated instrument. **The decisive point: UCAPS is a post-operative acute-pain instrument. None of these six was designed or validated as a euthanasia trigger.** Walker et al. 2020, which I read directly, put it plainly: research on practical quality-of-life assessment in cattle *"is lacking considerably, and arguably non-existent,"* and there is *"no suggestion of a consistent standardized tool to effectively assess the QoL of a dairy cow."*

### 6.3 The regulatory boundary: fit for transport vs euthanise on farm

📋 **9 CFR § 309.3(e)** — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/9/309.3). Cattle that become **non-ambulatory disabled after passing ante-mortem inspection** must be reported to FSIS and **"must be condemned and promptly disposed of."** FSIS removed case-by-case disposition in a [2009 rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/03/18/E9-5987/requirements-for-the-disposition-of-cattle-that-become-non-ambulatory-disabled-following-ante-mortem), and closed the veal-calf exception for calves too tired or cold to rise in a [2016 rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2016/07/18/2016-16904/requirements-for-the-disposition-of-non-ambulatory-disabled-veal-calves). ⚠️ Read by the delegated sweep via the Cornell mirror (eCFR redirected); I did not read it myself.

**Why this belongs in a welfare rubric:** it makes a cow that goes down anywhere between the farm gate and the ante-mortem pen a **total economic loss** — condemned, no salvage, freight already paid. That is precisely why AABP's tree asks *"eligible for slaughter?"* before *"fit for transport?"*, and why FARM's criterion is written as *"a reasonable chance it will become non-ambulatory at any time from leaving the farm to the slaughter facility."* The **3.4%–6.5% of producers in Wagner et al. who would cull/sell a non-ambulatory cow** are describing an action that federal law guarantees will end in condemnation — a welfare failure and an economic error simultaneously.

**"On-farm slaughter" is a genuine third terminal state** — for an animal eligible for slaughter but not fit for the truck — and its availability varies by state. Two farms facing the same cow can legitimately reach different correct answers on this branch.

### 6.4 Does a decision aid change behaviour?

**No one has measured this in cattle.** The delegated sweep found no study comparing farms with and without a written euthanasia protocol or decision tree on time-to-euthanasia, proportion of downers euthanised within 24 hours, or mortality composition — and neither did I.

The nearest evidence is in swine and is weak for this purpose: ✅ Alves et al., "Development and evaluation of an interactive case-based training tool for timely on-farm euthanasia decision-making in swine," *Porcine Health Management*, 2026, DOI [10.1186/s40813-025-00483-0](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40813-025-00483-0). **Pre/post, no control, not randomised; 47 Brazilian students (39 veterinary/animal science), not farm workers, making simulated decisions.** Simulated accuracy by case: rectal prolapse 47/47 · gastric ulcer 46/47 · shoulder sore 42/47 · BCS-1 sow 30/47 · **necrotic lesion 23/47 (48.9%)** — the ambiguous case, which is the kind that matters, was failed by more than half. ⚠️ The delegated sweep flags that the paper's stated overall "89.1% correct" does not reconcile with the mean of those five per-case figures (82.1%), and read the paper via extraction rather than in full — **do not use the 89.1% figure.** Partly funded by Elanco Animal Health (Care4Pigs Award #450009286); authors declare no competing interests.

The authors' own limitation, quoted by the sweep: *"Rather than constituting clinical or predictive validation of euthanasia outcomes, the present study provides evidence of educational effectiveness and learner engagement."*

**Counter-evidence that tools alone are not enough**, from Walker et al. 2020 (which I read in full): *"If training, written protocols, and market disincentives were all that were needed to ensure timely euthanasia, surely the industry would have come closer than it is today."*

⚠️ **No published decision tree for dairy cattle has been validated** — no sensitivity/specificity against outcomes, no inter-rater reliability, no agreement-with-expert-panel study. All are committee-authored and proposed, not tested.

## 7. Settled vs contested

### Settled — the welfare-correct action is well established, and deviation should score as error

| Claim | Basis |
|---|---|
| A cow non-ambulatory ≥24 h that is **not eating/drinking** or **not responding to treatment** should be euthanised | Green 2008 (✅), AABP 2025 (📋), FARM/CCQA tree (📋), BQA — independent convergence |
| A cow that **cannot hold herself sternal with head up**, or **cannot raise her front legs when assisted**, should be euthanised promptly | AABP, FARM/CCQA; no source dissents |
| **Never transport a non-ambulatory animal** | Guideline consensus **and** 9 CFR 309.3(e) — condemnation is certain |
| Once decided, **act within 4 hours, preferably much less** | AABP 2019 and 2025, verbatim and unchanged |
| **PCB requires an adjunctive step** in cattle | AVMA 2020 (§S3.2.1.2.1), AABP 2025, FARM |
| The adjunct must **follow confirmed unconsciousness**, never precede it | AVMA, AABP — KCl/MgSO₄ before unconsciousness is described as "very inhumane" |
| **Xylazine (or any α₂-agonist) followed by exsanguination, KCl or MgSO₄ is unacceptable** | AVMA unacceptable-methods list; AABP unacceptable-methods list |
| **Manually applied blunt force trauma to the head of calves or mature cattle is unacceptable** | AVMA (calf skulls "too hard"), AABP |
| A **second shot is required, not optional**, if the first does not produce immediate unconsciousness | AABP, verbatim |
| **Death must be confirmed before disposal**; no single sign except rigor mortis confirms it | AVMA §I7 |
| **A firearm muzzle is never held against the head; a captive bolt muzzle always is** | AVMA, AABP — opposite rules, both safety-critical |
| Barbiturate euthanasia requires **DEA registration** and forecloses rendering | AVMA §S3.2.1.1.1, §I8 |

### Contested — a justified minority or practical dissent exists

- **24 h vs 48 h for downers.** 24 h is the published consensus, but 28–48 h is documented normal practice (Wagner et al.), and Green 2008's basis is an odds ratio, not a discovered cliff at hour 24. A farm running 48 h *with* documented daily assessment, feed, water and pain control is worse than best practice but not indefensible.
- **Hypocalcaemia is a genuine exception.** OR 6.0 for recovery. A milk-fever cow down 20 hours and responding to calcium is a materially different case, and every guideline's "not responding to treatment" clause accommodates it. A rubric that penalises *any* continued treatment past a fixed clock will mis-score this.
- **Severe lameness.** Guidelines treat >3/5 as not fit for transport, but **61.6% of producers would treat and monitor** a severely lame adult cow (Wagner et al.). Practical dissent is widespread even where the guideline is clear.
- **Cost as an explicit euthanasia criterion.** AABP lists *"Disease conditions for which treatment will not be pursued due to cost"* as a legitimate **indication** for euthanasia. Some welfare positions find naming economics objectionable; the guidelines' position is that an honestly acknowledged cost constraint acted on *quickly* is better welfare than a slow underfunded treatment attempt. This is a real tension, not an error — and note it cuts the opposite way from the salvage-value barrier in §3.5.
- **Poll shooting as a primary single-step method.** Dewell et al. 2025 is the first supportive study; AABP explicitly says more research is needed; AVMA and the EU advise against it. Currently not endorsed.
- **The four-hour execution window has no empirical basis I could locate** in any cited study — it is an expert-committee figure propagated unchanged since at least 2019.

### Unresolved — no evidence either way

- Whether any decision aid improves any real on-farm outcome.
- Whether a valid bovine quality-of-life instrument can be constructed. None exists.
- Whether worker psychological burden causes measurable delay. The mechanism is documented in workers' own words; the effect has never been quantified.
