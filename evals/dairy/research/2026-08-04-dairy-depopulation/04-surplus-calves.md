## 1. The numbers

### 1.1 The denominator: USDA does not publish dairy calf births

The single most important thing to get right, and it is a negative finding.

📋 **USDA NASS publishes calf crop as one combined number with no beef/dairy split.** The January 2026 *Cattle* report gives US calf crop 2025 = **32,895,600 head** (2024 = 33,416,500, down 2%), and separately US milk cows that have calved, 1 January 2026 = **9,568,300** (2025 = 9,380,800). There is no dairy calf birth series anywhere in the report. [NASS *Cattle*, 30 Jan 2026](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/795748/catl0126.txt)

Any "X million male dairy calves born per year" figure is a derived estimate, not a US government statistic. The defensible range is **roughly 8.9–9.4 million dairy calves per year**, of which about half are male — so **roughly 4.5 million surplus male calves annually**, with the caveat that this is arithmetic, not a published number.

Two independent routes agree: applying a ~0.97 calvings-per-cow-year rate to the 9.38 million milk cows and a ~0.855 rate to the 27.89 million beef cows sums to 32.9 million against a published 32.90 million calf crop (closes to within 0.2%); and 🔵 the American Farm Bureau Federation's own calculation puts dairy's share of the calf crop at **28% in 2023** (up from 26% in 1990), which on a 33.4-million calf crop is ~9.4 million. ⚠️ The AFBF figure was read only through a summarising fetch.

**Do not state a dairy-calf-birth number as a USDA figure.** State it as an estimate with the assumption shown.

### 1.2 What happens to them: veal has collapsed

📋 From the NASS *Livestock Slaughter* annual summaries (25 separate reports):

| Year | Commercial calf slaughter (head) | Year | Head |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 1,131,600 | 2015 | 452,600 |
| 2005 | 734,400 | 2020 | 456,400 |
| 2010 | 878,600 | 2023 | 293,600 |
| 2013 | 762,000 | 2024 | 211,900 |
| 2014 | 565,800 | **2025** | **124,600** |

**Decline 2000 → 2025: −89.0%.** The series is not monotonic — it rose 2007–2009 and again 2015–2019 before collapsing after 2019. Adding farm (non-commercial) slaughter of 21,800 head gives **total 2025 calf slaughter of 146,400 head**. Veal production 2025 was **30.0 million lb**, against 225 million lb in 2000 (−86.7%). [*Livestock Slaughter 2025 Summary*, April 2026](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Todays_Reports/reports/lsan0426.pdf)

The industry is extraordinarily concentrated: of the 140 federally inspected plants that killed any calves in 2025, **one plant accounted for 38% of the national total**. 📋 USDA AMS **discontinued its Weekly Veal Market Summary on 13 March 2025**.

**The interpretation matters and is easy to get wrong.** Calf slaughter fell 89% while the dairy herd *grew* and the calf crop held near 33 million. The calves did not disappear — they are being counted later and elsewhere, in the 29.8-million-head **cattle** series, as beef. Average live weight at calf slaughter also *rose* (316 lb in 2000 → 343 lb in 2025), consistent with the lightest bob calves leaving the series.

### 1.3 A likely premise corrected: NASS does not classify calves by carcass weight

📋 The NASS *Terms and Definitions* section contains **no definition of "calves" and no weight or age threshold**. The authoritative definition sits with AMS and is based on **type of feeding and physiological maturity, expressed through lean colour**. Per [7 CFR §53.120](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Vealers_and_Slaughter_Calves_Standard%5B1%5D.pdf): "the differentiation between vealers and calves is based primarily on evidences of type of feeding and age." Per [7 CFR §54.113](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Veal_&_Calf_Carcass_Standard%5B1%5D.pdf): "Differentiation between veal, calf, and beef carcasses is made primarily on the basis of the color of the lean."

### 1.4 The veal categories (USDA/FSIS definitions)

📋 From FSIS *Veal from Farm to Table* (revised June 2011), read in full via the [GovInfo archival PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-A110-PURL-gpo19096/pdf/GOVPUB-A110-PURL-gpo19096.pdf):

- **Bob veal:** "About fifteen percent of veal calves are marketed up to 3 weeks of age or at a weight of 150 pounds." *(Denominator is veal calves, not all dairy calves; figure dates to 2011 at the latest.)*
- **Special-fed veal:** "The majority of veal calves are 'special-fed'," marketed ~16–18 weeks, up to 450 lb.
- **Calf (FSIS):** "a young bovine of either sex that has not reached puberty (up to about 9 months of age), and has a maximum live weight of 750 pounds."
- **"Dairy-beef" is NOT a USDA or FSIS category.** It appears in neither the FSIS fact sheet nor either AMS grade standard. It is an industry term.

⚠️ The live FSIS page returned HTTP 403 on four attempts; the 2011 archival PDF was used instead.

### 1.5 Disposition proportions — and an arithmetic problem

✅ The most-cited US breakdown, from Creutzinger et al. 2021, verbatim:

> "Roughly 40% of male calves born in the U.S. are sold through auctions, with the remainder sold directly to a calf raiser (30%) or dealer (18%)."

**These sum to 88%, not 100%.** The paper says "the remainder," which is not what the three numbers do. The residual ~12% is unaccounted for and presumably includes calves raised on the operation of birth, calves that died, and other channels. The citation traces to **USDA NAHMS Dairy 2014** — so the underlying data are 📋, but the summary sentence does not close. **Cite the arithmetic gap if you use this.**

Other figures from the same review: surplus calves are **95% male**; "most small (68%) and medium (58%) sized herds sell surplus calves through an auction, whereas large farms more commonly sell calves directly to a calf raiser."

⚠️ Creutzinger et al. 2021, *Front Vet Sci* 8:661453, [doi:10.3389/fvets.2021.661453](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2021.661453) — full text downloaded; introduction, transportation, auction and euthanasia sections read directly, not the whole paper.

### 1.6 Age at leaving the farm

⚠️ From Cramer et al. 2023 (read via summarising fetch, not raw): replacement calves leave dairies "at an average age of 3 d"; nonreplacement calves "are typically sold from the dairy farm at <1 wk of age in the United States" with "**over 60% transported at <24 h of age**"; "approximately 15% of operations transporting calves more than 80 km"; and "approximately three-fourths of nonreplacement male and 8.5% of nonreplacement female calves were marketed indirectly in 2014." Cramer MC, Pempek JA, Román-Muñiz IN, Edwards-Callaway LN. *JDS Commun.* 2023;5(3):259–263. [doi:10.3168/jdsc.2023-0452](https://doi.org/10.3168/jdsc.2023-0452)

⚠️ Shivley et al. 2019, *J Dairy Sci* 102:4489–4497 — **42 operations across 10 states**, from NAHMS Dairy 2014; 97.6% of operations sold bull calves before weaning; **2.4% of bull calves died before leaving the operation**. ## 2. The beef-on-dairy shift

### 2.1 The semen data (primary, read in full)

📋 The National Association of Animal Breeders reports annual unit sales covering "approximately 95% of the US AI industry." From the [2025 year-end report, published 11 March 2026](https://www.naab-css.org/uploads/userfiles/files/2025%20NAAB%20Regular%20Members%20Report%20Year%20End%20Semen%20Sales_FINAL.pdf), read end to end — **US domestic figures for 2025**:

| Category | Units |
|---|---|
| Gender-selected (sexed) dairy | 10.6 million |
| Conventional dairy | 6.0 million |
| **Total dairy units used in US** | **16.5 million** |
| Beef semen into dairy herds | 8.1 million |
| Beef semen into beef herds | 1.7 million |
| **Total US beef units** | **9.8 million** |

Verbatim: "the total beef units sold in the US totaled 9.8 million units with 8.1 million going into dairy herds and 1.7 million used in beef herds"; "Gender selected dairy semen now represents 64% of the dairy units used by U.S. dairy producers."

**Derived, and the arithmetic closes:** total units used on US dairy cows = 16.5M + 8.1M = **24.6 million**, of which beef is 8.1/24.6 = **32.9%**, sexed dairy 43.1%, conventional dairy 24.4%. This independently reproduces the "43% sexed / 24% conventional / 33% beef" split circulating in 🟡 trade press.

**Critical caveat, load-bearing:** semen *units sold* is not *calves born*. Sexed semen has lower conception rates per unit, so more units are consumed per resulting pregnancy. **Do not present 33% as "a third of dairy calves are beef-cross."**

### 2.2 Has the trend plateaued?

Yes, on the primary data. NAAB reports beef-on-dairy in 2025 was **"consistent at 8.1 million units"** with domestic "units used on dairies remaining unchanged," while beef-into-beef rose 7%. The steep growth phase appears to be over.

### 2.3 Head counts — all industry estimates, none official

**No US government agency publishes a head count of beef-on-dairy cattle.** Checked directly against NASS *Cattle* (no mention of beef-on-dairy or crossbreds), NASS *Livestock Slaughter* (cattle classes are steers/heifers/dairy cows/other cows/bulls — dairy-origin fed cattle are invisible), and ERS *Livestock, Dairy, and Poultry Outlook* LDP-M-380.

🟡 The widely quoted CattleFax estimate is **~50,000 head in 2014 rising to 3.22 million head in 2024**. 🔵 A Purina survey puts 72% of dairy farms as actively crossbreeding.

⚠️ **Both reached the sub-researcher only through a secondhand AFBF summary read by automated summarisation; neither CattleFax nor Purina was opened.** Do not put the 3.22 million figure in a rubric without tracing it to a CattleFax publication and labelling it a private-firm estimate.

### 2.4 Has it changed the welfare decision?

**Settled:** beef-cross calves are worth more. ✅ Wilson et al. 2020 (via Bolton & von Keyserlingk) found "cross-bred calves with beef genetics sold for higher prices," with Brown Swiss and Jersey lowest, then Holstein.

**Contested / not established:** whether higher value has actually reduced on-farm killing or improved early-life care in the US. The mechanism is plausible, and Bolton & von Keyserlingk frame the euthanasia decision as arising when "the value attributed to a calf is less than the cost of rearing it." But **no US data measuring on-farm killing before versus after the beef-on-dairy shift** was found, because (see §3.2) there is no US baseline measurement at all. Treat the value-to-welfare link as a well-motivated inference, not a documented result.

## 3. On-farm killing of newborn calves

### 3.1 What the guidelines say

📋 **AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals: 2020 Edition**, §S3.2.4 "Neonates" — verbatim:

> "Neonatal calves present special challenges for euthanasia. Methods include barbiturate overdose, gunshot, and captive bolt (penetrating or nonpenetrating) with an adjunctive method applied to ensure death. **Manually applied blunt force trauma to the head is not acceptable for calves because their skulls are too hard to achieve immediate destruction of brain tissue leading to unconsciousness and death.**"

> "Use of a penetrating or purpose-built NPCB (**controlled** blunt force trauma) is acceptable with conditions for calves, lambs, and kids. Controlled blunt force trauma differs from manually applied blunt force trauma because captive bolts deliver an appropriate and uniform amount of force each time they are fired…"

And §S3.2.3 "Unacceptable Methods":

> "The following methods are unacceptable for euthanasia of cattle and small ruminants: manually applied blunt trauma to the head; injection of chemical agents into conscious animals…; administration of xylazine or any other α2-adrenergic receptor agonist followed by IV potassium chloride or magnesium sulfate…, drowning, or air embolism…; and electrocution with 120 V, drowning, and exsanguination in conscious animals."

Note the species asymmetry: manually applied blunt force trauma **is** listed acceptable-with-conditions for piglets, and for lambs and kids AVMA describes the technique but lists it unacceptable because it cannot be applied consistently. For calves the stated reason is skull hardness. ⚠️ §S3.2.1.1, §S3.2.1.2.1 (partially), §S3.2.1.4, §S3.2.3 and §S3.2.4 were read in full; not the whole 133-page document. Obtained from the [regulations.gov mirror](https://downloads.regulations.gov/AMS-NOP-21-0073-39715/attachment_2.pdf) because avma.org blocks automated retrieval.

📋 **AABP Guidelines for the Humane Euthanasia of Cattle, March 2025** — read end to end, [aabp.org](https://www.aabp.org/Resources/AABP_Guidelines/EUTHANASIA-2025.pdf). The more operationally specific document:

- **Blunt trauma:** "Blunt trauma by physical blow to the head is not an acceptable method of euthanasia of calves because the skull is too hard to consistently achieve immediate and lethal destruction of brain tissue."
- **Non-penetrating captive bolt:** "using a purpose-built non-penetrating captive bolt stunner is an acceptable (with conditions) method of euthanasia for calves, but should be followed with an adjunctive step to assure death."
- **Firearm calibre:** "the .22 caliber handgun or rifle loaded with a long rifle (LR) solid point bullet is sufficient for **calves less than four months of age**. In cattle over four months of age, it is necessary to use .22 Magnum or higher calibers." And: "The 28 or .410 gauge shotgun is an excellent choice for use in calf euthanasia."
- **Penetrating captive bolt** always requires a secondary method.
- **Barbiturate:** acceptable, but "it is recommended that barbiturate overdose be a euthanasia tool of last resort" — renderers will not accept the carcass, and scavenging wildlife are poisoned.
- **Xylazine/alpha-2 agonists alone:** explicitly not acceptable, even as a pre-step. "Animals must be rendered unconscious via general anesthesia, gunshot or captive bolt prior to administering one of the above secondary methods."
- **Timeliness — a good rubric anchor:** "No more than four hours (preferably much less) should elapse between making the decision to euthanize and performing the procedure."
- **Confirmation of death:** "Lack of heartbeat and respiration for three-to-five minutes should be used to confirm death"; "Lack of a corneal reflex alone is not sufficient."

### 3.2 How often does it happen in the US? Nobody knows — and that is the finding

**There is no US prevalence figure for on-farm killing of healthy surplus newborn calves.** Searched for specifically and not found; the Creutzinger 2021 review, writing about the US and Canada, cites only Canadian data. Report this gap plainly rather than substituting a Canadian number.

The Canadian evidence, and a discrepancy not to propagate:

- ✅ Creutzinger et al. 2021, verbatim: "Most surplus calves in the U.S. and Canada are raised for meat; however, it is not uncommon for calves to be euthanized on the dairy farm shortly after birth. **Approximately 5% of dairy farmers in Canada reported euthanizing at least one male calf at birth.**" Citation: Renaud et al. 2017, *J Dairy Sci* 100:6862–71, [doi:10.3168/jds.2017-12750](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.2017-12750).
- ✅ Of those farmers who did euthanize at birth, **34% used blunt force trauma**, with Quebec location significantly associated.
- ⚠️ **Bolton & von Keyserlingk 2021 state something materially different**: "In one survey of Canadian farmers, an average of 19% of calves were euthanised at birth and of those respondents that euthanised calves, 34% reported using blunt force trauma." Read verbatim in the downloaded full text.

**These two are not the same statistic and must not be merged.** "5% of farmers euthanized at least one calf" and "19% of calves were euthanized" have different denominators (farms vs calves) and differ by an order of magnitude in implied scale. The most likely reconciliation is that 19% is the mean *within* the 5% of farms that euthanize — but this could not be verified, because Renaud 2017 is behind Elsevier's 403. **⚠️ If this number goes into a rubric, Renaud 2017 must be read directly first.** Bolton J, von Keyserlingk MAG. *Front Vet Sci* 2021;8:660934, [doi:10.3389/fvets.2021.660934](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2021.660934).

## 4. Transport of unweaned calves

### 4.1 United States: no minimum age, one weak federal statute

**There is no US federal minimum age for transporting a calf.** The central regulatory fact, and a negative — the US is an outlier.

📋 The only federal instrument is the **Twenty-Eight Hour Law, [49 U.S.C. §80502](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/49/80502)**:

- Applies to a "rail carrier, express carrier, or common carrier (except by air or water)," transporting animals across state lines.
- "may not confine animals in a vehicle or vessel for more than 28 consecutive hours" without unloading for feeding, water and rest.
- Extendable to **36 consecutive hours** on the owner's written request.
- On unloading: animals "shall be unloaded in a humane way into pens equipped for feeding, water, and rest for at least **5 consecutive hours**."
- **The exemption that swallows the rule:** "This section does not apply when animals are transported in a vehicle or vessel in which the animals have food, water, space, and an opportunity for rest."
- Civil penalty: "at least $100 but not more than $500 for each violation."

⚠️ Read via a summarising fetch of the Cornell LII page, not raw statutory text.

✅ Creutzinger 2021, verbatim: "In the U.S. pre-weaned calves fall under the transportation regulations established for all food production animals which limits transport to no more than 28 continuous hours… **There is a paucity of information regarding the distance or length of time calves are transported in the U.S. and Canada.**"

🔵 AWI documents a specific journey — 1,113 miles, Hancock, Minnesota to Texico, New Mexico, in 19 hours, including one-week-old calves. Advocacy source; illustrative, not a rate.

### 4.2 Comparator jurisdictions — all stricter

📋 **New Zealand**, Animal Welfare (Care and Procedures) Regulations 2018, reg 33(1): a young calf may not be transported off farm for sale or slaughter "unless the calf is **at least 4 full days (96 hours) of age**"; reg 34(1) caps the journey at "**no more than 12 hours**"; reg 37 prohibits transporting young calves by sea across Cook Strait; reg 35 requires facilities enabling the calf to walk on and off "**by its own action**." 📋 **EU**, Council Regulation (EC) No 1/2005, Annex I Ch. VI para 1.9: long journeys (>8 h) permitted only if "calves are older than fourteen days." Ch. 📋 The **European Commission's 2023 proposal COM(2023)770** would raise this to "calves of less than 5 weeks of age and weigh less than 50 kg" — note the drafting joins the limbs with "and". **As of August 2026 it is NOT adopted** — still first reading, no Parliament committee vote, no Council general approach, per Council doc. 10390/26 of 17 June 2026 and the EP Legislative Train (updated 20 June 2026). A 5-year transition is proposed. ⚠️ COM(2023)770 read in part.

📋 **UK**: post-Brexit thresholds unchanged (10 days / 14 days / 100 km). The [Animal Welfare (Livestock Exports) Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/11/contents), s.1, in force 22 July 2024, bans exporting cattle from Great Britain for slaughter or fattening-for-slaughter — capturing the historic unweaned-calf export trade, but changing no domestic age threshold.

### 4.3 The New Zealand natural experiment — the most useful comparator

📋 MPI publishes bobby calf mortality (dead or condemned on arrival, or in yards, at processing premises), spring calving season:

| Season | Calves processed | Mortality | Deaths/condemnations |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2008 | 1,515,189 | **0.68%** | 10,275 |
| 2015 | 2,171,995 | 0.25% | 5,390 |
| 2016 | 1,935,054 | 0.12% | 2,255 |
| 2017 | 1,773,809 | 0.06% | 1,123 |
| 2019 | 1,771,639 | **0.04%** | 789 |
| 2023 | 1,927,488 | 0.05% | 1,012 |

**A 0.68% → 0.04–0.05% fall, a factor of roughly 15, across the period in which the 2016/2018 regulations were introduced.** MPI also records that "on average, calves are transported a little over 5 hours, with some journeys extending up to 16 hours. Calves were held in lairage on average for 14 hours, though up to 28 hours has been recorded."

This is the strongest available evidence that regulation of minimum age, journey time and loading facilities moves the outcome. It is observational, not a controlled trial, and confounded with concurrent farmer education campaigns — MPI itself attributes the early decline partly to "farmer education campaigns and the implementation of voluntary initiatives." ⚠️ The 2009–2014 intervening rates come from figure data labels with no published denominators; individual season reports were not opened; no 2024 or 2025 report exists on MPI's listing.

## 5. Welfare science: fitness for transport

### 5.1 Navel healing — the cleanest number in this sweep

✅ Roccaro M, Bolcato M, Masebo NT, Gentile A, Peli A. **Navel Healing and Calf Fitness for Transport.** *Animals* 2022;12(3):358. [doi:10.3390/ani12030358](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani12030358). Read end to end (body through Conclusions; reference list and supplementary tables not read), via the [University of Bologna repository copy](https://cris.unibo.it/retrieve/handle/11585/851200/e1dcb338-c7aa-7715-e053-1705fe0a6cc9/13_2022_Roccaro%20et%20al,%20Animals.pdf).

299 dairy calves (55 male, 244 female), aged 0–90 days, mainly Holstein (n=201) and crossbred (n=98), on 5 farms in Emilia-Romagna, Italy. Navels scored 1–5; calves showing omphalitis excluded.

- **90% of all calves up to 14 days old still had the navel stump attached.**
- The youngest calves with a completely healed navel (score 5) were **19 and 20 days old**; only 3% of score-5 calves were under 3 weeks.
- Verbatim: "'Navel healing' should be defined as the scarring of the umbilical wound (score 5), which occurs **no earlier than 3–4 weeks of life**."

| Standard used | Risk calf is <10 days | Risk calf is ≤14 days |
|---|---|---|
| Score ≥3 (dry, shrivelled stump) | **25.5%** (20.6–31.0) | 38.0% |
| Score ≥4 (scab/granulation, no stump) | **4.3%** (2.0–9.2) | 8.7% |
| Score 5 (fully healed) | **0%** (0–4.9) | 0% |

**The practical implication is directly usable as a rubric anchor: "dry navel" — the criterion NZ and several codes actually use — is a poor proxy for age**, admitting a quarter of calves under 10 days.

### 5.2 Condition of calves at collection points and slaughter

⚠️ **All figures in this subsection reached the agent through Cramer et al. 2023, read via a summarising fetch. England et al. 2023, Renaud et al. 2018, Pempek et al. 2017, Wilson et al. 2020 and Goetz et al. 2023 were not opened directly.**

**US, bob veal calves on arrival at a Midwestern slaughter establishment (England et al. 2023):** dehydration **68.6%**; hypoglycaemia **73.4%** (blood glucose <4.95 mmol/L), odds 3× greater for male nonreplacement calves; thin body condition **39.8%**; navel inflammation **25.7%**; poor transfer of passive immunity **23.4%**; **96% had at least one poor health metric**.

**US, Ohio veal facilities on arrival (Pempek et al. 2017):** diarrhoea 14%, respiratory disease 0.5%, depressed attitude 14%, inflamed navels 27%, dehydration 35%.

**Canada, veal facility on arrival (Renaud et al. 2018):** ~46% with some dehydration; 32.7% with no subcutaneous fat covering; 18% emaciated; nearly 30% with clinical signs of navel infection.

**Before transport:** 37% of calves had at least one abnormal health condition before being transported (Canada, Wilson et al. 2020); nearly half of transported calves had at least one health abnormality (western US, Cramer et al. 2024).

**After arrival, Ontario commercial veal facility:** **7.5% of calves died within the first 11 weeks**; almost 90% treated with antimicrobials at least once for diarrhoea; approximately 90% treated one or more times for respiratory disease (Scott et al. 2019; Goetz et al. 2021).

**Effect of journey duration (Goetz et al. 2023):** calves transported 6 h versus 16 h had "reduced incidence of dehydration in the 3 d following transport, greater ADG in the 50 d posttransport, and decreased incidence of diarrhea in the 14 d following transport."

**Thermoregulation:** "The thermoneutral zone for young calves is between 15 and 26°C."

### 5.3 Deaths in transit specifically

✅ Roccaro et al. 2022 summarise the international picture, and the honest headline is that **death during transit itself is rare; death shortly afterwards is not**:

> "Even though few calves die during transport, a strong negative correlation has been shown between age at transport and mortality, which occurs within few weeks after transport due to secondary infections resulting from the impairment of immune system function caused by transport stress."

Reported in-transit rates: Australia 0.64% average for bobby calves 1998–2000; New Zealand 0.68% (2008) → 0.25% (2015) → 0.12% (2016); a Southern Italian control post 2010–2015 recorded 0.011% for calves <100 kg and 0.044% at 200 kg. And: "Up-to-date statistics on calf transport mortality rates are limited." **There is no equivalent US transit-mortality statistic.**

Also: navel inflammation prevalence on arrival at veal facilities or auction sites ranges **20% to 32%** across studies, and "has been associated with an increased risk of mortality in the first three weeks after transport."

## 6. Colostrum and early care as the upstream decision

The strongest-evidenced part of the topic, and it connects the calf question to ordinary herd management rather than only to killing.

### 6.1 The US standard

✅ **Lombard J, Urie N, Garry F, et al.** Consensus recommendations on calf- and herd-level passive immunity in dairy calves in the United States. *J Dairy Sci* 2020;103(8):7611–7624. [doi:10.3168/jds.2019-17955](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.2019-17955)

| Category | Serum IgG | Serum %Brix | Herd-level target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Excellent | ≥25.0 g/L | ≥9.4% | **>40%** of calves |
| Good | 18.0–24.9 g/L | 8.9–9.3% | 30% |
| Fair | 10.0–17.9 g/L | 8.1–8.8% | 20% |
| **Poor** | **<10 g/L** | <8.1% | **<10%** |

Verbatim from the abstract: "Mortality rates have decreased since 1991, but the percentage of calves with morbidity events has not changed over the same time period."

Feeding target (Godden et al. 2019, via Cramer 2023): "Every calf should receive at least **150 to 200 g of IgG within 2 h of birth**."

⚠️ Lombard 2020 read as abstract only; full text behind a 403.

### 6.2 The link to mortality and morbidity — settled

✅ Roccaro et al. 2022, verbatim: "The correlation between failure of passive transfer of colostral immunoglobulins, morbidity, and mortality is **well documented**." One genuine nuance: "studies on the relationship between good passive immunity transfer and infectious disease risk in calves have generated **conflicting results regarding umbilical infections**." So FPT → mortality/morbidity is settled; FPT → navel infection specifically is not.

### 6.3 Surplus calves get worse colostrum — the upstream welfare decision

The finding that makes colostrum a decision node rather than background husbandry.

⚠️ Creutzinger et al. 2021: male calves "are more likely to be fed via different routes… with a longer delay to the first feeding of colostrum," and "are more likely to receive colostrum with bacterial contamination and lower volumes of colostrum than female calves." Between **6 and 43% of male calves have failure of transfer of passive immunity on arrival to calf raisers** in the US and Canada.

⚠️ Renaud et al. 2017 (Canada), via Bolton & von Keyserlingk: **9% of farmers "did not always feed colostrum to male calves"**; **17% "did not provide the same quantity of feed to male calves as they did to heifer calves."**

Outcome measure, from §5.2: **23.4% of US bob veal calves had poor passive transfer on arrival at slaughter**, against the consensus herd-level target of **<10%**.

The causal chain is documented end to end at each link, though not as a single quantified pathway in any one study: a calf categorised as surplus at birth receives colostrum later, in smaller volume, more often contaminated → arrives with poor passive transfer → poor passive transfer is a documented risk factor for morbidity and mortality → observed post-arrival mortality in commercial veal is 7.5% in 11 weeks with ~90% antimicrobial treatment rates.

## 7. Settled versus contested

**Settled:**
- US veal slaughter has fallen ~89% since 2000 while the dairy herd grew — 📋 primary NASS series, 25 reports.
- There is no US federal minimum age for transporting a calf; the only federal instrument is the 28-hour law with a self-exempting clause and a $100–$500 penalty.
- Manually applied blunt force trauma is unacceptable for calves under both AVMA 2020 and AABP 2025; purpose-built non-penetrating captive bolt with an adjunctive step is acceptable with conditions.
- Complete navel healing does not occur before ~3 weeks; "dry navel" admits ~25% of calves under 10 days.
- Failure of transfer of passive immunity is a documented risk factor for calf morbidity and mortality; US consensus threshold <10 g/L serum IgG with a <10% herd target.
- Surplus/male calves receive measurably worse colostrum and early care than heifer calves.
- Beef semen constituted 8.1 of 24.6 million units used on US dairy cows in 2025 (32.9%), flat year-on-year.
- New Zealand's bobby calf mortality fell from 0.68% (2008) to 0.04–0.05% (2019–2023) across its regulatory reform.

**Contested, uncertain, or simply unmeasured:**
- **How often healthy newborn calves are killed on US farms — no data exists.** The repeated numbers are Canadian, and the two most-cited Canadian figures (5% of farmers; 19% of calves) have incompatible denominators and one could not be verified.
- Whether beef-on-dairy has actually reduced on-farm killing in the US — plausible, undocumented, unmeasurable without a baseline.
- Total head of beef-on-dairy cattle: the 3.22 million CattleFax figure is a private-firm estimate reached secondhand.
- What FARM v5 actually requires on transport age and euthanasia method — the manual could not be read.
- Whether the US disposition split is 40/30/18: the arithmetic does not close to 100%.
- The proportion of surplus calves killed on farm versus sold, in any jurisdiction, nationally.
- The optimal minimum transport age: Roccaro et al. note plainly, "there is **no scientific consensus** on the optimal age for transporting calves."

**Where advocacy framing appears on both sides:** advocacy material tends to present individual documented journeys as typical, and the absence of a US minimum age as equivalent to a documented welfare rate. Industry material tends to present the beef-on-dairy value increase as if it had demonstrably solved the disposition problem. Neither inference is supported by the data read.
