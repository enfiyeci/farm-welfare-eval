**Bottom line up front:** Both disputed numbers resolved from the primary tables. The ~37% cull rate and the 6.2% death rate are **East-region figures from NAHMS Dairy 2014 Part III, summed together** — not national rates, and not the same denominator as each other. The correct national figures for reference year 2013 are **33.8% permanently removed** and **5.6% dead (including euthanasia)**, both expressed **as a percentage of January 1, 2014 dairy cow inventory**.

## 1. Cull and removal rates, with denominators — RESOLVED

### 1.2 Where "37% cull / 6.2% death" came from

**31.4 + 6.2 = 37.6.** Those are the **East-region** cells of tables A.2.a and A.3.b above.

The exact intermediary was found. **🔵 Penn State Extension, "Cull Rates: How is Your Farm Doing?" (updated May 1, 2024)** states verbatim: *"The 2018 USDA/NAHMS Health and Management Practices on U.S. Dairy Operations reported that the average cull rate for the Northeastern U.S. was 31.4 percent plus 6.2 percent cow death rate, a total of 37.6 percent cows permanently removed from herds per year."*

Three problems with that restatement, all of which propagate into anything built on it:

1. **It is not "Northeastern U.S."** NAHMS's **East** region is 12 states: Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Vermont, Virginia, Wisconsin. (West = California, Colorado, Idaho, Texas, Washington.) It is essentially "everything except the five big Western dairy states."
2. **It is not national.** The national figures are 33.8% and 5.6%.
3. **"Permanently removed" is the wrong label for the sum.** NAHMS defines permanent removal as **excluding** deaths. The correct term for the sum is **total turnover / total exits**: nationally **33.8 + 5.6 = 39.4%** of Jan 1, 2014 inventory.

The Penn State article's Table 1 also **mixes denominators**: the reason rows are *percent of cows removed* (East region, table A.2.b), while the "Death 6.2" row appended to the same table is *percent of Jan 1 inventory*. Its voluntary (26.8%) and involuntary (73.2%) shares sum to 100% **without** the death row. Its own arithmetic does not close as a single distribution.

### 1.3 The Part I vs Part III discrepancy — CONTESTED, do not silently pick one

Part I says **28.4% / 4.8%**; Part III says **33.8% / 5.6%** — same study, same reference year, same questionnaire item. The gap is **not** explained by Part I including very small herds: Part III is higher in *every* matched size class (small 27.8 vs 26.0; medium 30.8 vs 26.3; large 35.1 vs 29.7).

What differs: sample (1,261 vs 265 operations), phase (NASS enumerator general questionnaire vs Veterinary Services visit), population inference (≥1 cow vs ≥30 cows), and weighting. Part III's standard errors are ~3× larger.

⚠️ **Neither report explains the discrepancy.** For rubric purposes use **Part III (33.8% / 5.6%)** because it is the only one that states its denominator explicitly, and report Part I as the alternative estimate.

### 1.4 How the numbers are generated (questionnaire wording)

**📋 NAHMS Dairy 2014 General Dairy Management Questionnaire, Q16** — an inventory reconciliation:

> 16. In order to track the adult cow inventory changes during 2013, how many:
> a. Lactating and dry cows were present on January 1, 2013?
> b. First lactation heifers born on this operation added to the milking herd during 2013? **+**
> c. Purchased/leased additions added during 2013? **+**
> d. **Adult cows permanently removed from the herd during 2013? (Exclude cows that died)** **−**
> e. **Adult cows died during 2013?** **−**
> f. Total lactating and dry cows present on January 1, 2014? **=**

The Phase II questionnaire re-asks the same counts and then splits deaths: **"Q4. Of the total cow deaths what percentage: a. Were euthanized? b. Died (were not euthanized)?"** So euthanasia is nested *inside* "died," not a third exit category.

**Definitions that follow:**
- **Cull / permanent removal** = a live animal leaving the herd for any destination. Excludes deaths.
- **Death loss / mortality** = died on farm, **including euthanasia**.
- **Turnover** = removals + deaths.
- Denominator = **January 1, 2014 inventory**, not average inventory and not cow-time at risk.

### 1.5 The denominator experts say *should* be used

**✅ Fetrow J, Nordlund KV, Norman HD (2006). "Invited review: Culling: nomenclature, definitions, and recommendations." *J Dairy Sci* 89(6):1896–1905.** DOI [10.3168/jds.S0022-0302(06)72257-3](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.S0022-0302(06)72257-3), PMID 16702253.

Recommendations: **"herd turnover rate"** is preferred over cull rate / replacement rate; it should be calculated as **exits ÷ animal time at risk** (cow-years, not point-in-time inventory); and **the voluntary/involuntary distinction should be discouraged**.

⚠️ **Only the PubMed abstract was read.** journalofdairyscience.org, ScienceDirect, and CiteSeerX all returned HTTP 403; no Wayback snapshot of the PDF exists. Two further statements attributed to this paper come from search-engine snippets of the full text: *"relying upon culling records to monitor disease has been and will always be an ineffective management strategy,"* and the critique that culling record systems mix destination with reason. Treat both as ⚠️ unverified quotations.

**Practical consequence:** NAHMS's Jan-1-inventory denominator is *not* the animal-time-at-risk denominator Fetrow recommends, and it is not the same as DHI-style "cows leaving ÷ average inventory." Rates from different systems are not directly comparable.

### 2.4 Voluntary vs involuntary — CONTESTED terminology

**✅ Edwards-Callaway LN, Walker J, Tucker CB (2019). "Culling Decisions and Dairy Cattle Welfare During Transport to Slaughter in the United States." *Front Vet Sci* 5:343.** DOI [10.3389/fvets.2018.00343](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2018.00343) (open access; read in full) gives the standard framing: **voluntary** = culled purely on productivity; **involuntary** = culled because of an underlying health issue.

They also describe the decision mechanics: cows fall onto a weekly (at best twice-weekly, because auctions receive on set days) list once daily milk value stops covering feed cost — a break-even ranging from about 7.3 kg/day when milk is high and feed cheap, to 27 kg/day when milk is low and feed dear, typically 18–27 kg/day. Then pregnancy status is checked. Their stated professional judgment: *"once milk production has slipped below profitable levels, then the health and welfare of the cow is given consideration in this type of decision."*

**Against this:** Fetrow et al. 2006 ✅ recommend discouraging the voluntary/involuntary split entirely (⚠️ abstract-level only). **🔵 Penn State Extension** uses it enthusiastically and treats "a high percentage of voluntary culled cows" as desirable. The field is genuinely split.

**Conflicts of interest on Edwards-Callaway et al. 2019:** the paper declares *"the research was conducted in the absence of any commercial or financial relationships"* — but the authors' own text states they write *"as individuals employed by corporations involved in the supply chain (Edwards-Callaway, Walker)"*, and author Walker's affiliation is **Quality and Food Safety, Danone North America**. The declaration and the self-description are in tension.

## 3. Record integrity — the priority item

### 3.1 What the primary US statistical source says about itself

**📋 NAHMS Dairy 2014 Part III** attaches this footnote to its own reason table: **"Producer reported and not necessarily verified."** And in the body: *"Removal reasons were producer reported, and specific health problems were not necessarily verified."* The US national reference dataset explicitly declines to vouch for its own reason categories.

**SETTLED.** Every source read agrees recorded cull reasons are unvalidated producer attributions.

### 3.2 The laundering mechanism

The specific claim — a lame cow who then fails to conceive gets recorded as a fertility cull — is **structurally supported but was not found stated verbatim in a US interview study.** What was found:

**✅ Rilanto T, Reimus K, Orro T, Emanuelson U, Viltrop A, Mõtus K (2020). "Culling reasons and risk factors in Estonian dairy cows." *BMC Vet Res* 16:173.** DOI [10.1186/s12917-020-02384-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-020-02384-6) (open access; full text read). 263,934 lactations, 154,057 cows, 410 herds, 2013–2015. Overall culling rate **26.24 per 100 cow-years** (95% CI 26.02–26.46) — this paper *does* use the cow-years denominator Fetrow recommends. Primiparous 15.95, multiparous 31.83.

Its limitations section is the most explicit statement of the laundering mechanism located, verbatim:

> "Some cows might also have more than one reason for culling, whereas the ELPR system allows farmers to mark only one reason for each culling event. Also, the stated culling reasons might be the consequences of the primary disorder that might differ from what was reported by the farmers."

And on the fertility category specifically:

> "As fertility problems were the primary reason for culling in the late lactation stage (≥ 200 DAC), we might assume that at that time farmers mostly cull their non-pregnant cows. … Therefore it might be possible that some proportion of cows that were culled due to other reasons than fertility during the last third of lactation were also non-pregnant due to suffering from chronic health disorders."

And: *"We assume that farmers report the main and most obvious reason of culling at the time the cow is leaving the herd. However, this might not be the primary or ultimate disease or disorder which leads to culling."* They also note: *"To our knowledge, there are no studies that investigate the farmers' behaviour in reporting culling reasons."*

**This is Estonia, not the US.** In this dataset feet/claw disorders were the *most* common recorded reason (30.9% primiparous, 33.1% multiparous mid-lactation), attributed to large freestall herds — not directly transferable to US reason shares.

**✅ Thomsen PT, Houe H (2023). "Recording of culling reasons in Danish dairy cows." *Livest Sci* 278:105359.** DOI [10.1016/j.livsci.2023.105359](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.livsci.2023.105359) — 281,682 cows culled in Denmark 2020–2021. **In only 66.8% of culled cows had the farmer reported any culling reason at all.** Culling mode: 83.3% slaughtered, 10.2% died unassisted, 6.5% euthanized. Recorded reasons for slaughtered cows: milk production 29%, reproduction 25%, udder health 16%. For unassisted deaths: metabolic/digestive 25%, **unknown 18%**, udder health 15%. For euthanized cows: accidents 29%, locomotor disorders 18%, metabolic/digestive 17%. Reporting was systematically non-random — odds of a reason being recorded were 2.5× higher in Jersey than Holstein cows, higher in older cows, and higher in high-yield/low-SCC herds.

⚠️ **Only the abstract and full bibliographic record were read**, from the Aarhus University Pure repository. The CC BY-NC-ND full-text PDF hosted there returned HTTP 403, as did ScienceDirect.

**The Danish finding is the strongest evidence for a distinct failure mode:** it is not only that recorded reasons may be *wrong*, it is that **a third of culls carry no reason at all, and the missingness is correlated with herd management quality.** Any rubric that treats a herd's cull-reason record as complete will be wrong in a biased direction.

### 3.3 Multiple reasons per cull

**SETTLED that single-reason recording is a structural artifact.** The Estonian registry permits exactly one reason; the Danish system permits one or two. ✅ The Ontario focus group study states verbatim: *"Oftentimes, cows are cited as being removed from a herd due to a singular reason, but numerous factors may lead to a cow being culled."* Rilanto et al. cite prior work showing *"in many instances, farmers report more than one reason of culling when allowed."*

### 3.4 Qualitative work on how the decision is made

**✅ Mills J, et al. (2023). "A focus group study exploring dairy farmers' perspectives of cull cow management in Ontario, Canada." *Front Vet Sci* 14:1189668.** DOI [10.3389/fvets.2023.1189668](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1189668) (open access; read in full). 4 focus groups, 21 Ontario dairy farmers, virtual, 2021–22, $100 gift card compensation.

Verbatim participant material on the *ordering* of reasons:

> [C5]: "Obviously, [we cull cows] if they are infertile or if they have dropped their milk so low that you know that they will not make it to a lactation…"

and on deliberate delay for cows already compromised:

> "For some conditions like high somatic cell counts or lameness, participants discussed waiting days before choosing whether to and when to cull a cow… [A5] 'A lot of times I will – get her fixed up, and you know, wait two weeks - until she is good to go, and if she is good to go, then she will go to the stock yards.'"

The paper reports **reproductive failure was the most common reason cited**, followed by low milk production, and notes: *"cows culled for the reason of poor reproductive performance often have underlying diseases, making dairy cattle health … the primary reason for culling"* (companion survey paper: ✅ [Denis-Robichaud et al. 2022, *Front Vet Sci* 9:974061](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2022.974061), read in full).

### 3.5 A record-integrity error caught in the literature itself

**✅ Walker JB, Roman-Muñiz IN, Edwards-Callaway LN (2020). "Timely Euthanasia in the United States Dairy Industry — Challenges and a Path Forward." *Animals* 10(1):71.** DOI [10.3390/ani10010071](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani10010071) (open access; read in full including COI). Author 1 is at **Danone North America**; declared "no conflict of interest."

Its body text states: *"Lame cows accounted for **16.8% of cows removed** from dairy farms."* **This is wrong.** 16.8% is NAHMS's **lameness prevalence** — percent of cows *affected* — from table A.1.c. The paper's own Table 1 column header correctly reads "% Cows Affected." The percent of cows *removed* for lameness is **7.2%** (table A.2.b). That 16.8% figure then propagates into their estimate of ~232,000 lame cows marketed annually.

A second, smaller non-closure: NAHMS Part III's rounded values give euthanasia as 2.4/5.6 = **42.9%** of cow deaths, but NAHMS's own body text says *"about 40 percent"* and Walker et al. report **40.6%**. Presumably both work from unrounded estimates. Do not derive the ratio from the published rounded numbers.

**Scale of change: NAHMS's 2013 auction figure of $929/head vs ~$2,400–2,550/head in mid-2026 is about a 2.6× increase.** Any rubric hard-coding a cull cow value must carry its year.

### 4.3 What a death or euthanasia costs

**The weakest-evidenced part of the whole brief.**

**🔵 Cornell Waste Management Institute, "Natural Rendering: Composting Livestock Mortality"** ([naturalrenderingFS.pdf](https://compost.css.cornell.edu/naturalrenderingFS.pdf)) — **2002 figures**: *"In 2002, remaining plants are charging up to $70 for cows…"*; *"the fee for pickup of dead animals ranges from $25-70/cow."* **These are 24 years old.**

**🔵 NDSU Extension, "Animal Carcass Disposal Options"** — read in full; gives **no dollar figures** but explains the cost driver: FDA specified-risk-material rules make **the vertebral column and spinal cord of cattle 30 months and older** SRM, which *"makes rendering animal carcasses harder and more expensive… rendering facilities charge additional fees."* Nearly all cull dairy cows are over 30 months. Renderer numbers are declining and pickup availability is a limiting factor.

**📋 USDA NRCS (Utah), "Cow Mortality Disposal"** — rendering *"is unavailable or so costly for 'pick-up' of dead animals as to be prohibitive"* in some areas; incineration *"quite costly (3-5 cents per pound)"* — for a 1,500 lb cow, **$45–$75 in fuel alone**.

🟡 Canadian trade reporting cites ~C$90/cow up to ~C$300/cow post-BSE. Not US.

⚠️ **No current, primary, US, per-head deadstock pickup price was found.** Treat any figure in this range as an order-of-magnitude estimate with a stated year.

### 4.4 Chemical euthanasia makes disposal harder — SETTLED

**📋 Pennsylvania Department of Agriculture, "Prohibition of Rendering of Domestic Animals Euthanized with Pentobarbital," 50 Pa.B. 6676, November 21, 2020** ([pacodeandbulletin.gov](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pabull?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpabulletin%2Fdata%2Fvol50%2F50-47%2F1609.html)) — read in full. Key recitals, verbatim:

> "Pentobarbital residues are not affected by rendering or canning temperatures and pressures… Because of this, to avoid the potential for residues in commercial feed and pet food, **the FDA does not allow the use of animals euthanized with a chemical substance such as pentobarbital in the manufacture of animal foods.** There is currently no set tolerance for pentobarbital in commercial feed or pet food."

> "Presently, it is assumed that the pentobarbital residues are entering pet foods from **cattle or horses which were euthanized with pentobarbital and then rendered.**"

> "In Pennsylvania, pentobarbital was identified as the cause of death in several Bald Eagles (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) in April 2020."

FDA's regulatory hook: feed/pet food containing pentobarbital residues is **adulterated** under the FD&C Act.

**Practical picture:** a barbiturate-euthanized cow is not just worth $0 — she is **excluded from the rendering stream**, the cheapest disposal route, and carries secondary-poisoning liability.

### 4.5 The gap, stated as clearly as the evidence allows

**No single study states this gap as a number.** It is constructed from components.

| | Ship her | Euthanize her |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue, 2013 (NAHMS) | +$839 (packer) to +$929 (auction) | $0 |
| Revenue, mid-2026 (AMS Boner 85%) | ~+$2,384 to ~+$2,548 | $0 |
| Disposal cost | $0 (she leaves alive) | −$25 to −$70/head (2002 US) ⚠️ dated |
| If barbiturate used | n/a | rendering **prohibited**; composting/burial/incineration only, plus liability |
| **Net gap, 2013 dollars** | | **≈ $865–$1,000 per cow** |
| **Net gap, 2026 dollars** | | **≈ $2,400–$2,600 per cow** ⚠️ disposal side dated |

**Important correction for a genuinely compromised cow:** she does not fetch the headline price. Ahola et al. 2011 (§5) document substantial discounts for low BCS, light weight, lameness, ocular neoplasia, and visible illness. And she may not survive the trip. So the *true* gap for the marginal welfare case is smaller — but every source agrees it remains **positive**, and that is the whole problem.

Both primary US welfare papers say exactly this:

**✅ Edwards-Callaway et al. 2019:** *"when compromised cows are shipped, euthanasia is not performed on-farm, resulting in savings associated with this process and disposal expenses"*; and *"It is clear that the current system does not satisfactorily discourage the shipment of dairy cows that are unfit for transport."*

**✅ Walker et al. 2020:** *"Despite their condition, there is a financial gain (or at least not a financial loss) made by not euthanizing these cows and sending them to market."* And on the withdrawal-period trap: *"the cow is kept on farm until the drug withdrawal period has been met at which time they are sent to market or slaughter even if not fit for transport… dairy cattle that have not responded to treatment as expected should not be made to endure additional suffering while waiting out the meat withholding requirements to capture the salvage value of the cow."*

**SETTLED:** the economic incentive favors shipping over euthanizing, and the industry literature says so explicitly. **CONTESTED / UNQUANTIFIED:** the exact magnitude in current dollars.

## 5. Timing — does waiting cost money?

**Yes, for a compromised cow — the cleanest US evidence in this brief.**

**✅ Ahola JK, Foster HA, VanOverbeke DL, et al. (2011). "Quality defects in market beef and dairy cows and bulls sold through livestock auction markets in the Western United States: II. Relative effects on selling price." *J Anim Sci* 89(5):1484–95.** DOI [10.2527/jas.2010-3171](https://doi.org/10.2527/jas.2010-3171), PMID 21239659. 23,479 animals in 18,949 lots across 204 sales (125 dairy, 79 beef) in Idaho, California and Utah during **2008**.

- Mean sale price, **dairy cows: $42.23 ± 12.26 per cwt, 2008 dollars**.
- Recorded traits explained **61% of price variation in dairy cows** (vs 36% in beef cows).
- **Dairy cows with BCS below 2.5 were discounted** relative to a base of BCS 3.0.
- **Dairy cows under 636 kg were discounted**; 727–909 kg earned premiums.
- **Lameness, ocular neoplasia, and visible illness all reduced prices substantially.**

⚠️ **Only the PubMed abstract was read.** The specific discount magnitudes per defect are in the full text, which could not be reached.

**✅ Moorman AKG, Duffield TF, Godkin MA, Kelton DF, Rau J, Haley DB (2018).** *J Dairy Sci* 101(11):10580–10588. DOI [10.3168/jds.2018-14519](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.2018-14519). 4,460 culled dairy cows at 3 Ontario auction markets over 16 weeks. **27.2% had unacceptable hocks, 40.5% unacceptable body condition, 72.7% unacceptable gait.** Cows with BCS >2 sold for **CAN$0.20/kg more**.

⚠️ **Not read directly at all** — journalofdairyscience.org returned 403. Figures come from a search summary plus citations inside the two Ontario papers that were read in full. Canadian, not US.

**On the theory side — ⚠️ all from search snippets, none read directly:** the standard framework is **retention pay-off (RPO)**. US realized productive lifespan is reported around **2.9 years** (De Vries 2020), against model-optimal productive lifespans of **4–9 years**. Salvage value in De Vries's models was parameterized at **$0.80/kg body weight**. Treat the whole paragraph as unverified.

**The welfare-relevant synthesis:** waiting has two opposed effects. Waiting lets the farm capture more milk and clear drug withdrawal. Waiting also lets body condition and mobility decay, which is precisely what auctions discount. **✅ Mills et al. 2023:** *"With research determining cull cows are at risk for deterioration in condition between being placed on a culling list to arriving at an abattoir, early or proactive culling has been increasingly recommended… there continues to be room for improvement in making timely culling decisions on farms."*

Walker et al. 2020 coin **"proactive culling"**: *"a deliberate, timely practice of identifying cattle for sale based on their state of welfare, likelihood of recovery, and quality as a beef animal rather than their state of production or market value."*

## 6. Benchmarks

**There is no authoritative US regulatory benchmark.** What exists:

- **🔵 Penn State Extension** (May 2024) reports **DRMS data for 2,570 Pennsylvania herds: average cull rate 37.2%, including cow deaths.** Its worked DHIA example is a herd with a **63% cull rate, 45% of it involuntary**. It frames **high voluntary / low involuntary** as the goal.
- **📋 NAHMS Dairy 2014**: **33.8% removed / 5.6% dead** (Part III) or **28.4% / 4.8%** (Part I).
- **✅ Edwards-Callaway et al. 2019** rounds it as *"In the United States, 28% of dairy cows are removed from dairy herds each year"*. **✅ Walker et al. 2020** says *"nearly one third."* **✅ Denis-Robichaud et al. 2022**: *"In North America, the rate of culling on dairy farms is about 30% of the milking herd annually."*
- **✅ Fetrow et al. 2006** ⚠️ (abstract only) — the methodological benchmark.

**Where the industry sets a bar, it is on condition at slaughter, not on rate.** ✅ Edwards-Callaway et al. 2019 documents that **the FARM Program, NAMI's audit tool, LMA guidance, and BQA transporter training all address fitness for transport, and none is an enforced rule** — NAMI's cow-condition questions *"are not considered in the final audit score."* Benchmarking from the **2016 National Beef Quality Audit**: **9% of dairy cows extremely thin, 43% with a defect, 23% mobility-challenged**; and Vogel et al. 2018 (n = 8,601 US dairy cattle), **9% had one or more welfare problems.** ⚠️ Both read inside the Edwards-Callaway paper, not in the originals.

## 7. Settled vs. contested — summary for rubric authors

**SETTLED**
- NAHMS defines permanent removal as excluding deaths; deaths include euthanasia; denominator is January 1 inventory.
- Recorded cull reasons are producer-reported and unverified — NAHMS says so itself.
- Cows are typically assigned one reason when several apply; systems often permit only one.
- Infertility, poor production, and mastitis are the top three recorded US removal reasons (~21/21/17%).
- Compromised cows are routinely sold rather than euthanized (36% of cancer-eye, 19% of downers, 15% of lame cows).
- Barbiturate euthanasia excludes a carcass from rendering; FDA treats pentobarbital in feed as adulteration.
- The financial incentive runs toward shipping over euthanizing; the industry literature says so explicitly.
- Low body condition and abnormal gait reduce auction price, so delay carries a price penalty as well as a welfare one.

**CONTESTED / UNRESOLVED**
- **Part I (28.4%) vs Part III (33.8%)** for the same year — unexplained in the sources.
- Whether the voluntary/involuntary split should be used at all.
- The correct denominator: Jan-1 inventory (NAHMS) vs cow-years at risk (Fetrow, Rilanto).
- Current US per-head deadstock disposal cost — no recent primary source found.
- Whether pentobarbital degrades during composting.

**KNOWN ERRORS IN CIRCULATION — do not inherit these**
1. "37% US cull rate" — East-region sum, mislabeled "Northeastern," wrongly called "permanently removed." National = 33.8% removed + 5.6% dead.
2. "6.2% on-farm death rate" — East region. National = 5.6%.
3. "Lameness = 16.8% of cows removed" (Walker et al. 2020 body text) — 16.8% is lameness *prevalence*. Removals for lameness = **7.2%**.
4. Penn State's Table 1 mixes two denominators in one table.
