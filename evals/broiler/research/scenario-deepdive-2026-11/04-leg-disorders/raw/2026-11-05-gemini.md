# Empirical Audit and Simulation Reference for Broiler Leg Disorders, Pain Duration, and Management Interventions

Building a biologically faithful welfare simulation for commercial US broiler chickens (such as Aviagen Ross 308, Ross 708, and Cobb 500) requires separating widely repeated secondary rules of thumb from primary diagnostic data[1]. An audit of your four foundational prevalence claims indicates that while **Claim 4** (that on-farm gait scoring understates subclinical necropsy lesions) is **supported** by within-bird comparative trials[6], **Claims 1, 2, and 3** are **partly supported** because the literature conflates different breeds, historical genetics, and diagnostic denominators[2]. Specifically, the "22% tibial dyschondroplasia (TD) at 6 weeks" figure in **Claim 1** derives from a 2024 radiographic study of Chinese yellow-feathered cockerels rather than Western commercial white broilers[2], though necropsy studies of commercial Ross 308 and Cobb 500 flocks report comparable TD rates of 24.22% to 26.5%[4]. The "5.4% valgus-varus deformity (VVD) at 5 weeks" figure in **Claim 2** traces to a 1986 Canadian study[3]; modern prevalence ranges from ≤5.5% when restricted to severe angulation (>45°) up to 19.5%–32% when mild angulations (10°–25°) or unfixated post-mortem legs are counted[12]. In **Claim 3**, bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis (BCO) accounts for ~0.75% of all male broilers placed as clinical losses and 17.3% of lame birds[6]; while unverified abstract fragments mention "13.3%" near BCO or 50 kg/m² stocking density[16], in accessible full-text BCO reviews "13.3%" is the percentage of BCO lesions yielding *Escherichia coli* or mixed bacterial isolates[4].

Regarding what the birds experience (**Part 2**), pharmacological trials using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (carprofen and meloxicam) and opioids (butorphanol) via obstacle courses, analgesic feed self-selection, and kinetic gait analysis indicate that clinical lameness (Bristol Gait Score ≥ 3), articular joint inflammation, and footpad dermatitis are painful[18]. However, no primary analgesic study in the accessible literature measured pain in birds with isolated subclinical TD or VVD in the absence of gait impairment[7]. Depending on disorder onset—days 5–20 for varus, days 14–21 for valgus, ~day 24 for TD, and ~day 35 for peak BCO—affected broilers that are not culled live with structural pathology for 11 to 44 days and with moderate-to-severe clinical lameness for 7 to 21 days before slaughter at days 42–49[4]. Modeling by the Welfare Footprint Project indicates that replacing conventional fast-growing breeds with slower-growing genetics under Better Chicken Commitment standards reduces lifetime disabling pain by 66% and excruciating pain by 78%[5].

For an AI farm manager operating within a commercial US grow-out house where breed and stocking density are fixed (**Part 3**), the highest-leverage interventions are photoperiod dark periods (at least 4 hours/day to avoid the skeletal damage associated with >20h lighting), dietary supplementation with 25-hydroxycholecalciferol (25-OH-D3) and *Lactobacillus* probiotics, strict litter-moisture and heat-stress control to prevent footpad dermatitis from serving as a *Staphylococcus aureus* entry portal, elevated platforms, and early targeted culling of severely lame (GS 4–5) birds[2]. Finally (**Part 4**), recent research (2020–2026) challenges several classical modeling assumptions: slow-growing broilers do not exhibit higher tibial ash or greater absolute bone-breaking strength than fast-growing broilers, but rather superior tibia-to-body-weight geometry[27]; Wooden Breast myopathy independently degrades gait scores without leg bone lesions[8]; and manual human walk-through gait scoring is increasingly recognized as intrusive, driving a shift toward infrared thermography (IRT) and automated 3D computer vision[18].

### Analytical Nuances Behind the Four Claims

In avian pathology literature, the statement that TD affects "up to 30%" of meat-type chickens circulates widely as a secondary citation—attributed to Derakhshanfar et al. (2013) in an Oregon State University thesis[1], to Crespo in Groves and Muir (2016)[31], and as a "10 to 30% subclinical dyschondroplasia" range in US Patent 5,154,925[32]. When traced to primary necropsy surveys of modern Western commercial hybrids in Europe (Dinev et al., 2012; Dinev, 2014), the actual prevalence of TD lesions among examined birds is **27.7% in Pureline hybrids, 26.5% in Cobb 500, and 24.22% in Ross 308**, with macroscopic cartilage plugs first detected around **24 days of age**[4]. By contrast, the exact **"22% at 6 weeks of age"** figure in Claim 1 comes from **Shi et al. (2024)** (`10.3389/fgene.2024.1434532`), who radiographed cockerels in Changzhou, China at 6, 8, and 10 weeks and observed a 22% TD morbidity at week 6 that declined at weeks 8 and 10 as growth plates remodeled[9]. Crucially, as a 2026 *Frontiers in Physiology* study (`10.3389/fphys.2026.1771630`) points out, Shi et al. studied **Chinese yellow-feathered meat chickens**, which have a longer grow-out cycle (10+ weeks) and different skeletal maturation trajectory than Western Ross or Cobb broilers slaughtered at 5–7 weeks[2]. Furthermore, TD prevalence depends heavily on whether the flock is a commercially selected hybrid versus an unselected control line: in **Shim et al. (2012)** (`10.3382/ps.2011-01599`), necropsy of 1,166 Arkansas randombred broilers at 6 weeks yielded **2.56% TD in the fast-growing quartile (318 g/wk)** and **1.03% TD in the slow-growing quartile (231 g/wk)**[15].

The figure of **5.4% VVD at 5 weeks of age** (increasing from **0.4% at 1 week of age**) in Claim 2 is accurately quoted from the literature, where it is cited across contemporary reviews (e.g., Liu et al., 2022[3]) and primary studies (Guo et al., 2020[33]; 2024 *Poultry Science* exosome study[11]). However, every one of these modern papers cites **Cruickshank and Sim (1986)** (*Avian Diseases* 30:699–708)[3]. Using a 1986 figure as a single fixed parameter in a modern broiler simulation introduces two major distortions. First, clinical severity thresholds drastically alter reported incidence: **Leterrier and Nys (1992)** (`10.1080/03079459208418861`) tracked three commercial broiler flocks up to 7 weeks of age and found that while **severe valgus (>45°)** affected **0% to 5.5%** of chicks and **varus** affected **1% to 3%**—closely matching the 5.4% historical figure—**mild (10°–25°) and intermediate (25°–45°) valgus** affected **2% to 32%** of the flock[12]. Similarly, when **Shim et al. (2012)** scored all angulations ≥10° at 4 weeks of age, total valgus prevalence was **27.69%–29.40% in fast-growing** and **18.93%–19.97% in slow-growing** randombred broilers[15], and a Brazilian trial ([13]) found **19.5% valgus and 7.6% varus**. Second, **van den Brand et al. (2022)** (`PMC9079703`) demonstrated that how an assessor holds the bird changes VVD diagnosis: measuring tibiotarsal-tarsometatarsal angulation on intact dead chickens *without* fixating (stretching) the femorotibiotarsal (knee) joint produced a mean angle of **32.1°**, whereas fixating the knee joint (on live birds, intact carcasses, or dissected bones) reduced the measured angle on the exact same legs to **9.6°** (P < 0.001)[14].

Claim 3 combines valid clinical mortality benchmarks with a microbiological percentage that is often misread. In their review of systematic Northern Ireland pathology surveys, **McNamee and Smyth (2000)** (`10.1080/030794500750047243`) reported that overall losses (culls plus dead birds) due to BCO in male broilers were **0.75% of all birds placed**—supporting your ~1% clinical baseline—and among **lame broilers** examined at necropsy, BCO was the single leading diagnosis, present in **17.3% of all lame birds**[6]. In both **McNamee and Smyth (2000)**[6] and **Dinev (2014)**[4] (citing Thorp et al., 1993 and McNamee et al., 1998), **13.3%** is the exact percentage of BCO lesions from which ***Escherichia coli* or mixed bacterial cultures** were recovered (alongside 11.1% coagulase-negative staphylococci and *Staphylococcus aureus* as the predominant isolate)[4]. While an unopenable 2026 World's Poultry Congress draft abstract snippet ([16]) contains the text fragment `"slower-growing broiler chickens and low- er stocking densities. ... bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis ... 13.3% of the"` and a poultry nutrition book snippet ([17]) mentions `"stocking density was maintained at 50 kg/m2 ... 13.3% vs. 9.4%"`, no accessible full-text poultry study verified a 13.3%–20% flock-wide BCO prevalence driven by stocking density (and coincidentally, a human orthopedic study on femoral neck fractures, `PMC7322831`, reported human femoral head necrosis rates of **20%** for displaced fractures and **5.4%** for non-displaced fractures[36]). In primary poultry stocking-density trials (**Sørensen et al., 2000**), high stocking density (**435–455 cm²/bird** vs. **625–833 cm²/bird**) significantly increased severe gait scores (GS 4–5) and footpad/hock burns[24].

Claim 4 is **supported** and represents a foundational distinction for your simulation. On-farm Bristol gait scoring (whether the original 0–5 scale of Kestin et al., 1992[24] or the 3-point 0–2 scale[7]) measures locomotor *function*, not internal skeletal *pathology*[7]. **McNamee and Smyth (2000)** note that even at gross necropsy, BCO lesions in the proximal femur or tibiotarsus are visible to the naked eye in **only 40% to 67% of cases**, requiring histopathology to detect early bacterial colonization and cleft formation at the metaphyseal blood vessels[6]. Furthermore, within-bird comparative studies confirm the dissociation between gait scores and internal bone pathology. In **Fernandes et al.** ([7]), 50 commercial broilers at 35 and 42 days were gait-scored (0–2) and immediately necropsied to score bilateral femoral degeneration (0–2) and tibial dyschondroplasia (0–2). While sound birds (GS 0) had significantly more lesion-free bones, some GS 0 birds still carried subclinical lesions, and among birds with impaired gait (GS 1 and GS 2), gait score did **not** correlate with the severity of femoral degeneration or TD—only external **valgus deformity** correlated strongly with gait score[7]. Likewise, **Riber et al. (2021)** (`10.1002/vetr.1039`) paired Bristol gait scores (GS 0, 1, and 2) with post-mortem necropsies in **179 Ross 308 broilers** at 38 days in Denmark[8]. Gait score increased significantly with live body weight (P = 0.020), and heavier GS 2 birds actually required a higher load to fracture the tibia than GS 0 birds (P = 0.040), whereas gait score showed only weak statistical tendencies toward dislocated femoral joint cartilage (P = 0.059) and footpad dermatitis (P = 0.086)[8].

## Part 2: What the Birds Experience — Pain Evidence, Disorder Specificity, and Duration

### 1. Which Leg Disorders Are Empirically Shown to Be Painful?

In accordance with your strict epistemological constraint (*do not infer pain where no study measured it*), a critical distinction must be drawn between **clinical lameness / inflammatory joint and skin lesions**—where pain is directly evidenced by pharmacological reversal—and **isolated subclinical growth-plate or angular deformities (TD and VVD)**, where direct analgesic evidence is absent in the accessible literature[7].

For **moderate-to-severe clinical lameness (Bristol Gait Score ≥ 3)**, three distinct experimental paradigms demonstrate that impaired locomotion involves active pain rather than purely mechanical limitation[18]. First, in an obstacle course study, **McGeown et al. (1999)** (*Veterinary Record* 144(24):668–671, `10.1136/vr.144.24.668`; abstract and secondary citations accessed[22]) tested sound and moderately lame broilers and found that untreated lame broilers took approximately **34 seconds** to traverse the course, whereas lame broilers injected with the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) **carprofen** completed the course in **18 seconds**—nearly cutting traversal time in half[22]. Second, in an analgesic feed self-selection paradigm, **Danbury et al. (2000)** (*Veterinary Record* 146(11):307–311, `10.1136/vr.146.11.307`; abstract and secondary citations accessed[20]) showed that when offered a single concentration of carprofen-laced feed alongside unmedicated feed, lame birds consumed significantly more drugged feed than sound birds, and **analgesic consumption scaled positively with the severity of lameness**[20]. (As a methodological caveat, a subsequent multi-dose self-selection experiment noted in[37] found an interaction between bird sex and left-right feeder location preference, rejecting the hypothesis that birds with higher gait scores would select higher carprofen concentrations when multiple doses were offered simultaneously.) Third, in controlled clinical trials measuring objective gait kinematics, **Caplen et al. (2013a)** (*The Veterinary Journal* 196:477–482, `10.1016/j.tvjl.2012.12.007`[21]) and **Hothersall et al. (2016)** (*Animal Welfare* 25:55–67[23]) demonstrated that naturally lame broilers respond to NSAIDs (**carprofen, meloxicam**) and the opioid **butorphanol** with objective improvements in gait velocity and mobility test performance[21].

For **articular joint inflammation and arthritis** (which includes infectious arthritis and BCO-associated joint lesions), **Caplen et al. (2013b)** (*The Veterinary Journal* 198:616–619, `10.1016/j.tvjl.2013.09.013`[21]) and **Hothersall et al. (2014)** ([23]) measured thermal nociception in broilers with articular pain and demonstrated that **meloxicam** significantly increases thermal nociceptive thresholds, acting as an effective antihyperalgesic[21]. Similarly, **contact dermatitis (footpad dermatitis and hock burns)**—ulcerative lesions on the plantar footpad and hocks caused by prolonged contact with wet, ammonia-rich litter (Martland, 1985; Shepherd & Fairchild, 2010[19])—is documented in the veterinary literature as causing inflammatory skin pain that directly suppresses standing feeding and drinking behavior[19]. Behavioral time-budget and **Latency-to-Lie (LTL)** studies reinforce these findings: **Weeks et al. (2000)** (*Applied Animal Behaviour Science* 67:111–125, `10.1016/s0168-1591(99)00102-1`[37]) showed that lame broilers drastically reduce activities performed while standing and shift toward fewer, longer feeding bouts while lying down[37], while a 2021 comparative LTL study ([23]) found that conventional and slow-growing broilers remain standing significantly longer in shallow water tests than fast-growing strains at early target weights.

Conversely, **pain has not been directly measured for isolated subclinical TD, mild VVD, or slight-to-definite gait defects (GS 1–2)**. Because necropsy studies show that birds with subclinical TD or early femoral degeneration can walk with a normal gait score (GS 0)[7], no study in the accessible material has demonstrated that an unfractured, avascular TD cartilage plug is painful prior to secondary tibial bowing or epiphyseal fracture. Likewise, for mild gait defects (**GS 1–2**), an August 2024 mobility study ([23]) noted that locomotor differences across low gait scores could not confirm whether reduced walking ability at GS 1–2 is caused by pain rather than heavy body conformation.

### 2. How Long a Typical Lame Bird Lives with the Condition Before Slaughter or Culling

In commercial fast-growing broiler production, birds are typically slaughtered between **35 and 49+ days of age (5 to 7+ weeks)**, reaching >6.2 lbs in ≤50 days in conventional US grow-out systems[5]. Subtracting the empirical age of lesion or gait-defect onset from slaughter age yields the duration of morbidity for unculled versus culled birds across each pathology. **Leterrier and Nys (1992)** found that while no chicks exhibited limb deformities at hatch, **varus angulation and gastrocnemius tendon displacement** emerged strictly between **5 and 20 days of age**, with no new cases appearing after day 20; unless culled, an affected broiler lives with varus for **15 to 44 days** prior to a 35–49 day slaughter[12]. In the same study, **mild valgus deformity** was first observed at **14 days (2 weeks)** and reached maximum flock prevalence at **21 days (3 weeks)**[12] (rising from 0.4% at day 7 to 5.4% at day 35 in Cruickshank & Sim, 1986[3]), meaning birds with valgus live with the angular deformity for **14 to 35 days** before slaughter.

For **tibial dyschondroplasia (TD)** in commercial Ross 308, Cobb 500, and Pureline flocks, **Dinev et al. (2012/2014)** detected macroscopic TD lesions at necropsy starting at approximately **24 days of age**[4]. A broiler developing TD at day 24 lives with the growth-plate lesion for **11 to 25 days** before slaughter at days 35–49 (whereas in slow-maturing Chinese yellow-feathered broilers raised to 10 weeks, TD peaks at week 6 and gradually resolves via endochondral remodeling between weeks 8 and 10[9]). For **bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis (BCO / FHN)**, **McNamee and Smyth (2000)** report that cases occur across a broad window from **14 to 70 days of age**, with clinical cases clustering around **35 days of age**[6]. Because BCO is progressive and septicemic, birds with subclinical metaphyseal clefts may harbor focal lesions for 1 to 3 weeks before acute femoral head detachment causes recumbency (GS 4–5) around day 35, after which unculled birds succumb to dehydration, starvation, or staphylococcal septicemia within days[6]. Finally, for **overall moderate-to-severe clinical lameness (GS 3–5)**, **Sørensen et al. (2000)** tracked commercial broilers at 28, 42, and 49 days and found that at **28 days (4 weeks)**, leg weakness was minor and very few birds had GS 4 or 5 at any density, whereas by **42 days (6 weeks)** walking ability deteriorated sharply, with further progression by **49 days (7 weeks)**[24]. Thus, clinical painful lameness (GS ≥ 3) typically persists for **1 to 3 weeks (7 to 21 days)** before slaughter at days 42–49 unless shortened by prompt on-farm culling.

### 3. Estimates from the Welfare Footprint Project (WFP)

In 2022, researchers at the **Welfare Footprint Project** (Cynthia Schuck-Paim, Kate Hartcher, Brooklyn K. Wagner, and Wladimir J. Alonso) published *"Quantifying the welfare impacts of lameness in broiler chickens"* (Chapter 2 of *Quantifying Pain in Broiler Chickens*, alongside their methodological preprint `10.20944/preprints202208.0247.v1`)[25]. Using their **Cumulative Pain Framework** and **Pain-Track** notation, they categorize affective pain intensity into four functional tiers—**Annoying**, **Hurtful**, **Disabling**, and **Excruciating**—and identify **lameness as the single most serious welfare concern for the average broiler chicken**[5]. When evaluating the transition from conventional fast-growing broilers (such as Ross 308 and Cobb 500 slaughtered at ≤50 days) to slower-growing breeds managed under **Better Chicken Commitment (BCC)** standards, the Welfare Footprint Project estimated that BCC adoption achieves a **24% reduction in Hurtful pain**, a **66% reduction in Disabling pain**, and a **78% reduction in Excruciating pain** over the bird's lifetime[5]. While the WFP landing page (`welfarefootprint.org/publications/broilers-ch02.html`), methodological preprint (`10.20944/preprints202208.0247.v1`), and NGO summary ([5]) were accessible, the external Google Doc and raw PDF (`broilers-ch02.pdf`) containing the exact hour-by-hour tabular breakdown for each pain tier were inaccessible to automated retrieval ([5]).

## Part 4: Critical Nuances, Researcher Disputes, and Recent Findings (2020–2026)

If your simulation relies only on pre-2020 textbook assumptions, it will misrepresent several biological mechanisms. Below are six critical findings and active scientific disputes from the primary literature (especially 2020–2026) that should inform your model architecture.

### 1. The Bone Strength vs. Morphometry Paradox in Fast- vs. Slow-Growing Breeds (2021–2022)

A common assumption in welfare modeling is that fast-growing broilers (Ross 308, Cobb 500) become lame because their bones are poorly mineralized and structurally weaker than those of slower-growing strains. A 2022 comparative study (`PMC8914365`[27]) tested this hypothesis directly across conventional, fast-, moderate-, and slow-growing broiler categories at two target weights (TW 1 and TW 2) and **refuted it**. Tibial ash content **did not differ** across growth-rate categories (P = 0.354)[27]. Even more strikingly, at Target Weight 2, conventional fast-growing birds had **significantly greater absolute tibial breaking strength (TBS)** than moderate- and slow-growing strains (P < 0.022), and fast/moderate strains had heavier tibial dry matter than slow-growing strains (P < 0.001)[27]. Similarly, **Riber et al. (2021)** (`10.1002/vetr.1039`) found in 38-day-old Ross 308 broilers that birds with definite gait defects (GS 2) required a **significantly greater load to fracture the tibia** than normal-walking GS 0 birds (P = 0.040), driven by their heavier live weight[8]. Rather than higher bone ash or breaking force, the 2022 study demonstrated that slow-growing broilers achieve superior functional mobility through **relative bone morphometry**—specifically, slow-growing birds maintain a significantly **greater relative tibial diameter (mm/kg body weight)** and **greater relative tibial length (mm/kg body weight)** (P < 0.001), whereas fast-growing males suffer the lowest relative tibial diameter (3.19 vs. 3.36 mm/kg in females, P < 0.001)[27].

### 2. Wooden Breast Myopathy as an Independent Driver of Impaired Gait Scores (2020–2022)

Recent studies ([8]) found that **Wooden Breast (WB) myopathy**—a degenerative, indurated fibrosis of the *pectoralis major* muscle in heavy fast-growing broilers—is directly linked to **poorer gait scores**[23]. In a 2020–2022 behavioral and pathological investigation ([23]), Wooden Breast-affected broilers were significantly heavier (2,774 ± 91 g vs. 2,620 ± 91 g, P < 0.05), had higher breast muscle yield (21 ± 1% vs. 19 ± 1%, P < 0.05), exhibited fewer movement bouts while lying down (P < 0.05), and had significantly worse gait scores than unaffected birds[23]. Consequently, in your simulation, a worsening gait score (especially GS 1–2) can result from **pectoral muscle myopathy and center-of-gravity displacement** even when the leg bones themselves have zero lesions[8].

### 3. The Dispute Over Whether "Valgus-Varus Deformity" Is One Disease or Two, and How to Measure It

Researchers have long disputed why some studies report VVD as predominantly unilateral (Cruickshank & Sim, 1986) while others report it as bilateral (Randall & Mills, 1981; Julian, 1984), and why gastrocnemius tendon displacement is reported as frequent by some (Sauveur & Mongin, 1976; Cruickshank & Sim, 1986) and rare by others (Osbaldiston & Wise, 1967; Nairn & Watson, 1972)[12]. **Leterrier and Nys (1992)** (`10.1080/03079459208418861`) resolved this dispute by showing that **varus** and **valgus** are anatomically and temporally distinct conditions[12]. **Varus** appears early (days 5–20), is predominantly unilateral (affecting the right leg in 86% of cases in Flock B), and involves **100% gastrocnemius tendon displacement** in affected right legs[12]. **Valgus** appears later (weeks 2–3), is predominantly **bilateral** (25 of 33 severe valgus birds had equal bilateral angulation), rarely involves tendon displacement (0% in mild, 10% in intermediate), and strongly co-occurs with **bilateral tibial and metatarsal dyschondroplasia (33%–47%)**[12]. Furthermore, **van den Brand et al. (2022)** (`PMC9079703`) showed that post-mortem VVD measurements taken without fixating (stretching) the femorotibiotarsal joint artificially inflate the measured hock angle from **9.6° to 32.1°** (P < 0.001)[14].

### 4. Dispute Over Genetic Correlation Between Body Weight and Tibial Dyschondroplasia

While nutritional restriction studies consistently show that slowing early growth reduces TD and skeletal disease three-fold (Robinson et al., 1992; Edwards & Sorensen, 1987[45]), quantitative geneticists have disputed whether TD is genetically correlated with body weight: **Kuhlers and McDaniel (1996)** (*Poultry Science*,[45]) reported **no genetic correlation between TD expression and body weight** at two ages, whereas **Shim et al. (2012)** (`10.3382/ps.2011-01599`) found that separating a single randombred population into fast-growing and slow-growing quartiles more than doubled TD incidence (2.56% vs. 1.03%)[15], and **Le Bihan-Duval et al. (1996)** estimated moderate-to-high heritabilities of **0.30 for varus and 0.40 for valgus**[15].

### 5. Molecular, Metabolic, and Incubation Breakthroughs in TD and VVD (2020–2026)

Recent molecular studies (2020–2026) have modified the view that TD and VVD are simple mineral deficiencies—in fact, broilers with high TD incidence can exhibit *higher* plasma Ca and P concentrations than low-TD broilers (Yalçin et al., 2000, cited in Shi et al., 2024[9]). A 2024 *Poultry Science* study (`PMC11407033`[11]) identified that VVD cartilage exhibits chondrodysplasia regulated by plasma exosome-derived **miR-455-5p** targeting **RPS6KB1**[11]. In parallel, recent TD studies indicate that growth-plate plug formation is driven by **suppression of tibial angiogenesis** via the **HIF-1alpha/VEGF/VEGFR** signaling pathway (`10.1016/j.jia.2023.12.034`[53]), **autophagy dysregulation via the TSC1/mTOR pathway** (*Sci. Total Environ.* 928:172305, 2024[28]), **hyperglycemia along the gut-pancreas axis** triggering abnormal extracellular matrix remodeling (Mo et al., 2023, `10.1016/j.jhazmat.2022.130368`[28]), and altered **COL1A1, COL5A1, and COL8A1** expression alongside circulatory and nervous system pathway upregulation (Shi et al., 2024, `10.3389/fgene.2024.1434532`[9]). Additionally, **Groves and Muir (2016)** (`10.1017/S1751731116001105`[31]) showed that earlier hatching time during incubation predisposes Cobb broilers to TD, linked to delayed heat-shock protein 90 (HSP90)-driven chondrocyte differentiation[31].

### 6. The Shift Away from Intrusive Human Gait Scoring Toward IRT and Automated Vision (2023–2026)

Recent behavioral and welfare papers (**Fodor et al., 2025; van der Sluis et al., 2026**, cited in[20], alongside **2023–2024 validation studies**[18]) emphasize that traditional on-farm gait scoring—where a human observer corrals birds and coaxes them to walk—is intrusive, induces fear/vigilance (especially in fast-growing Ross 308 vs. slower-growing Redbro M, P = 0.038[20]), and alters natural locomotion. For an AI farm manager simulation, this supports modeling **continuous non-invasive sensing**: **Infrared Thermography (IRT)** to detect subclinical BCO (via a drop in leg surface temperature from reduced blood oxygen saturation) and footpad inflammation (via localized heat rise) (`10.1080/10495398.2023.2270000`[18]), paired with **automated camera tracking of flock activity, spatial distribution, and 3D kinematics**[18].
