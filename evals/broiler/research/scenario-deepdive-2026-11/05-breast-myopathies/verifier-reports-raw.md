## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation (and things that contradict the raw answers)

1. **Nothing in either paper observes living birds.** All data are fillets scored after slaughter and chilling; no gait, pain, lameness, or behaviour. This paper cannot support any claim about what the living bird experiences. (Both papers, read in full.)
2. **Most birds show mild-to-moderate lesions at a light 2.36 kg.** Birds were slaughtered at 36 to 43 days and 1.94 to 2.63 kg; still 82.4% had some WB and 11.8% severe WB; only 3.3% of fillets were completely clean. Per-flock severe WB ranged 2.4% to 32.8%, SM 10.4% to 66.8% (Results, Prevalence). Useful as a spread-between-flocks parameter for a reactive model.
3. **Seasonal swing in severe WB.** At Plant A (air-chilling, 4,750 fillets, 19 flocks) severe WB was highest in spring (26.6%, 95% CI 25.4 to 27.9) and lowest in winter (9.0%, 8.2 to 9.9). SM was highest in summer (39.8%) and lowest in winter (18.9%). Summer vs winter for any WB: OR 1.33 (1.05 to 1.70) in Table 5, but the text says "2.38", an internal inconsistency.
4. **Processing-line SM effect.** SM was 39.8% at the water-chilling plant vs 32.1% at the air-chilling plant (t-test, P < 0.001); the authors propose SM may be partly created at the plant by mechanical stress on a weakened scaffold, so SM is a weak proxy for the on-farm condition.
5. **Pre-slaughter handling variables associated with WB:** lairage (holding) time was U-shaped, under 111 min OR 1.31 and over 160 min OR 1.52 vs 111 to 160 min; loading over 100 min and more than 10 birds per crate also raised odds. The authors caution that these cannot act through tissue change in under 24 h, and suggest stress could alter rigor development and so change how the fillet feels when scored, i.e. a possible scoring artefact, not a real on-farm effect (Discussion, Transportation).
6. **Selection bias the authors state:** severely ill birds would have been culled before slaughter, so fillets under-represent the sickest birds.
7. **Sex:** see A5. In univariable results males had higher WB odds (1.56) and females higher SM odds (2.25); not retained after adjustment.
8. **Contradiction with the raw answers:** Gemini's "strain not reported" (A6) is wrong (all Ross 708) and Gemini's "paper did not evaluate sex" is wrong (it was collected and tested univariably). Elicit's "flock sex was collected" is right. Anyone reading the live-weight ORs as severe-WB ORs (memo C4) is wrong: they are for WB1 + WB2 combined.
9. **Companion Che 2022a (not a claim, for context):** 179 fillets from 3 flocks at one Ontario plant (April to May 2019): male Ross 708 (37 d, 2.2 kg), mixed-sex Cobb 500 (36 d, 2.4 kg), mixed-sex Ross 708 (40 d, 2.1 kg). 18.4% of fillets had no macroscopic myopathy, 68.2% had more than one. Histology of 60 fillets: myodegeneration, perivascular inflammatory cuffing and fibrosis/fat in all myopathy types; mild lesions also in macroscopically normal muscle; macroscopic vs histological score rho 0.45; no bacterial infection seen. Sampling was targeted at fillets with myopathy, so its percentages are not prevalence figures.

## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation, and contradictions with the raw answers

- No live-bird welfare content at all. The words "pain" and "welfare" appear only in one reference title (Hartcher and Lum 2019, "Genetic Selection of Broilers and Welfare Consequences"). The Introduction says only that fast growth "resulted in cardiovascular diseases, different metabolic and musculoskeletal disorders", including WS, WB and SM. Nothing on gait, activity, pain, mortality or culling. This paper cannot support any claim about what the living bird experiences.
- Mechanism lines the authors quote from others (not their own findings): omics work suggests reduced vascularity, ischemia and hypoxia leading to WB; RNA-seq shows localised hypoxia, oxidative stress and raised intracellular calcium (Mutryn 2015, via this paper).
- Relevant to group I claim I2: the Introduction states, citing Bailey et al. 2015, that environmental and management factors contribute "greater than 65%" of the variance in WS incidence and "more than 90%" of the variance in WB incidence. So that figure is Bailey's published statement, not Gemini's arithmetic, at least as relayed here (Bailey 2015 itself is group I's job).
- Relevant to group A claim A5: the Discussion says Che et al. 2022b sampled 9,250 chickens at average live weight 2.36 kg from flocks of only males, only females, and both sexes, and "did not identify sex as a risk factor" (stated for SM). This is Bordignon's description of Che, secondary to Che. It conflicts with Gemini's "Che did not evaluate sex" (Che collected flock sex but did not find an effect, per Bordignon).
- Relevant to group A: Bordignon says Che 2022b found higher live weight a risk factor for both WB and SM, high grow-out temperature a risk factor for SM, and no coccidiosis vaccination a risk factor for WB. Consistent with A7 and A9.
- Cobb 500 had higher WS odds (OR 2.75) but lower WB prevalence than Ross 308 (5.00% vs 18.7%, trial averages, Table 3; WB OR 0.35 among males, CI 0.10 to 1.26, not significant). Useful if the simulation separates strains: WS and WB do not move together by genotype in this dataset.
- The all-chicken WS average is 64.7% (range 44.5% to 77.0%); females 60.0%, males 68.6%. SM is more common in females at heavier slaughter weight (above 2,935 g).
- Weight response shape: this paper uses tertile classes (low, intermediate, high), so it gives no curve and no threshold at 2.7 kg. It reports only that the top tertile of ADG (males, WB) or slaughter weight (females, SM) carries higher odds, and that the lowest tertile does not differ from the middle one for WB or SM. WB risk is therefore "high-growth tail", not a smooth rise. ADG and slaughter weight correlate strongly (R2 0.86, Discussion).
- Meat-quality outcomes (not welfare, but memo C2-adjacent): WB alone cooking loss 29.4% vs 23.6% for normal breasts; shear force 4.30 vs 2.92 kg/g (all-animal comparison, Figure 4 text). In males WB cooking loss 30.8% vs 24.6% normal; shear force 5.34 vs 3.03 kg/g.
- Discussion also relays from other papers (not verified here): Kuttappan 2017a severe or very severe WS 14% at 6 weeks vs 69% at 9 weeks; Santos 2021 WB 41.9% males vs 26.8% females; Lake 2021 WB 87% vs 71% males vs females. Verify in groups H and I.
- Contradictions with raw answers: none of substance. Gemini's C7 attribution correction (figures come from Bordignon, not Ontario) is right. Gemini's coverage note says the supplement and the 95% CI for female WB OR 0.55 were "omitted from the accessible document text"; the CI (0.40 to 0.75) is in fact in the main text. Elicit's one-line "meta-analysis, aggregated risk factors" description is misleading for the same reason as B4 (pooled own trials, not a literature synthesis).
- Table note: the "Estimate" column in Tables 4 to 6 is effect-coded (I checked: the ORs reproduce from the estimates under effect coding), so the per-row P-values in those tables are not the same test as the OR confidence intervals. The ORs and CIs are the figures to quote.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation, and contradictions with the raw answers

All from the same paper unless noted.

1. **WB was scored on the dead bird, not by live palpation.** The abstract says "determined by palpation", but the Methods say birds were killed by CO2, the breast muscle was removed immediately and scored by one trained researcher (C.H.) on the 3-point Tijare 2016 scale. Gemini (C5 row: "Postmortem manual palpation") and Elicit ("Palpation at target age") both blur this. Do not use this paper as evidence for live detection.
2. **Gemini over-reads several things in the exercise row of its trade-offs table.** The paper contains no labour-time figure (Gemini's "60 min/weekday" is its own 6 x 10 min per pen), no equipment cost, no statement that routine wellness walks are standard on US farms, and no statement that a higher barrier could impede lame birds. Hock burn and footpad were not tested (see C5), so "no negative leg or footpad impact" is not a finding.
3. **Lameness numbers are not flat across treatments.** Ranger Gold gait >= 2: 6 exercised vs 1 control (7 of 213). Ross 708: 7 exercised vs 11 control (18 of 188, about 9.6%). The authors treat these as no difference; with these counts that is a judgement call, and every such bird had WB.
4. **Gait score 2 or above occurred only in WB birds.** That is the paper's only direct link between WB and what the living bird does (25 of 401 birds, 6.2%, mostly Ross 708). Neither the age at which gait fell nor severity of WB in those birds is reported.
5. **The reported SEs on overall mean weight look wrong.** "3.20 kg +/- 0.002" and "2.76 kg +/- 0.001" cannot be standard errors for n = 188 and n = 213 with a range of over 2 kg (that would imply a standard deviation of about 0.03 kg). Do not quote them.
6. **Design weaknesses that matter if the 12.9 vs 24.2% and 4.7% vs ~0% effects are used as reference-policy effect sizes.** Only 4 pens per cell. The tests were 1-tailed Fisher's Exact on pooled birds, with no pen term (pen was a random effect only in the BMC/BMD, body-weight and activity models), so the effect is likely overstated in precision. Strain is confounded with barn position (north vs south wall) and drinker-line height. Light 16L:8D (fluorescent), ad libitum feed named only as "Purina Flock Raiser", not described as a commercial broiler diet. Stocking: 23 to 27 birds per 9.3 m2 pen (about 2.5 to 2.9 birds/m2), far below commercial density. One scorer, blinding not stated.
7. **Context numbers the paper cites (secondary, from its Introduction and Discussion):** inactivity can be more than 76% of the time budget of fast-growing commercial broilers (Weeks 2000, Dawson 2021); Che 2022 in Canada: total WB 82.4% and severe WB 11.9% (the paper quotes 11.9%; Gemini and Group A quote 11.8%, so check A2 against Che's own table); WB cost to industry at least 200 million dollars a year (Kuttappan 2016); Caldas-Cueva 2021: 10 to 55% moderate-plus-severe WB in 2 strains at 4 ages; Santos 2021 at 48 d: WB in about 70 to 85% of conventional fast strains, moderate-to-severe about 45 to 80%, and fewer than 15% WB in the slowest-growing strains; WB seen histologically from 2 weeks (Papah 2017). I did not read Che, Caldas-Cueva, Santos, Papah or Dawson here, so these are the paper's claims about them, not verified. Groups A, G and I own those sources.
8. **The paper finds no spontaneous-activity difference at 6 weeks but cannot say anything about whether WB reduced activity.** Authors flag future work on mobility differences (Che et al. 2023) between WB and non-WB birds.
9. **Abstract typo risk:** the abstract's severe-WB sentence reverses the Ranger Gold order relative to Ross 708. Any extraction from the abstract alone (Elicit) will invert it. Use the Results text.

## 3. Extra facts and contradictions for a welfare simulation

1. **Gemini misapplies 76.9%.** Gemini's closing section says pronounced wing partition "predicts a 76.9% failure rate to self-right". In the paper 76.9% is the failure rate of severe-WB birds (20 of 26). For pronounced-partition birds the failure rate is 23 of 28 (82.1%). Keep the two groupings separate.
2. **Gemini overstates "validated".** It calls live palpation, wing partition and serum CK "validated" tools (and says Hisasaga et al. Nov 2025 "validated ... wing-partition measurement"). Actual state: the 2025 note is about palpation only, and its abstract says live palpation scores "can be variable"; Kawasaki 2016 is n = 3 with no accuracy; Kong 2021 gives a correlation with fillet firmness, no diagnostic accuracy; only Hisasaga 2026 reports sensitivity and specificity, in 58 selected birds.
3. **The 45% severe-WB share and 76.9% failure rate are sample artefacts, not flock rates.** Hisasaga 2026 deliberately balanced wing categories (26 of 58 were severe WB, only 14 normal). The sentence that catch-frame sampling found few pronounced-partition birds hints that pronounced wing impairment is a minority in a normal flock, but the paper gives no flock prevalence.
4. **What the living bird experiences (Hisasaga 2026).** A bird with severe WB placed on its back usually could not right itself within a minute (20 of 26), while normal birds righted in about 1.4 s. The test is a 60 s trial on litter, not an observation of how long birds stay stuck on the farm; it measures no pain or distress. The authors argue it is a welfare concern citing Gall et al. 2019 (pressure of the breast on heart and lungs, pulmonary congestion, death if on the back a long time), and say few studies have looked at how WB affects the animals themselves.
5. **Left breast weight (Hisasaga 2026):** normal 331 g, moderate WB 411 g, severe WB 436 g (normal differs from both, P < 0.05; moderate vs severe P = 0.36). Same-company flocks, three unidentified fast-growth strains, birds 45 to 52 d old.
6. **Welfare outcomes in Kawasaki 2018 (small, hand-picked birds, n = 10 per group):** of the 10 affected, 5 appeared exhausted with markedly suppressed weight gain and 4 died (at about 37, 44, 52 and 54 d); 3 had splayed-leg weakness from 29 to 42 d; 2 had ascites; 1 had deep pectoral myopathy. No unaffected bird died. Affected birds were heavier early (0.98 vs 0.85 kg at 19 and 27 d) and lighter after 41 d. These outcomes come from a deliberately selected, early-hardened subgroup and should not be read as typical WB.
7. **Che 2022c, text read while checking F8 (Group D will cover it fully):** DRS birds were bright and alert and could walk when helped to stand, but most could not fully abduct their wings (lifted to under 45 degrees); some had skin ulceration or feather loss at wing pressure points, most bilateral; market weights in the flocks ranged 2.8 to 4.2 kg. The authors link the wing limitation to reduced P. major extensibility.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

1. **Live-bird signs are thin at the time of onset.** Sihvo's dissertation: daily observation of the experimental flocks "revealed no clinical signs associated with WB", but only a short visual check without physical examination or prolonged movement observation. Handlers weighing and euthanising birds noticed no change in breast hardness. Source: Sihvo dissertation, discussion section "Clinical signs and welfare issues related to WB" (read).
2. **What the authors say about welfare and pain.** Sihvo cites reduced wing mobility (Kawasaki 2016, Papah 2017), inability to rise from dorsal recumbency and decreased activity (Papah 2017), and poorer standing and walking (Norring et al., then "submitted"), and concludes "WB reduces the welfare of the affected animals". On pain: "the amount of pain associated with WB is difficult to demonstrate" and the only support offered is that severe myodegeneration in humans causes substantial pain (analogy, not measurement). Source: same section (read). These are secondary statements in the dissertation, not primary evidence from Sihvo's own birds.
3. **Ante mortem detection by palpation** has been possible only at about 4 to 6 weeks at the earliest (Clark and Velleman 2016, Papah 2017, Griffin 2018, as cited by Sihvo). So a live-bird check before about 4 weeks would miss lesions that are already present at 2 to 3 weeks. Source: Sihvo dissertation (read).
4. **Prevalence is not monotonic with age in Sihvo's data**: 73% at 35 d, 53% at 38 d, 65% at 42 d (different birds per age, euthanised from a flock; the authors say prevalence peaked around 35 days). A simulation built on "prevalence rises steadily to slaughter" would not match this data set. Source: dissertation Table 6 (read).
5. **Feed restriction delays lesions only while it lasts.** Sihvo's discussion, citing Radaelli 2017, says the restriction (weeks 2 to 3) delayed lesions, but the effect was lost once ad libitum feeding resumed. Radaelli's abstract agrees: "no residual effect was recorded after re-alimentation". Source: Sihvo dissertation (read) and Radaelli abstract.
6. **Weight is not clearly a cause at young ages.** Sihvo found no significant live weight difference between WB and unaffected birds in rearing 1, and argues most weight studies are at slaughter age and cannot separate cause from effect. Chen 2019's abstract does report a significant correlation with body weight. Source: dissertation discussion (read); Chen abstract.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

Facts from the sources I read directly (what the living bird experiences is thin here; these are mostly meat and genetics papers):

1. Santos 2021, discussion (secondhand citations of live-bird studies, not Santos's own data): WB lesions are detectable as early as 1 week of age (Papah 2017); birds with WB were unable to right themselves after falling on their backs and were reluctant to move (Papah 2017); Gall 2019 linked WB to dorsal recumbency, pulmonary disease and late mortality; Norring 2018 found poorer gait and less movement while lying; Lake 2020 found blood gas changes consistent with respiratory acidosis in WB males; Kawasaki 2016 found WB birds could not fully lift their wings. Santos et al. conclude these "may have significant welfare implications and should be further investigated". The same text says Papah did not assess pain. Santos also reports in unpublished data that plasma AST, LDH and CK were highest in the conventional strains and strain F, with CK 3.9 to 5.8 times higher in strain F than in the other FAST strains. These secondhand statements need tracing to their own papers (other groups cover this).
2. Aguirre 2020, citing Griffin: white striping first appeared at day 16 and the first WB at day 23, with more severe features from day 30 (secondhand). Aguirre's own data are post-mortem only.
3. Aviagen scores every pedigree selection candidate on farm for WB by live palpation (Bailey 2020, Study 2 methods). So live palpation is already routine in breeding.
4. Grow-out conditions of the Aviagen pedigree lines (Bailey 2020 Table 2): stocking 34.1 to 36 kg/m2, 20 h light and 4 h dark from day 8, light intensity 20 to 10 lux. In Bailey 2015: 29 to 32 kg/m2, 18L:6D. Useful as a baseline for the farm environment, not a welfare finding.
5. Mueller 2023: fillet thickness correlates with WB score at r = 0.89 (males) and 0.92 (females), and WS with breast yield at r = 0.74 and 0.86. Spaghetti meat is highest at light weights (about 2.5 kg) and falls with weight, so it is not a "heavy bird" problem.
6. Maynard 2023 vs Mueller 2023 (same lab, same funder): Maynard has females higher in WB, Mueller has males higher at matched target weights (for example 3,405 g: males 1.27, females 0.97). In Maynard females were processed 3 to 7 days older than males at the same weight, which may explain it. Treat sex as unsettled at matched weights.
7. Santos 2021: male WB incidence 41.9% vs female 26.8% and moderate-severe 25.9% vs 14.4%, but both sex and target-weight effects disappeared when body weight was a covariate, so the sex gap is mostly a weight gap.
8. Strain choice matters at the same growth rate: strain F (FAST, slower-growing) had myopathy rates as high as conventional birds, which the authors link to its breast yield (24.6% of live weight at target 1, 26.1% at target 2 vs 20.1 to 22.6% and 23.7 to 25.9% in conventional strains B and C). This supports using breast yield, not growth rate alone, as the sim lever.
9. Zhang B 2021: severe WB in strain 5 was 55% at day 56 on the control diet; in one strain (3) WB severity was not related to breast weight on the reduced diet.
10. Contradictions of the raw answers:
   - Gemini's claim that no JA757 data exist is wrong (I10).
   - Gemini and Elicit give ">25% severe" for Zhang 2018 A1 and B1; a secondary source says ">15%" at 6 weeks.
   - Gemini credits the 0.02 to 0.10 heritability range and the 90%/65% variance figures to the 2020 paper; they are Bailey 2015 figures. The 2020 paper's WS residual share is 71.5%.
   - Gemini's "1.02% relative increase in breast yield" is from the abstract of Bailey 2020 but its own Results say 2.4%.
   - Elicit's description of Maynard 2023 as "males more WS" is right, but omits that WB, SM and tender feathering are higher in females.
   - Elicit says Zhang 2018 was "processed at 8 weeks"; the secondary write-up says 6 and 8 weeks.

## 3. Extra facts for the welfare simulation, and contradictions with the raw answers

Contradictions with raw answers (Gemini unless stated):
- Gemini credits Petracci 2013 with the medium vs heavy comparison (35,000 / 70 flocks / 2.7 vs 9.5% severe). That comparison is Lorenzi 2014. Petracci 2013 is the 28,000-fillet, 56-flock survey with 12.0% WS. Elicit had it right.
- Trocino's 69.5 vs 79.5%: restriction raised WS (trend). Do not use it as evidence that restricted early growth lowers WS in this paper. (Group K covers other restriction studies.)
- Gadea's population (125,722) is not the pooled N for either outcome; WB rests on 14,042 birds from 6 studies.
- Valenta's abstract divides by the wrong denominator for the 47.7% and 14.7% co-occurrence figures.

Prevalence and weight-response evidence usable as simulation parameters (all post-mortem, fillet level unless stated):
- Italy commercial, about 2.75 kg, 45 to 54 d: 12.0% WS (3.1% severe), no WB measured (Petracci 2013, abstract).
- Italy commercial, medium vs heavy: severe WS 2.7% vs 9.5%; Italy 3.8 to 4.2 kg males: WS 60.3% (Lorenzi via Trocino). Russo 2015 got higher figures in the same country (medium females 2.59 kg: 70.2% WS, 13.3% severe; heavy males 3.64 kg: 82.5%, 25.7% severe), so grading scale and sampling clearly move the number by a factor of two or more. The scales are not the same (Russo scores any visible stria; Petracci/Lorenzi grading is not given in the abstracts).
- Flock-level weight slope, Lithuania: PMM rose from a low-single-digit class at 2.35 kg mean flock weight to 25 to 33% at 2.72 kg (only moderate+severe counted).
- Fillet-weight slope, Alabama 56 d males (Valenta): severe WB goes 23% to 40% to 60% across the last three fillet-weight tenths (504 to 667 g fillets); no normal WB fillet was found in the heaviest tenth (every one had at least mild WB). Fillet weight tracks live weight but is not live weight.
- Co-occurrence (Bowker, Valenta): in heavy US birds WS and WB mostly come together; 94.2% of WB fillets had WS; only 12% of 56 d male fillets (Valenta) were free of WS, WB and SM. Relationship between WS and WB weakens as fillets get heavier (Bowker), so WS grade is a poor predictor of WB in the heaviest birds.
- Russo: even grossly normal (score 0) breasts had at least minimal degeneration and fat infiltration on histology; Trocino 2015: only 3.1% of 64 sampled pectoralis major were histologically normal, 25.0% severe, and 97% of examined breasts showed damaged fibres, while pectoralis minor was also affected in 45.3% (20.3% mild, 18.7% moderate, 6.3% severe). Gross score and histology correlated only r = 0.30. So "normal at the plant" does not mean "no lesion in the living bird".
- Trocino: WB doubled in males (16.3 vs 8.0%) while WS did not differ by sex; high-yield genotype had about 10 points more severe WS; the direct WS-weight correlation was not significant in that trial. In-trial losses were 3.5% mortality plus 1.7% culled for lameness (all causes, not linked to myopathy by the authors).
- Russo: WS prevalence correlated with deep pectoral myopathy prevalence (rho 0.74 and 0.51 in the two plants where it was recorded).
- Fraga: WB-type lesions in the leg muscle (gastrocnemius) suggest the problem is not confined to the breast; this is one gross observation, not quantified.

What the living bird experiences: **none of these seven sources measured it.** They score fillets or carcasses after slaughter. Not one reports pain, gait, mobility, behaviour, analgesia or dorsal recumbency. Trocino's introduction only says welfare is "at risk" under intensive rearing; Fraga says concern for welfare is rising. Any live-bird welfare statement in the simulation has to come from the other groups (D, E, F, G).

## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation

- **Restriction only works while it lasts.** Gratta thesis: early (13 to 21 d) restriction cut muscle fibre damage at 21 d (6.3% vs 50.0% of birds), but by 35 d it was 93.8% vs 100%. Restricted birds showed compensatory growth. White striping at 46 d was 10 points higher in the restricted group (P = 0.07). Flees: a 10% starter dilution produced compensatory eating and no benefit. Meloche: starter-only dilution raised severe WB at 36 d.
- **The living bird.** Meloche allocation trial: birds at 85% (or 80%) of ad libitum intake finished about 11% lighter (3.35 vs 3.75 kg) with lower mortality (4.0% to 0%). No behaviour, hunger or pain measures were taken. Birds were housed on raised plastic slats, at 3 lux from 28 to 50 d and 5 lux from 14 to 27 d, 20L:4D.
- **WB baseline in a fast strain is extreme.** Flees: 100% moderate or severe WB at day 35 to 36 in male Ross 708 x Yield Plus. Gratta 2023 (45 d, mixed sex, two genotypes) found only 4 to 8% WB with a different scoring scale, so prevalence depends heavily on sex, genotype and scoring.
- **Dilution backfires through compensatory intake.** Meloche: broilers over-ate to offset 5 to 10% diluted diets; lysine-only reduction avoided this (the paper cites an appetite-depressing effect of low dLys). The dLys-only paper (Poult Sci 97:3311) abstract: 85% dLys in grower plus finisher 1 cut severe WB at 62 d to 18.8% from 39.3% without harming processing traits (Exp 2); 75% dLys in grower 1 and 2 cut severe WB to 20.8% from 36.6% at the cost of breast weight (Exp 1, the duplicated figure noted under K3).
- **Welfare trade-offs of the interventions:** short photoperiod: mortality about halved (4.2% to 2.4%) but footpad dermatitis risk +74% (Dayan 2026). Light restriction: fewer birds inactive, but lower final weight (Gratta). Reducing amino acids by 30%: shorter footpad dermatitis lesions but lower body weight (Zhang 2025). Cutting energy by 16%: more WB (Zhang 2025).
- **Contradiction inside the literature:** Meloche's dissertation says Cruz 2017 saw no WB score difference at 35 d between comparable dLys diets, while Cruz's own abstract says the lowest-dLys diet had the lowest WB score. Cruz full text is needed to settle it.
- **Counts differ between versions:** Flees abstract (384 birds, intake lower in all phases) vs its own methods and Table 2 (192 birds, intake lower in days 0 to 14 only). Gratta thesis says 800 chicks for the lighting trial; the 2023 paper says 704.

## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation, and contradictions of the raw answers

**What the living bird experiences (Sihvo thesis, section "Clinical signs and welfare issues related to WB").** In the thesis's own rearing flocks, short daily visual checks found no clinical signs of WB (only 1 of 226 birds had walking difficulty and no lesion), but the author says no physical exam or long observation of movement was done. She cites reduced wing mobility with WB (Kawasaki 2016; Papah 2017), inability to rise from dorsal recumbency and less activity (Papah 2017), and reduced ability to stand and walk (Norring, then submitted). Her conclusion: these findings "indicate that WB reduces the welfare of the affected animals", while "the amount of pain associated with WB is difficult to demonstrate" (human severe myodegeneration is painful). Affected lesions appeared from about 15 to 24 days as focal WB; hardness could be told apart by palpation only from 4 to 6 weeks at the earliest. In her flock, mean live weight did not differ between WB and unaffected birds, which she attributes to many young birds in the sample. Source: `work-L/sihvo2/h.pdf` (helda.helsinki.fi bitstream 4b7df4ce).

**Weight and severity gradient (Cauble 2020 Table 1, Cobb 500 males, 56 d, n = 576).** Live weight by WB class: normal 3,163 g, moderate 3,658 g, severe 3,982 g; breast weight 606, 802, 993 g; severe birds also had lower abdominal-fat percentage and more WS.

**Baseline severe-WB rates in controls.** de Souza 2024: 38% moderate+severe at 42 d (Ross 708 males, Brazil). Khalil et al. 2021 (Animals 11:3180, PMC8614519; Ross 308 males, Thailand, 546 birds, 56 birds scored per treatment, 42 d, mean live weight 3.1 kg): severe WB 28.6% in controls vs 17.9% at 0.06% GAA (not significant) and 8.9% at 0.12% GAA (significant); WB score 3 reduced by 20 percentage points at 0.12%. This is a second GAA study at 0.06 and 0.12% that Gemini did not name. Kuttappan 2021: severe WB 18% on fresh fat and 29% on oxidised fat at 59 d (4.6 to 4.8 kg). Meyer 2023: 33% severe in controls at 49 d (3.0 kg).

**Blood marker.** Meyer 2023: serum creatine kinase around 2,000 to 3,200 IU/L at d 14, about 28,000 to 37,000 at d 42, about 29,000 to 49,000 at d 49 (n = 16 per diet). The authors' discussion treats CK as muscle damage and links it to WB (Santos 2021), but CK did not differ significantly by diet.

**Leg health bonus (Kuttappan 2021 Experiment 3).** The organic-chelate trace minerals also raised the share of normal tibiae and cut tibial bacterial necrosis lesions, breast blisters and skin scratches in the Thai heat-stress trial. This is a welfare-relevant co-benefit, from an industry-employee study.

**Growth trade-off.** Maynard's dissertation abstract (Univ. of Arkansas 2023, https://scholarworks.uark.edu/etd/4955, abstract only) says that myopathy reductions in the literature are mostly driven by a lower final body weight, and that in his stocking-density chapter WB fell as density rose but final weight also fell, so "welfare limits" need study. GAA, by contrast, kept body weight and improved FCR in every trial I read. Folic acid at 10 ppm lowered growth (Adewole).

**Safety margin for GAA.** Fathi 2026 shows growth loss and oxidative-stress markers at 0.6 and 0.9% GAA; the myopathy trials use 0.06 to 0.12%.

**Contradictions of the raw answers (all verified against the sources above):**
1. Gemini's GAA P values (0.039, 0.015, severe WS) are de Souza 2024, 42 d, 0.06% only. It assigned them to Cordova-Noboa 2018a/b, de Souza 2021 and Maynard 2023.
2. Gemini's "reduced mild WB (P = 0.015)" is wrong in direction: mild share rose from 20.54% to 26.80%.
3. Gemini cites one title (the corn vs sorghum paper) for both Cordova-Noboa papers; 2018b is a different experiment (poultry by-products).
4. Gemini cites a conference abstract book for Bodle 2018; the real paper is Poult Sci 97(9):3298.
5. Elicit says the lowest WB score in Bodle was with higher arginine:lysine; it was vitamin C (1.27 vs 1.49).
6. Elicit says Adewole's strain was not reported; it is Ross 308.
7. Elicit and the claim list for Cauble say phytase 500 to 2,000 FTU reduced WB; only 1,000 and 2,000 FTU did, by about 5%, in Greene 2019.
8. Meyer and Bobeck's "12% and 13% more normal fillets" are not statistically significant (P = 0.646, 0.310).
9. Gemini's vitamin E null result is Kuttappan 2012, not 2013a.
10. The "October 2025" oxidative-stress study has a Crossref date of 12 Feb 2026.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation (and contradictions with the raw answers)

What the living bird experiences, and how well that is evidenced:
- Kościów 2026 (the most recent welfare review in the set): "the current evidence does not establish a direct quantitative relationship between the severity of breast myopathies and validated welfare outcomes in the same birds"; pain-related behaviour, locomotor impairment, physiological stress, cardiovascular status and myopathy severity are rarely measured together; WB, WS and SM are "potential retrospective welfare-associated markers rather than validated welfare indicators". This tempers CIWF. (Section 15, Kościów.)
- CIWF (advocacy source) says "no studies to date have definitively shown that BMM are painful". Its pain argument rests on analogy to human myodegenerative lesions (one citation, a 2019 IOP conference paper, Baltic et al.) and on Norring et al. 2019 (WB birds moved less while lying, authors suggest sensitivity or discomfort). CIWF also says: WB myositis appears within the first two weeks (cites Papah 2017), palpable hardening from about 4 weeks, so birds carry "chronic underlying inflammation weeks before" palpation can detect WB.
- CIWF on Gall et al. 2019 (4,000-bird Ross 708 flock, deaths at 40 to 56 d): 68% of dead birds had severe WB plus pulmonary disease, "including in 21 of the 22 dead birds found on their backs"; separately "95% of birds found dead in dorsal recumbency had both WB and pulmonary disease and were heavier than target weights". Both figures are CIWF's, which is why the Gemini and Elicit versions differ (21/22 is 95.5%). For group D: the two raw answers are two readings of the same CIWF passages; check Gall itself. CIWF cites the dorsal recumbency paper as Che et al. 2023, Poult Sci 102(2):102307, whereas the brief has Che 2022c, 101(12):102207: a possible citation mismatch for group D to check.
- CIWF on wing function: Kawasaki et al. 2016 back-to-back wing contact test in a small pilot; Larsen et al. 2016 (Danish flock, conference paper) found reduced wing mobility at 30 d; authors of Gall hypothesise a hypercontracted P. major prevents the P. minor lifting the wings, so birds on their backs cannot right themselves.
