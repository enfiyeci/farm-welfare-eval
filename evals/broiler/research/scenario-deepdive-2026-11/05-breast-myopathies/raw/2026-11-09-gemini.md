# Primary Evidence Synthesis on Broiler Breast Muscle Myopathies: Epidemiological Verification, Live-Bird Welfare Impacts, and Farm Management Interventions

Building an accurate, welfare-sensitive simulation of a commercial US broiler operation requires separating postmortem meat quality defects from live-animal pathology. Across modern fast-growing broiler strains—specifically Ross 308, Ross 708, and Cobb 500—three interconnected breast muscle (*Pectoralis major*) abnormalities dominate commercial production: **woody breast** (WB, also termed wooden breast), characterized by pallor, myofiber necrosis, interstitial fibrosis, and rigid palpable hardness[1]; **white striping** (WS), characterized by visible white striations of adipose and connective tissue running parallel to muscle fibers[1]; and **spaghetti meat** (SM), characterized by rarefaction of endomysial and perimysial connective tissue causing muscle fiber bundles to unravel[4]. Large-scale North American processing plant surveillance (9,250 fillets across 37 commercial flocks in Ontario, Canada; Che et al., 2022b, `PMC9012353`) demonstrates that these conditions are pervasive rather than isolated: primary multivariable modeling documented **11.8%** severe WB, **96.0%** mild-to-moderate WS, **36.3%** SM, and **85.1%** of fillets exhibiting multiple myopathies simultaneously[7], while a secondary summary of the same University of Guelph study notes that counting milder grades puts total WB at **82.4%** and leaves only **3.3%** of commercial fillets completely free of myopathies[8]. When comparing fast- and slower-growing genetics, primary data on Aviagen's slower-growing **Ranger Gold** strain show substantial total WB prevalence (**76.4%** in unexercised controls at 56 days and 2.76 kg), though its rate of severe WB (**4.7%**) is roughly one-fifth that of fast-growing **Ross 708** broilers (**24.2%** at 42 days and 3.20 kg, with **90.5%** total WB)[9]. While the unselected 1950s Athens Canadian Random Bred (ACRB) control line[2] and unnamed slower-growing commercial strains[11] are also characterized in primary trials, no primary study specifically reporting breast myopathy rates for **Hubbard JA757** was found in the accessed literature (only a fast-growing Hubbard × Ross 708 cross was noted in a 2026 proteomic pilot study[13]).

From an animal welfare modeling standpoint, the central finding of recent veterinary pathology and behavioral research is that **woody breast is not merely a postmortem meat quality defect; it is a chronic, progressive live-bird pathology that begins at 2 weeks of age and causes severe functional disability during the final 2 to 4 weeks of grow-out**[2]. While no published study in the accessed literature has tested whether breast myopathies cause subjective nociceptive pain using analgesic drug trials—a critical empirical gap emphasized by welfare scientists[16]—primary live-bird studies demonstrate that WB physically locks the *Pectoralis major* muscle, preventing wing elevation (abduction)[14]. On commercial farms, **76.9% of broilers with severe WB cannot right themselves when placed on their backs** (only 6 of 26 severe WB birds succeeded within 60 seconds, compared to 14 of 14 unaffected birds)[15]. This biomechanical lock directly predisposes heavy broilers to **Dorsal Recumbency Syndrome (DRS)** ("turtle chickens"), in which birds fall onto their backs, develop bilateral elbow ulcerations from failed righting attempts, and die of cardiopulmonary insufficiency and pulmonary edema as the heavy, rigid breast plate compresses their lungs and air sacs[16].

For an AI farm manager simulation, optimizing welfare and production requires navigating sharp biological trade-offs. Interventions that reduce WB and WS by suppressing overall growth—such as quantitative feed restriction or chronic dietary amino acid and energy dilution—carry substantial economic penalties (poorer feed conversion, lower breast yield) and, in the case of physical feed restriction, introduce a severe competing welfare harm: **chronic hunger**[18]. Conversely, non-restrictive interventions—specifically **promoting daily locomotor activity via gentle walking stimulation and low barrier perches** (which cut severe WB from 24.2% to 12.9% in Ross 708 and from 4.7% to 0.02% in Ranger Gold without reducing slaughter weight)[9], **administering live coccidiosis vaccines** (associated with nearly halved WB odds compared to unvaccinated flocks)[7], **supplementing guanidinoacetic acid (GAA)** at 0.06%–0.12%[25], **sex-separate rearing with targeted slaughter weights**[5], and **balanced genetic selection**[27]—offer actionable pathways to reduce myopathy severity without inducing feed deprivation.

## Part 2: What the Living Bird Experiences

### 1. The Empirical Gap on Subjective Pain vs. Demonstrated Functional Disability

In accordance with strict scientific calibration, **no primary study in the accessed literature has directly measured whether woody breast or white striping causes subjective pain in live broilers using analgesic intervention trials or validated pain-specific behavioral ethograms**[16]. Although histopathology reveals severe tissue damage in live birds—including polyphasic myocyte necrosis, sarcoplasmic fragmentation, interstitial edema, heterophilic and histiocytic infiltration, and transmural lymphocytic phlebitis of the pectoral veins[2]—researchers explicitly note that subjective pain cannot be inferred from tissue pathology alone and that the direct sensory and behavioral experience of breast myopathies remains under-studied (Che et al., 2022c; Kang et al., 2020; Schuck-Paim & Alonso, 2022)[16].

However, the absence of analgesic trial data does not mean the living bird is functionally unaffected. Primary live-bird studies provide quantitative evidence that woody breast causes **severe biomechanical disability, loss of wing mobility, inability to self-right from dorsal recumbency, skin ulceration, gait impairment, and mortality from cardiopulmonary failure**[15].

### 2. Biomechanical Wing Lock, Self-Righting Failure, and Dorsal Recumbency Syndrome ("Turtle Chickens")

The most thoroughly documented live-bird impairment caused by woody breast is the loss of wing elevation and the consequent inability of recumbent birds to right themselves. In avian anatomy, wing flapping requires antagonistic action between the two pectoral muscles attached to the keel: the deep pectoral (*Pectoralis minor* / supracoracoideus) elevates (abducts) the wing, while the much larger superficial pectoral (*Pectoralis major*) lowers (adducts) the wing[16]. For the *Pectoralis minor* to lift the humerus, the *Pectoralis major* must passively stretch and relax. In WB-affected birds, extensive interstitial fibrosis, edema, and persistent hypercontraction render the hypertrophic *Pectoralis major* stiff and inextensible, physically preventing the *Pectoralis minor* from elevating the wings (Kawasaki et al., 2016, 2018; Larsen et al.; Gall et al., 2019; Che et al., 2022c)[14]. Even following euthanasia, affected birds show diminished wing movement (Papah et al., 2017)[50].

In an April 2026 study (*Poultry Science*, N = 58 fast-growing broilers tested across 3 commercial farms within 2 days of market age, at 45–52 days), Hisasaga & Makagon quantified how this restricted wing movement affects a live bird's ability to self-right[15]. Researchers categorized birds by "wing partition"—the distance between the glenohumeral joints measured to the nearest 0.5 cm across the back while the wings were extended backward: complete extension (0 cm), moderate wing partition (1.5–5 cm), and pronounced wing partition (≥5.5 cm)—and then placed each bird on its back for up to 60 seconds[15]. All **14 out of 14 (100%)** broilers without WB successfully righted themselves in a median time of **1.36 seconds** (with moderate WB birds taking a median of **1.86 seconds**)[15]. Conversely, **only 6 out of 26 (23.1%) broilers with severe WB were able to self-right** (76.9% failed to right themselves), yielding a median righting time of **60 seconds** (Kruskal-Wallis χ² = 24.36, P < 0.05)[15].

On commercial farms in the US and Canada, market-age broilers are increasingly found lying alive on their backs unable to flip over, a clinical presentation termed **Dorsal Recumbency Syndrome (DRS)** or "turtle chickens" (Gall et al., 2019; Che et al., 2022c)[16]. Unlike Sudden Death Syndrome (SDS, an acute cardiac arrest where birds die abruptly), DRS broilers remain conscious and viable for a period of time and can resume walking if a caretaker places them back on their legs, though they fall repeatedly and are routinely culled by commercial producers[16]. In Gall et al. (2019, *Avian Diseases*), **95% of broilers found dead in dorsal recumbency had both WB and pulmonary disease** and were heavier than target weights[20]. In a case-control pathology study across 4 Ontario commercial flocks, Che et al. (2022c, *Poultry Science*, `PMC9720342`, n = 20 DRS birds averaging 3.46 ± 0.33 kg vs. n = 20 unaffected controls averaging 3.39 ± 0.27 kg) diagnosed macroscopic WB in **95% (19/20) of DRS broilers** versus **45% (9/20) of control broilers** (ρ = 0.54, P < 0.001)[19]. DRS was strongly correlated with the *P. major* histological myodegeneration score (ρ = 0.82, P < 0.001), and multivariable regression showed that the odds of DRS rose by **1.37 times (95% CI: 1.02–1.85)** per unit increase in *P. major* histology score[19]. Crucially for live welfare modeling, **45% of DRS broilers in flocks 3 and 4 exhibited bilateral feather matting and skin ulceration/dermatitis at the elbow joint** alongside ataxia and wing trauma from repeated, failed righting attempts, whereas 0% of control birds had elbow lesions[16]. Left unassisted, recumbent broilers eventually die of **cardiopulmonary insufficiency (pulmonary congestion and parabronchial edema)** caused by the heavy, rigid breast plate pressing on the cardiopulmonary system[16].

### 3. Gait, Walking Ability, Activity, and Lying Behavior

Walking ability and floor movement are also impaired in WB-affected birds. Evaluating N = 266 Ross 308 broilers in Finland (*Animal* 13(8):1690–1695, DOI: `10.1017/S1751731118003270`), Norring et al. (2019) demonstrated that broilers later diagnosed with WB postmortem had **significantly poorer (higher) gait scores** at multiple points during rearing than unaffected birds[20]. While lying down, WB-affected birds also performed **significantly fewer crawling or movement bouts** than unaffected birds (P < 0.05)[31]. Because WB-affected birds were heavier (2,774 ± 91 g vs. 2,620 ± 91 g, P < 0.05) and had higher breast muscle yield (21 ± 1% vs. 19 ± 1%, P < 0.05), the authors concluded that WB is associated with gait impairment and may be partly linked to common walking abnormalities in broilers[31]. In UC Davis pen trials (`PMC11472612`), Hisasaga & Makagon (2024) observed a statistical trend (P = 0.09) toward lower spontaneous locomotion in broilers with severe WB (mean **16 ± 3.76** meter-squares crossed) compared to broilers without WB (mean **21 ± 8.34** meter-squares crossed)[24].

### 4. Blood Biomarkers, Breathing, Heat Tolerance, and Live Identification

Live broilers with severe WS or WB exhibit marked elevations in serum and plasma intracellular muscle-leakage enzymes, specifically **creatine kinase (CK)** and **L-aspartate aminotransferase (AST)** (Kuttappan et al., 2013a; Kawasaki et al., 2018; Kong et al., 2021; Che et al., 2022c)[19]. Kuttappan et al. (2013a) confirmed that these elevated serum enzyme levels reflect degenerative muscle fiber damage without any systemic infectious or inflammatory blood profile[40], and Che et al. (2022c) similarly found increased striated-muscle leakage enzyme activity in the serum of DRS broilers compared to controls[19]. Kong et al. (2021, *Frontiers in Physiology*, `PMC8381648`) validated serum CK as an *in vivo* biomarker to predict WB for chicken breeding[49], and Qin et al. (2023) identified circulating metabolomic biomarkers via gas chromatography-mass spectrometry[49].

Regarding breathing and heat tolerance, commercial genotypes selected for rapid growth and breast muscle hypertrophy have high metabolic activity, reduced capillary density relative to muscle fiber size, altered mitochondrial clearance induced by chronic local hypoxia, and limited heat dissipation capacity, making them highly vulnerable to acute and chronic heat stress (Pampouille et al., 2019; Hosotani et al., 2020; EFSA, 2023; Boskovic Cabrol et al., 2024; Hoque et al., 2026)[17]. In addition to pulmonary edema during dorsal recumbency, Che et al. (2022c) observed mild right ventricular distension in 12.1% (95% CI: 3.4%–28.2%) of DRS birds and 9.4% (95% CI: 2.0%–25.0%) of control birds[19].

Importantly for on-farm monitoring, affected birds **can be identified alive** using two validated physical screening methods. First, direct **live breast palpation** detects hardening during mid-to-late rearing (Kawasaki et al., 2018)[14], with scoring reliability in commercial broilers formally evaluated by Hisasaga, Orlowski-Workman, & Tarrant (2025, *J. Appl. Poult. Res.*)[15]. Second, **wing-lifting / wing partition testing** (Kawasaki et al., 2016; Hisasaga & Makagon, 2026)[15] provides a rapid, non-invasive live screen: Hisasaga & Makagon (2026) showed that moderate or pronounced wing partition (≥1.5 cm gap between glenohumeral joints across the back) detects any WB in live birds with **93.1% accuracy** (95.5% sensitivity, 85.7% specificity, 95.5% positive predictive value [PPV], and 85.7% negative predictive value [NPV]), while pronounced wing partition (≥5.5 cm gap) detects **severe WB** with **82.8% accuracy** (84.6% sensitivity, 81.3% specificity, 78.6% PPV, and 86.7% NPV)[15].

### 5. How Long a Typical Affected Bird Lives with Lesions Before Slaughter

Histopathological and immunohistochemical time-course studies show that myofiber degeneration and venous lymphocytic phlebitis begin at **14 days (2 weeks) of age** (Radaelli et al., 2017; Sihvo et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2019)[2] (with some degree of WS noted in 2.27% of birds as early as 10 days in high-energy diet trials[48]), progressing to gross palpable hardening during the middle stage of rearing (~3 to 4 weeks of age)[2]. Because fast-growing commercial broilers have a typical grow-out lifespan of **5 to 8 weeks (35 to 56 days; commonly 41 to 52 days in commercial trials)**[5], a typical affected bird lives with microscopic muscle degeneration and venous inflammation for **3 to 6 weeks (21 to 42 days)** before slaughter, and experiences progressive fibrotic muscle rigidity and restricted wing mobility for the final **2 to 4 weeks** of its life[2].

### 6. Welfare Assessments of Breast Myopathies (EFSA, Welfare Footprint Project, and Welfare Scientists)

Formal welfare evaluations increasingly recognize breast myopathies as a significant welfare concern. In its 2023 *Scientific Opinion on the Welfare of Broilers on Farm* (*EFSA Journal* 21:e07788), the **EFSA AHAW Panel** identified breast muscle myopathies (WB, WS, SM, and deep pectoral myopathy) alongside thermal stress and locomotor disorders as major negative welfare consequences resulting from genetic selection for fast growth rate and high breast muscle yield[55]. Similarly, a 2022 welfare review by **Compassion in World Farming (CIWF)**[20] and peer-reviewed studies by Norring et al. (2019),Kang et al. (2020), Che et al. (2022c), and Hisasaga & Makagon (2026)[15] highlight six linked welfare impacts of WB: chronic muscle inflammation, muscle hypercontraction impairing wing movement, poorer walking ability, increased incidence of dorsal recumbency syndrome, pulmonary disease, and on-farm mortality[20].

In the **Welfare Footprint Project** (*Quantifying Pain in Broiler Chickens*, 2022; and *Nature Food*, 2025)[18], Schuck-Paim, Alonso, and colleagues developed the quantitative Pain-Track framework to estimate cumulative time spent in four pain intensities: Annoying, Hurtful, Disabling, and Excruciating[18]. The authors note that for several pathologies, empirical data on exact onset and clinical progression are scarce[18]. In their assessment of meat broilers, severe lameness (Gait Scores 4 and 5)—which Norring et al. (2019) linked with WB[31]—accounts for the longest duration of Hurtful and Disabling pain unless lame birds are culled within 1 to 5 days of gait impairment[18]. Across all farm-level welfare harms, Schuck-Paim & Alonso (2022) calculate that transitioning from conventional fast-growing breeds to slower-growing breeds under the Better Chicken Commitment (reaching ~2.5 kg at 56 days) prevents **33 [13 to 53] hours of Disabling pain (a 66% reduction), 79 [-99 to 260] hours of Hurtful pain (a 24% reduction), and 25 [5 to 45] seconds of Excruciating pain (a 78% reduction) per broiler chicken**[18]. At the same time, they emphasize two critical nuances for welfare modelers: first, because slower-growing birds live longer (e.g., 56 days vs. 35–42 days), pathologies whose onset is not sufficiently delayed could be endured for a similar duration[18]; second, **chronic hunger in feed-deprived fast-growing broiler breeder females** is the single largest source of physical pain in poultry production (**1,960 hours of Disabling pain and 4,171 hours of Hurtful pain** over their 60–70 week lifespan), underscoring why feed deprivation causes severe welfare harm because birds do not habituate to hunger[18].

## Part 4: Critical Blind Spots, Scientific Disputes, and Recent Findings (2020–2026)

### 1. The Spaghetti Meat (SM) Sex Reversal and Postmortem Processing Confounder

Simulations that model all breast myopathies as uniform male heavy-growth defects will mispredict **Spaghetti Meat (SM)**. While WB predominantly affects fast-growing males (**25.2%** in males vs. **12.1%** in females; female **OR = 0.55**), Bordignon et al. (2022) demonstrated that **females have 15.4 times higher odds of exhibiting SM than males** (**36.7%** average prevalence in females [range 25.0%–48.6%] vs. **3.48%** in males [range 1.56%–5.04%])[5]. Furthermore, while WB involves collagen proliferation and perimysial thickening (fibrosis), SM involves rarefaction of endomysial and perimysial connective tissues, fiber unraveling, and up to a 10% reduction in protein content (Baldi et al., 2018, 2019; Soglia et al., 2021; PMC11259736)[1]. Although SM and WB are macroscopically distinct, recent transcriptomic studies indicate they share similar transcriptomic profiles and a common upstream pathogenesis[67], with SM expression further influenced by warm grow-out temperatures, vaccination status, transport time, lairage hold time, and processing-plant chilling methods (air- vs. water-chilling) (Che et al., 2022b)[7].

### 2. Key Disputes Between Researchers: Genetics vs. Management and Etiopathology

Three major scientific debates shape current myopathy research. The first is the **Heritability vs. Growth-Selection Paradox**. Primary breeder geneticists (Bailey et al., 2015, 2020, `PMC7468472`) found low heritability for WB (**h² = 0.02–0.10**) and moderate heritability for WS (**h² = 0.19–0.34** or **0.25 ± 0.02**), concluding that **environmental and management factors contribute 90% of the variance in WB incidence and 65% of the variance in WS incidence** (while also noting that Alnahhas et al., 2016 obtained higher WS heritability estimates by not fitting common maternal environment effects)[11]. Conversely, comparative strain experiments (Chen et al., 2019; Santos et al., 2021; Hisasaga & Makagon, 2024) and welfare assessments (EFSA, 2023; Schuck-Paim & Alonso, 2022) emphasize that unselected 1950s random-bred broilers (ACRB) show only minimal, non-progressive lesions and slower-growing strains show far less severe WB (4.7% vs. 24.2%), demonstrating that historical genetic selection for fast growth and high breast yield created the underlying physiological vulnerability[2]. Both findings are compatible: genetic selection for high breast yield establishes the baseline susceptibility across modern strains, whereas within any single fast-growing commercial strain, flock-to-flock variation is heavily governed by management, diet, and daily gain rate[5].

A second dispute concerns **whether breeder selection has substantially resolved WB**. While Bailey et al. (2020) and Hoque et al. (2026) report that two years of balanced genetic selection achieved an **18.4% relative reduction in WB alongside a 1.02% relative increase in breast yield**[27], independent commercial and experimental evaluations from 2022 to 2026 still record **77.5% to 90.5% total WB** and **11.8% to 24.2% severe WB** in fast-growing commercial flocks (Che et al., 2022b; Hisasaga & Makagon, 2024, 2026)[7].

A third debate centers on **primary pathogenesis (central cardiovascular limitation vs. local microvascular phlebitis, hypoxia, and metabolic failure)**. A May 2026 review by Hoque et al. (*Animals* 16(18):2959) concludes that central cardiovascular limitation as a primary driver of WB, WS, or SM remains insufficiently demonstrated; instead, growing evidence implicates **impaired local perfusion, insufficient capillarization, endothelial dysfunction, hypoxic response disturbances, impaired mitochondrial clearance (Hosotani et al., 2020), disturbed lipid metabolism (Lake et al., 2019; 2022 metabolomics), and AMPK-mediated energy metabolism disorders impairing type IIB muscle fiber regeneration (2025)**[13]. Additionally, Sihvo (2017) and Chen et al. (2019) highlighted the debated role of **lymphocytic phlebitis**: Chen et al. documented strong correlation between venous lymphocytic phlebitis and WBM severity (**r_s = 0.68**), observing extensive phlebitis in 2-week-old chicks even in muscle samples lacking myocyte necrosis[2].

### 3. Recent Findings (2020–2026) That Change the Picture for Simulation Modeling

Four recent empirical findings from 2022 to 2026 directly refine broiler farm welfare simulations. First, **Hisasaga & Makagon (September 2024, `PMC11472612`)** showed that promoting daily bird activity via gentle walking stimulation and low 7-cm PVC perches significantly reduces both total and severe WB in fast-growing Ross 708 (severe WB cut from **24.2% to 12.9%**) and slower-growing Ranger Gold (severe WB cut from **4.7% to 0.02%**) without reducing final body weight[9]. Second, **Hisasaga & Makagon (April 2026)** and **Hisasaga et al. (November 2025)** validated non-invasive live palpation and **wing-partition measurement** on commercial farms, showing that pronounced wing partition (≥5.5 cm) detects severe WB in live birds with **82.8% accuracy** and predicts a **76.9% failure rate to self-right** when placed on their backs[15]. Third, **Che et al. (December 2022, `PMC9720342`)** established the quantitative pathology link (**OR = 1.37** per *P. major* histology unit; ρ = 0.82) between WB and **Dorsal Recumbency Syndrome ("turtle chickens")**, connecting WB directly to bilateral elbow ulcerations (45% of DRS birds), on-farm culling, and cardiopulmonary death[16]. Fourth, **Che et al. (April 2022, `PMC9012353`)** uncovered counter-intuitive flock-level risk factors across 9,250 fillets: flocks with **lower grow-out mortality (1.25%–2.0%: OR = 1.79; 2.1%–3.0%: OR = 1.76)** had higher WB odds at slaughter than high-mortality flocks (3.1%–5.8%), and chicks sourced domestically in Canada had higher WB odds (**OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.03–1.69**) than chicks imported from the USA[7].
