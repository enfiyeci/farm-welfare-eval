# Evidence Base and Welfare Modeling Parameters for Ascites Syndrome in Commercial Broiler Simulations

For an AI farm-management simulation evaluating animal welfare in commercial US broiler operations, **ascites syndrome (pulmonary hypertension syndrome, PHS, or "water belly")** represents a central physiological trade-off between rapid metabolic growth and cardiorespiratory capacity[1]. In modern fast-growing strains—such as **Aviagen Ross 308**, **Ross 708**, and **Cobb-Vantress Cobb 500** (achieving average daily gains of 61–65+ g/day)—intense selection for breast muscle yield and low feed conversion ratio (FCR) has produced birds whose oxygen demand frequently exceeds the anatomical blood-flow capacity of their rigid, fixed lungs and proportionally small hearts[1]. Across the ten claims evaluated in **Part 1**, eight are supported or partly supported by primary peer-reviewed trials and veterinary reference texts, while one (C5, claiming Cobb is universally the most susceptible strain and Ross the least) is contradicted by multi-hybrid trials showing that strain rankings reverse depending on environmental conditions[7], and another (C6, claiming ventilation and vitamin C cannot offset fast growth) overstates the inefficacy of dietary antioxidants, as controlled trials demonstrate significant mortality reductions from supplemental vitamin C (300–500 mg/kg) and coenzyme Q10 (40 mg/kg) under cold stress[10].

From a live-bird welfare perspective (**Part 2**), ascites is fundamentally different from Sudden Death Syndrome (SDS): whereas SDS kills apparently healthy broilers in 37 to 69 seconds[12], ascites is a progressive right-sided congestive heart failure that unfolds over **2 to 4 weeks**[12]. Pre-ascitic hypoxemia (arterial blood oxygen saturation, SpO2 < 80%), elevated venous carbon dioxide (pvCO2), and right ventricular hypertrophy begin as early as days 11 to 14[3], progressing by weeks 4 to 6 into overt peritoneal fluid transudation (up to 300 mL), abdominal air-sac compression, open-beak dyspnea, cyanosis, anorexia, and impaired locomotion lasting **2 to 5 days** before death[3]. The **Welfare Footprint Project (2023)** estimates that each fatal ascites case inflicts roughly **170 to 390 waking hours of Hurtful pain, 81 to 180 hours of Disabling pain, and 18 to 60 minutes of Excruciating terminal breathlessness**[12], though welfare scientists explicitly note a major empirical gap: **no study has directly measured subjective pain or affective distress in live ascitic broilers** via analgesic self-selection or behavioral aversion[12].

In a simulated US commercial grow-out house (**Part 3** and **Part 4**), an AI manager cannot treat clinical ascites once right ventricular failure develops; affected birds must be humanely culled[1]. However, the manager can substantially reduce flock incidence—which currently kills roughly **0.3% to 2.0%** of fast-growing broilers on the farm, causes **0.5% to 0.7%** abattoir condemnations in fast-growing flocks (versus **0.09%** in slower-growing hybrids like Hubbard JA787), and affects **2.5% to 7.5%** subclinically[12]—through targeted environmental and nutritional controls. The highest-leverage, welfare-positive interventions are preventing brooding cold stress during the first 21 days (maintaining 28–30 °C floor temperatures)[1], providing 6+ hours of uninterrupted darkness daily (reducing ascites mortality by 30%–40% without hunger penalties)[12], maintaining single-stage incubation eggshell temperatures at 37.8 °C[21], keeping water sodium below 0.12% and feed sodium below 0.20%[15], and keeping house carbon dioxide below 3,500 ppm[13]. Conversely, aggressive physical feed restriction reduces ascites but introduces severe welfare trade-offs via chronic hunger and feeder competition[6].

## Part 2: What the Living Bird Experiences

To model animal welfare accurately in an AI farm manager simulation, pathology at necropsy must be separated from the physiological, behavioral, and affective states experienced by the living bird. Unlike Sudden Death Syndrome (SDS)—in which well-fleshed broilers experience acute ventricular fibrillation, flap their wings, squawk, and die within **37 to 69 seconds** (or under 53 seconds) without prior illness[12]—ascites syndrome is a chronic, progressive cardiorespiratory collapse that unfolds over **several weeks**[12].

### 1. Timeline of Onset, Progression, and Survival Duration by Age

A live broiler progressing toward clinical ascites passes through four sequential stages with distinct age milestones:

1. **Early Pre-Clinical Stage (Days 7 to 14):** Fast-growing broilers are predisposed to cardiac arrhythmias detectable on electrocardiograms (ECG) as early as **7 days of age**[12]. By **day 11**, susceptible juveniles already exhibit significantly elevated partial pressure of carbon dioxide in venous blood (pvCO2), which reliably predicts clinical ascites at week 5[5]. A separate, small sub-population (**~0.5%** of most commercial flocks) dies of ascites before **14 days of age** due to congenital interventricular or interatrial septal defects[57], or early acute sodium toxicosis / brooder aspergillosis[1].
2. **Pre-Ascitic Pulmonary Hypertension Stage (Days 14 to 28):** Right ventricular hypertrophy and pre-ascitic hypoxemia typically develop around **2 weeks of age**[12]. Experimental studies using hypobaric chambers or cold stress show a consistent **2-week lag time**: broilers develop clinical ascites roughly **14 days after exposure** to hypoxia or chilling, and conversely cease developing new ascites cases **14 days after removal** from the stressor[20].
3. **Clinical Onset and Peak Mortality Window (Days 21 to 49+):** Overt clinical signs do not appear until elevated pulmonary arterial pressure forces right ventricular failure (RVF), tricuspid valve insufficiency, central venous congestion, and plasma transudation from the liver capsule into the hepatoperitoneal spaces[1]. Clinical ascites mortality begins from **3 weeks (day 21) onward**[11], is most commonly diagnosed clinically at **4 to 5 weeks (days 28–35)**[12], and peaks sharply during **weeks 5 and 6 (days 35–42)**[1] (extending through weeks 5–8 in longer grow-outs[60]).
4. **Duration of Living with Clinical Signs Before Death:** The **Welfare Footprint Project (2023)** models the pre-fluid phases of **Hypoxemia** at **1 to 2 weeks** (98–280 waking hours, assuming 4–10 hours of sleep/day) and **Pulmonary Hypertension** at **1 to 3 weeks** (98–420 waking hours)[12]. Once Phase iii (**Fluid Transudation / overt clinical ascites**) begins, primary clinical studies report that chicks live with visibly dilated abdomens, tachypnea, and progressive bradycardia for **"several days"**[58], quantified by the Welfare Footprint Project as **2 to 5 days** (81–180 waking hours, mean **130 hours**) before succumbing to terminal cardiac failure and acute suffocation (**18 to 60 minutes**, mean **39 minutes**)[12]. However, the **Merck Veterinary Manual (2024)** and **Baghbanzadeh & Decuypere (2008)** emphasize that not all broilers dying of pulmonary hypertension syndrome survive long enough to accumulate abdominal fluid; some develop acute pulmonary edema and die suddenly before clinical ascites is observed[1].

### 2. Breathing Effort, Panting, Gasping, and Air-Sac Compression

Live clinically ascitic broilers experience severe, progressive respiratory compromise driven by two converging anatomical mechanisms. First, because avian lungs are rigid and fixed into the dorsal thoracic cavity, their blood capillaries can expand very little when cardiac output surges[1]. As pulmonary arterial pressure rises above its normal ~20 mmHg baseline[3], fluid leaks into the lung interstitium and air capillaries, creating extreme pulmonary congestion and edema[1]. Second, in advanced right ventricular failure, high central venous pressure causes plasma to transude from the congested liver into the ventral hepatoperitoneal and pericardial spaces, pooling **up to 300 mL of clear yellow fluid and fibrin clots** inside the abdomen[1]. Both **Wideman (2000)**, the **Aviagen Ross Tech Note on Ascites**, and the **Welfare Footprint Project (2023)** document that this massive fluid accumulation physically **compresses the abdominal air sacs**, mechanically restricting inhalation and exhalation[3]. In the living bird, this manifests clinically as a markedly **elevated respiratory rate (tachypnea)**, **dyspnea (labored breathing)**, **panting**, and **breathing with the beak held open** even at cool or thermoneutral house temperatures[1].

### 3. Behavioral Changes: Movement, Posture, Feeding, and Drinking

Primary clinical trials and veterinary manuals report a consistent syndrome of behavioral depression and physical impairment in live ascitic birds. Affected broilers exhibit **decreased exercise tolerance**, **loss of general activity**, and **markedly reduced mobility**[1]. The distended, fluid-heavy abdomen produces a **"potbellied appearance"**[58] and causes direct **difficulty in walking**[60]. Sick birds stand or squat with **sagging (drooping) wings**, **ruffled feathers**, and **shuttered (half-closed) or dull eyes**[16]. Concurrently, terminally ascitic broilers develop **anorexia (cessation or sharp reduction of feed intake) and reduced drinking**[3]. Because somatic growth halts as soon as right ventricular failure sets in, clinically ascitic broilers at weeks 5–6 are **significantly smaller and lighter than their healthy penmates** (`p < 0.05` in cold-stressed 5-week broilers[40]), even though high early growth rate predisposed them to the condition[1].

### 4. Live-Bird Blood Oxygen Saturation, Blood Gases, and Biomarkers

Unlike subjective pain, cardiorespiratory derangement in live pre-ascitic and ascitic broilers has been rigorously quantified using pulse oximetry, venous blood gas analyzers, hematology, and electrocardiography. Using non-invasive wing-vein pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas sampling (**Julian & Mirsalimi 1992**, *Avian Diseases* 36:730–732[62]; **Peacock et al. 1990**[63]; **Wideman & Kirby 1995a**[63]; **Wideman 2000**[3]), researchers established that fast-growing broilers have lower baseline hemoglobin oxygen saturation than slow-growing broilers even at sea level because red blood cells transit the pulmonary capillaries too rapidly to pick up a full oxygen load[3]. In live birds entering the pre-ascitic and ascitic stages, **arterial blood oxygen saturation drops below 80%**, which is the quantitative threshold that produces visible cyanosis[3].

Simultaneously, live broilers progressing to ascites exhibit **hypoxemia (low venous pO2), hypercapnia (high venous pCO2), and blood acidosis**[15]. **Scheele et al. (2003, 2005)** demonstrated across weekly venous blood samplings (weeks 2 to 5) that elevated venous pvCO2 at **day 11 of age** accurately predicts 5-week ascites[5]. Furthermore, **van As et al. (2010)** documented sex-divergent acid-base disturbances in juvenile broilers coinciding with ascites, with lower venous blood pH in affected males and higher pH in females[12]. Acidosis further impairs oxygen delivery by reducing hemoglobin-oxygen affinity[2].

In response to tissue hypoxia and elevated plasma corticosterone, the kidneys release erythropoietin, driving polycythemia[2]. In live ascitic broilers, **hematocrit (HCT / PCV)** rises sharply from normal levels of **26%–28%** to **33%–40%+** (`p < 0.0001`[7]). **Luger et al. (2003)** showed that chronic corticosterone elevation in ascitic chickens arrests erythrocyte differentiation, flooding the circulation with **partially immature red blood cells containing less hemoglobin**[2]. Combined with hypoxia- and sodium-induced **erythrocyte rigidity (loss of RBC deformability)**, this polycythemia spikes blood viscosity, further overloading the failing right ventricle[1]. While initial hypoxia triggers compensatory tachycardia, live broilers in clinical right ventricular failure develop **progressive bradycardia (declining heart rate)** and reduced cardiac output prior to death (**Olkowski & Classen 1998**[58]). In addition, **Maxwell et al. (1994, 1995a,b)** demonstrated that live pre-ascitic and ascitic broilers have significantly elevated **serum Troponin-T** concentrations, providing an early circulating biomarker of myocardial cell injury before abdominal fluid accumulates[40].

### 5. On-Farm Mortality vs. Slaughterhouse Condemnation Split

In commercial fast-growing broiler flocks at low altitude, affected birds partition across on-farm mortality, transport mortality, slaughterhouse condemnation, and subclinical survival. Across commercial fast-growing flocks with 3.2%–6.0% total cumulative mortality, ascites accounts for roughly **5% to 40% of on-farm deaths**—representing **0.3% to 2.0% of the total flock** dying on the farm[12]. In a recent multi-farm necropsy study of 22 commercial Ross 308 flocks (`1,504` dead birds > 7 days old necropsied; total flock mortality 3.25%, post-7-day mortality 2.31%), **8.3% to 10.1% of dead birds had gross peritoneal ascites** (~0.20%–0.23% of the live flock), while **34.1% to 34.7% had cardiovascular collapse with cardiac dilatation** (~0.80% of the live flock) and **83.3% to 86.8% had subcutaneous edema**[67]. Many ascitic birds that survive to catching die during crating and transport (**dead-on-arrival, DOA**) due to acute cardiopulmonary decompensation[12].

Affected birds that survive catching and transport are condemned at post-mortem inspection[18]. In Norway's 63.2-million-bird dataset (**Forseth et al. 2023**), **0.661%** of all Ross 308 broilers reaching the slaughterhouse were condemned for ascites (making ascites the #1 condemnation cause in Ross 308, representing **30.6%** of all 2.165% condemned carcasses), compared to **0.093%** in slower-growing Hubbard JA787[18]. Beyond fatal and condemned cases, the **Welfare Footprint Project (2023)** estimates that **2.5% to 7.5%** of fast-growing broilers experience non-fatal pulmonary hypertension (Pain-Track 1) during grow-out without progressing to severe fluid transudation or death before slaughter[12].

### 6. Early Recognition by Farm Staff (and AI Simulation Sensors)

Veterinary references emphasize that **no overt clinical signs appear until right ventricular failure and fluid accumulation begin**[1]. However, once decompensation starts, farm staff (or computer-vision/audio sensors in an AI simulation) can identify affected birds through five checkable cues:

1. **Comb and Wattle Cyanosis:** A dark bluish-purple discoloration of the comb and wattles (reflecting arterial SpO2 < 80%) is the earliest visible sign in an otherwise healthy-looking bird[3].
2. **Abdominal Distension and Reddened Skin:** A swollen, fluid-filled lower abdomen ("water belly") with reddened abdominal skin and engorged peripheral vessels[1]. Palpation reveals a fluctuant, fluid-filled peritoneal cavity.
3. **Thermoneutral Panting / Open-Beak Breathing:** Rapid respiratory rate and open-beak breathing when house temperatures are normal and healthy penmates are breathing quietly with closed beaks[1].
4. **Locomotor Reluctance and Growth Stunting:** Birds sitting hunched with ruffled feathers, sagging wings, and half-shut eyes that waddle reluctantly when approached and fall noticeably behind penmates in body size after day 28[1].
5. **Supine Mortality Posture:** During daily mortality walks, broilers that died of ascites or SDS are classically **found dead on their backs**[1]. The **RSPCA Broiler Welfare Assessment Protocol (2017, 2026)** mandates that any bird found dead on its back be recorded and investigated as a suspected cardiac/ascites death[70].

### 7. Formal Welfare Assessments and the Empirical Gap on Live-Bird Suffering

The **EFSA Panel on Animal Health and Welfare (2010, 2023)** identifies ascites as a major metabolic welfare hazard resulting from genetic selection for fast growth[72]. In its 2023 Scientific Opinion (*Welfare of broilers on farm*, EFSA Journal 21(2):e07788), EFSA concluded that **fast-growing broilers (average daily gain > 50 g/day) should no longer be kept** because severe cardiorespiratory and locomotor welfare consequences are inevitable[74]. Similarly, the **RSPCA (2017, 2020, 2026)** ([70]) and academic welfare reviews (**Bessei 2006**[13]; **Kalmar, Vanrompay, & Janssens 2013**[12]; **Hartcher & Lum 2019**[56]) classify ascites as a severe welfare issue because its gradual onset forces birds to endure prolonged breathlessness, physical abdominal tension, and lethargy ("prolonged agony"[24]) over days to weeks. Under the **RSPCA Broiler Breed Welfare Assessment Protocol (2026)**, any breed exceeding **1.0% ascites mortality** fails welfare certification (with **Hubbard JA757** required as the benchmark control breed)[70].

The **Welfare Footprint Project (2023)** quantifies the cumulative pain burden of ascites using two Pain-Tracks[12]. A bird experiencing non-fatal pulmonary hypertension (**Pain-Track 1**) endures an estimated **84 hours (42–130 h) of Annoying pain, 280 hours (170–390 h) of Hurtful pain, and 84 hours (42–130 h) of Disabling pain**[12]. A bird progressing to fatal ascites (**Pain-Track 2**) endures an additional **130 hours (81–180 h) of Disabling pain** during 2–5 days of peritoneal fluid transudation, culminating in **39 minutes (18–60 min) of Excruciating pain** from acute breathlessness and cardiac failure[12]. Switching from conventional fast-growing breeds to slower-growing breeds (2.5 kg at 56 days) cuts total **Excruciating pain per average broiler by 78% (25 seconds saved per bird; 87%–90% under full Better Chicken Commitment reforms)**, driven primarily by eliminating fatal ascites and sepsis[24].

In strict adherence to primary evidence standards, **no experimental study in the retrieved literature has directly measured pain or affective distress in live ascitic broilers** (unlike lameness, where **Danbury et al. 2000** demonstrated that lame broilers self-select analgesic-medicated feed[4]). The **Welfare Footprint Project (2023)** explicitly describes its Pain-Track intensity assignments as a **"tentative hypothesis"**[12], noting that inferences of breathlessness and abdominal pressure discomfort rely on physiological homology and clinical signs, and stating plainly that **it remains unknown whether reduced feed intake in ascitic broilers stems from a metabolic lack of appetite, physical pain/discomfort, or both**[12].

## Part 4: Critical Context for Simulation Design — Prevalence, Breeder Progress, Scientific Disputes, and 2020–2026 Breakthroughs

### 1. Current Commercial Prevalence, Mortality, and Slaughter Condemnation Data (US vs. Europe)

To parameterize a realistic commercial simulation, it is essential to distinguish between **total flock condemnations**, **ascites-specific abattoir condemnations**, **on-farm ascites mortality**, and **hidden cardiorespiratory pathology**. In the monthly *Poultry Slaughter* reports published by the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS) from Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) plant records through October 2026 (~5.03 to 5.77 billion pounds of young chickens inspected monthly at average live weights of **6.36 to 6.55 lbs [2.88–2.97 kg]**), **ante-mortem condemnations** (birds dead or moribund at the plant) consistently represent **0.19% to 0.24%** of live weight inspected (~11.8–12.2 million lbs/month), and **post-mortem whole-carcass condemnations** represent **0.52% to 0.65%** of quantities inspected (~24.6–28.6 million lbs/month)[101]. While USDA-FSIS inspectors condemn carcasses with ascitic fluid accumulation ([69]), the standard NASS monthly releases (`2021–2026`) aggregate post-mortem condemnations and do not publish a separate monthly ascites line item[101]. Furthermore, the **Welfare Footprint Project (2023)** notes that US integrators treat recent on-farm cause-specific mortality datasets as proprietary[12]. Synthesizing veterinary manuals and available surveys, current US and global fast-growing flocks experience **4% to 6%** cumulative 42-day mortality, of which ascites accounts for **5% to 40%**—yielding **0.3% to 2.0% fatal on-farm ascites** and **2.5% to 7.5% non-fatal pulmonary hypertension**[12].

In Europe, large-scale commercial abattoir and on-farm necropsy datasets provide exact comparisons between fast-growing Ross 308 and slower-growing hybrids. In Norway's national abattoir dataset covering 63.2 million broilers across 4,295 slaughter batches from 139 farms (**Forseth et al. 2023, 2024**), total post-mortem carcass condemnation was **2.165%** (`623,493 / 28,804,890`) in fast-growing **Ross 308** (slaughtered at median **33 days, 1,357 g** carcass weight) versus **0.645%** (`221,818 / 34,404,525`) in slower-growing **Hubbard JA787** (slaughtered at median **46 days, 1,654 g**)[18]. **Ascites was the single largest cause of condemnation in Ross 308 at 0.661%** (`190,537` birds; median batch **0.54%**, IQR **0.34%–0.85%**), which was **7.1 times higher** than the **0.093%** ascites condemnation rate in Hubbard JA787 (`31,865` birds; median batch **0.08%**, IQR **0.05%–0.12%**)[18]. In winter, the negative binomial regression prevalence ratio for ascites in Ross 308 reached **6.54 times that of Hubbard JA787** (`95% CI: 6.13–6.98`)[25]. Variance partition coefficients (VPC) showed that unexplained variation in Ross 308 ascites was split across **slaughter batch (35.1%)**, **flock (37.4%)**, and **farm (27.4%)**[25]. In their companion study (**Forseth et al. 2024**), on-farm **mortality after the first week (MAFW) was more than twice as high in Ross 308 as in Hubbard JA787 (IRR: 2.13, 95% CI: 1.82–2.49)**[107], and integrator Norsk Kylling reported a **76% drop in transport DOAs** and **80% drop in ascites** after switching breeds[68].

Complementing these abattoir figures, a commercial farm necropsy study (`PMC13625790`) examined `33` commercial flocks (`11` flocks each of slower-growing **Rustic Gold**, **Ross 308 at lower density [LD]**, and **Ross 308 at higher density [HD]**; total `2,505` necropsied birds that died after day 7; overall flock mortality `3.19%` in Rustic Gold and `3.25%` in Ross 308-LD)[67]. Gross peritoneal **ascites was present in 10.1% (76/754) of dead Ross 308-LD and 8.3% (62/750) of dead Ross 308-HD birds, compared to 4.7% (47/1,001) of dead Rustic Gold birds** (`p_adj = 0.024`; PR = **2.12 [95% CI: 1.37–3.30]** for Ross 308-LD vs. Rustic Gold)[67]. Crucially, necropsy revealed that gross peritoneal ascites (`7.4%` overall) is only part of the cardiorespiratory mortality burden: **cardiovascular collapse with cardiac dilatation was present in 34.1%–34.7% of dead Ross 308 birds vs. 20.1% of Rustic Gold** (`p_adj = 0.027`; PR = **1.72–1.74**), **subcutaneous edema in 83.3%–86.8% vs. 72.7%** (`p_adj = 0.024`), and **lung edema/congestion in 84.8%–87.7% vs. 75.5%**[67].

### 2. Has Breeder Selection Reduced Ascites Over the Past 20 Years?

There is strong evidence that primary breeder selection has substantially improved genetic robustness per unit of growth, alongside clear evidence that continuous increases in growth rate still leave fast-growing broilers vulnerable. Beginning in the late 1990s and 2000s, primary breeders (**Aviagen** and **Cobb-Vantress**) integrated non-invasive **wing-vein pulse oximetry (SpO2)**, sib-testing, and cardiovascular livability into balanced multi-trait selection indices[1]. In a 2023 peer-reviewed paper authored by Aviagen geneticists (**Neeteson et al. 2023**, *Animals* 13(19):3150[98]), national slaughterhouse data from **1995 to 2022** (Figure 15 in[98]) demonstrate a marked, sustained multi-decade decline in ascites / abdominal edema condemnations per 10,000 broilers, achieved simultaneously with major improvements in FCR, breast yield, and gait scores. Similarly, recent genetic cohorts (representing two additional years of selection progress) demonstrated an **18.4% relative reduction in Wooden Breast myopathy alongside a 1.02% relative increase in breast yield**, confirming that multi-trait selection can simultaneously advance yield and reduce metabolic pathology[93].

However, independent welfare scientists (**Kalmar et al. 2013**; **Zhang, Schmidt, & Lamont 2018**; **Hartcher & Lum 2019**[56]) and commercial abattoir audits (**Baxter et al. 2021**[108]; **Forseth et al. 2023**[18]) emphasize that while selection prevented ascites from exploding as growth rates quadrupled, **cardiovascular disorders and ascites still occur at 2 to 7 times the rate of slower-growing strains** and remain the #1 condemnation cause in commercial Ross 308 flocks[18]. Because modern fast-growing birds operate near their physiological ceiling of pulmonary capillary blood flow, any management lapse—such as winter brooding drafts, high incubator eggshell temperatures (> 38.9 °C), or high-density pelleted rations—rapidly pushes a subset of the flock past the threshold of right ventricular decompensation[20].

### 3. Key Scientific Disputes: Genetics vs. Management and Pathophysiological Triggers

Researchers remain divided across three foundational debates that directly affect how an AI farm simulation should weight genetics versus environment. The first dispute pits **polygenic growth-rate coupling (Julian, Wideman, Moghadam, EFSA) against oligogenic independence (Druyan & Cahaner)**. **Julian (1998, 2000)**, **Wideman (2000, 2013)**, **Moghadam et al. (2001)**, and **EFSA (2010, 2023)** view ascites as an inherent anatomical and quantitative-genetic consequence of selecting for high muscle mass and rapid growth without commensurate lung growth (genetic correlation r_g = +0.22 to +0.35 with BW; SaO2 r_g = -0.33 with BW)[3]. Under this model, any bird growing > 50–60 g/day has a narrow cardiopulmonary safety margin[74]. Conversely, **Druyan, Hadad, & Cahaner (2007a, 2007b, 2008)** argue that there is **little or no direct genetic correlation between potential growth rate and ascites susceptibility**[29]. They showed that under cold/pellet challenge, a slow-growing 1986 broiler line suffered **32%** ascites (comparable to **31%** in a 2002 fast-growing line), that birds remaining healthy within commercial fast-growing lines had the exact same early growth rate (days 0–19) as birds that died of ascites, and that ascites susceptibility segregated as **two complementary dominant major genes**, allowing divergent lines to differ by **93.9% vs. 9.5%** in ascites with only a **5%** difference in growth[29]. In Druyan's framework, fast growth is merely a metabolic *trigger* that exposes susceptible genotypes, meaning breeders can eliminate the susceptibility alleles without slowing growth[29].

A second dispute centers on **excessive oxygen demand (Julian 2000) versus impaired oxygen supply and functional hypothyroidism (Decuypere et al. 2000, 2005)**. Whereas **Julian (2000)** attributes low-altitude ascites primarily to excessive tissue **oxygen demand** driven by rapid growth and high feed intake[57], **Decuypere et al. (2000, 2005)** counter that low-altitude ascites is caused by **impaired oxygen supply** linked to **functional hypothyroidism**: intense selection for low FCR favored birds with lower maintenance heat production and blunted thyroid hormone (T3) activity during embryonic development, retarding lung and cardiovascular maturation and causing chronic juvenile hypoxemia and hypercapnia (high pvCO2)[2]. A third mechanistic distinction involves **hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction versus cold-induced cardiac output surges (Wideman & Tackett 2000)**: while high altitude triggers ascites via alveolar hypoxia and acute pulmonary arteriole vasoconstriction[29], **Wideman & Tackett (2000)** demonstrated that cold stress at low altitude elevates pulmonary arterial pressure primarily by forcing a **cold-induced surge in cardiac output** through rigid lung capillaries, rather than via hypoxemic vasoconstriction[20].

### 4. Recent Findings (2020–2026) That Change the Picture

Recent molecular, genomic, and epidemiological research between 2020 and 2026 has transformed scientific understanding of ascites from a purely mechanical hemodynamic overload into an active cellular, metabolic, and immunogenetic disorder. A 2026 study in the *Pakistan Veterinary Journal* (46(6):1545–1556, DOI: `10.29261/pakvetj/2026.146`[64]; see also **Kang et al. 2025**) revealed that in naturally ascitic broilers, **Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Kinase 4 (`PDK4`)** drives glycolytic lactate accumulation in **pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs)**. Lactate directly stimulates PASMC proliferation, accelerates the G1/S cell cycle transition, and suppresses apoptosis, causing **pulmonary vascular remodeling (PVR)** and medial arterial wall thickening[64]. Parallel myocardial metabolomics in *Animals* (**2022**, 12(19):2488, DOI: `10.3390/ani12192488`[110]) demonstrated that the right ventricle of clinically ascitic broilers shifts its energy supply toward **enhanced glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation while suppressing the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle**, a shift governed by **Macrophage Migration Inhibitory Factor (`MIF`) and AMP-activated protein kinase (`AMPK`)**[110].

These cellular pathways have opened new molecular and nutritional targets. A September 2023 study in *Poultry Science* (102(9):102859, PMID: `37390553`[111]) demonstrated that activating **peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (`PPARγ`)** attenuates pulmonary vascular remodeling and serves as a targeted molecular treatment for broiler ascites. Studies between 2022 and 2024 further showed that activating the **FOXO3a signaling pathway** in lung tissue regulates oxidative stress and PASMC apoptosis[89], and that supplementing cold-stressed broilers on canola-meal diets with **L-arginine or guanidinoacetic acid (GAA) combined with phenylalanine** (Oct 2024[87]), or combinations of **phytobiotics, L-arginine, vitamin E, and captopril** (July 2024[87]), directly modulates ascites-related gene expression. At the breeding level, **Lee (2022)** ([112]) and recent genomic updates ([16]) validated Whole-Genome Resequencing (WGR) for marker-assisted selection against ascites in commercial grandparent lines, identifying candidate polymorphisms in exon 8 and intron 6 of the **carboxypeptidase Q (`CPQ`) gene on chromosome 2 (~127 Mbp)**, alongside **cadherin 6 (`CDH6`) and melanocortin-4 receptor (`MC4R`) on chromosome 2** (governing male resistance) and the **angiotensin II type 1 receptor (`AGTR1`) on chromosome 9**[16]. Finally, 2025 and 2026 studies (*Animals* **2026**, 16(18):2959[93]; Feb 2025 cardiac RNA-seq study and April 2026 truckload condemnation study[107]) establish that pectoral muscle myopathies (**Wooden Breast**) and central cardiorespiratory failure (**pulmonary hypertension and ascites**) are interconnected: RNA sequencing in 2025 revealed a distinct molecular pathology footprint in the hearts of broilers with severe Wooden Breast[107], and 2026 physiological syntheses urge modeling cardiac output, pulmonary capillary transit, peripheral microcirculation, and muscle oxygen utilization as a single integrated system[93].

(2008):** Direct automated full-page extraction of the ResearchGate/OUP PDFs returned access blocks[26], so analysis relied on extensive indexed text excerpts, abstracts, and table snippets from those exact papers[2].
