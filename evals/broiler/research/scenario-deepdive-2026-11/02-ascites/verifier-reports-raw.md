## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation (and contradictions with the raw answers)

- Merck says there are "no clinical signs until RVF occurs and ascites develops", and that death can come suddenly before signs or fluid. For a simulation this supports a hidden pre-clinical phase followed by a short visible phase; Merck gives no duration for either.
- Merck: growth stops as right ventricular failure develops, so affected birds may be smaller than penmates, but sometimes the largest birds are affected. The raw answers' picture of "small sick birds" is only part of it.
- Merck: "Not all broilers that die from ascites syndrome actually have ascites"; a thickened right ventricular wall is enough for the diagnosis. This matters for any simulated on-farm mortality classification (ascites deaths are under-counted if fluid is the test).
- Tech note, visible signs: comb and wattles darken slightly early in the disease, then turn dark blue once fluid restricts breathing; this is the only staged visible-sign description in group A. Mechanism chain (Figure 1): higher oxygen demand, more cardiac output, enlarged and partly failing heart, liver fluid leak into the abdomen, ascites.
- Tech note, ventilation: "most influential environmental factor" is the oxygen content of incoming air; ventilation must be linked to house biomass; cold-stress duration matters more than temperature. Tech note also states Aviagen claims selection has cut ascites predisposition.
- Contradiction with Gemini: Gemini puts 300 mL under Merck [1]; it is in the Acta review (introduction), which attributes it to a Serbian trade-journal article (Maslic-Strizak et al. 2012, Tehnologija mesa). Gemini's "must be humanely culled [1]" is not in Merck.
- Contradiction with Gemini's lighting row: the tech note says lighting programmes for ascites are unnecessary at low altitude for Aviagen birds because they cost growth and breast yield; the summary box alone omits that limit. The Acta review separately says shortening daylight at 3 to 4 days of age "can reduce the incidence of ascites by 30 to 60%" (citing Decuypere et al. 2000), and that restricted lighting lowered ascites mortality (Hassanzadeh 2003); this is a secondary-review figure, not a trial result, and bears on group J (J7: the 30 to 40% figure).
- Acta review, other secondary-review figures that may surface in the raw answers (all second-hand): 7 mmHg fall in oxygen partial pressure per 1,000 m (cites Druyan 2012); about 5% of broilers and 20% of roasters die of ascites (cites Balog 2003); about 1 billion US dollars worldwide annual loss (Maxwell and Robertson 1997); first-week feed restriction cutting mortality by 75% (Maslic-Strizak 2012); skip-a-day feeding days 7 to 13 with low-protein diet cutting ascites "from over 30% to 5%" (Maxwell and Robertson 1997); McGovern et al. 2000 (Poult Sci 79(3):324, not 1999) found 6.3% ascites mortality with temperature fluctuations versus 4.6% control, not significant; Julian 1992 sodium trial: more Na raised ascites deaths and day 3-4 gain, lowered day 42 weight; Shlosberg 1998: 10% feed restriction cut ascites, potassium carbonate in water cut it but gave the lowest day-42 weights; Akşit 2008: 200 mg/kg vitamin E gave some mortality reduction but no improvement in oxidative-stress or troponin T measures; Villar-Patino 2002: vitamin C 400 mg/kg or vitamin E 75 mg/kg improved growth but not ascites mortality.
- The Acta review itself states that broilers with ascites show distended abdomens, respiratory distress and cyanosis, dark breast muscle, and a grey enlarged liver; it contains nothing on duration of illness, pain, or welfare (no "agony", "pain" or "welfare" in its text). Neither does Merck. Merck's page contains no welfare or pain language either.
- Year discrepancy: the Acta review lists Julian, Friars, French, Quinton as "1986" with volume 31 (the volume is 1987); relevant to group C's citation check.

## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation, and contradictions

- **Same sentence in two reviews.** The last two sentences of the Baghbanzadeh and Decuypere 2008 abstract ("As the high metabolic rate (fast growth) is a major factor... seem practically viable methods, since final body weight is not compromised. Optimization of the house temperature and ventilation in cold weather seem helpful practices...") are almost word for word the same as the abstract of Aftab and Khan 2005. One review appears to have borrowed from the other, so these two cannot be counted as independent support for the "restriction does not cost final weight" claim.
- **That claim is contradicted inside Aftab and Khan itself.** The abstract says final body weight is not compromised, but the body reports Acar 1995 restricted birds gained less (725 g and 721 g vs 822 g) with lower breast yield, and the Balog 2000b group on 8 h/day feed for 6 weeks weighed less at 42 d. The review's own data show weight and yield costs at least for some regimens. This matters for the restriction lever in the simulation.
- **Breeder-style rule of thumb for brooding** (Deaton 1996, via Aftab): suggested minimum temperatures 29.4, 26.7 and 24.4 °C in weeks 1 to 3. Colder (26.7, 24.4, 21.1 °C) raised ascites mortality at 6 weeks. This is a controlled-trial finding quoted by a review, not an industry guideline.
- **Furosemide** may lengthen survival time in diseased birds without helping the lung vessels (Forman and Wideman 2001, via Aftab). This is relevant to how long a sick bird lives, but it is a drug study and not a farm intervention.
- **Aftab and Khan say nothing about the experience of the living bird**: no signs, durations or pain. Neither do the Baghbanzadeh, Julian or Kalmar abstracts. The Wideman 2013 abstract says the disease has autoimmune/inflammatory features, with no sign or timeline.
- **Mortality range from a secondary source**: "mortality in affected flocks is over 1% and may rise to 20%, although incidence averaged 4.7% in a 1996 international survey (Maxwell and Robertson, 1997)" (chapter "The Impact of Disease on Welfare", PMC7121082, read as Europe PMC full text). This supports the 4.7% figure as real and its source as a trade-magazine survey, not a peer-reviewed paper.
- Gemini's coverage note itself says "Direct automated full-page extraction ... returned access blocks" for Baghbanzadeh 2008, and lists Kalmar as cited via secondary reviews. Gemini's detailed claims for B2, B3, B4 and B6 therefore rest on indexed snippets and secondary text, not on reading the papers. I could not test them either, so they stay unverified.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

From Moghadam 2001 (Canada, experimental lines, ad lib feed to day 49):
- Age at death from ascites (chronic right heart failure): mean 42 days (SD 15) in Cornish, 47 (SD 20) in White Rock; earliest 6 days, latest 95 and 103 days (Table 2). Hazard peaks around day 45, then falls (the authors note a feeding change at day 49, from broiler ration ad libitum to skip-a-day, as a possible reason).
- The authors state that mortality "could be expected to occur earlier in crossbreds, at high altitudes, or during cold temperatures".
- Deaths by acute heart failure before 65 days were treated as sudden death or flip-over, so sudden death and ascites share one necropsy-based boundary. Deaths cluster in families: of 2,062 Cornish sire families, 332 had one affected progeny and only 1 had eight.
- Prevalence in these experimental lines was low (1.1% to 1.5%); the study offers no data on what the living bird experiences.

From Pakdel thesis (cold schedule 30 °C at hatch down to 10 °C by day 22; 23 h light; 20 birds per m²; normal schedule 33 to 34 °C down to 17 to 18 °C by day 35):
- Total mortality 16% under cold vs 4% to 5% under normal conditions; the author attributes the excess to ascites but notes cause of death was not recorded.
- Of 3,697 cold-reared birds killed at 5 weeks, 155 (4.2%) had fluid in the abdomen (132 scored 2, 23 scored 1). Fluid in the heart sac was scored 1 or 2 in 1,915 of 3,696 (51.8%). These are survivors examined at slaughter, so they show subclinical disease frequency, not deaths.
- Mean RV:TV 27.9% under cold vs 20.6% under normal conditions; mean haematocrit 35.4% (cold). Males and slow-feathering birds had higher ascites-trait values.
- The thesis (ch. 1) describes fluid as plasma leaking from the liver and lists generalised oedema, pericardial fluid, lung oedema, liver cirrhosis and pale comb as signs; it quotes mortality "up to 5% in broiler flocks and up to 20% in roaster flocks" and 4.7% worldwide (Maxwell and Robertson 1997). It says nothing about behaviour, pain or time from first sign to death.
- Genetic correlation between body weight and RV:TV flips sign with the environment: -0.27 under cold (5 weeks) vs +0.50 under normal temperature (6 to 7 weeks); for HCT -0.23 vs +0.55. Pakdel explains the cold result as birds only reaching high weight if they resist ascites, and warns age differs between the two groups. Closter 2012 shows the same drift within one data set (positive at 2 weeks, negative by 7 weeks). Moghadam's positive +0.22 and +0.35 are from a normal-temperature experiment. This undercuts the memo's wording of claim C10 ("heritable separately from growth rate"): the data show moderate heritability, but the link to growth depends on disease incidence and measurement age; the only evidence of selecting growth up while holding ascites flat is the simulation (E3a).
- Heart-sac fluid, liver and abdominal score traits are strongly correlated with total mortality (genetic correlation 0.96 for fluid-in-abdomen vs mortality), which supports treating cold-trial mortality as mostly ascites.

From Lubritz 1995 abstract: cold-stressed male cockerels from three commercial male lines killed at 8 weeks showed fluid in the abdomen in 17.5%, 18.7% and 33.5%, mean RV:TV about 0.30.

From Neeteson 2023: Canadian condemnation data are the only prevalence trend in the paper and show it only as a picture; the authors credit breeding plus management together.

## 3. Extra facts useful for the welfare simulation (not in the claims)

- **When birds die of ascites (Huchzermeyer 1988, 1,350 m, winter):** first cases on **day 6**; percent of birds present that died of ascites per age band: 0-9 d 0.13 %, 10-16 d 0.37 %, 17-23 d 0.85 %, 24-30 d 2.41 %, 31-37 d 3.76 %, 38-44 d 3.26 %, 45-51 d 3.25 %. Ascitic birds had RV:TV 0.30 to 0.64, with a peak at 0.38 to 0.46. This is the only place in my sources that says how common undiagnosed right heart overload is in live, healthy-looking flocks (they were killed at weekly samples, so it is real data on slaughtered birds, not on birds that later died).
- **What affected birds look like (Feizi 2012):** stunted growth, smaller than flockmates, ruffled feathers, dark comb, swollen abdomen, weak pigmentation; at necropsy fibrin-containing fluid in the abdomen, oedematous liver, severe lung congestion, right ventricle hypertrophy, fluid around the heart. First cases week 3, mostly weeks 4 to 6.
- **No data on duration of illness or on the bird's experience** in any of these nine papers; they record deaths and post-mortem measures only. Nothing here addresses pain or breathlessness.
- **Rate benchmark:** Azizian cites a worldwide average ascites mortality of 4.7 % for fast male broilers (Gonzales 1998b; Hassanzadeh 2008) and found 4.3 % across its high-productivity hybrids. Varmaghany 2021 cites "5 to 8 %" (via Baghbanzadeh 2008, not checked by me).

- **Contradictions with the raw answers:** wrong author names (Feizi; Huchzermeyer); wrong issue number for Feizi (6(13)); "weeks 4 to 6" (first cases week 3); Elicit's "two susceptible, two resistant" for De Ruyck is right but strains are unnamed there; Gemini's per-group "cases" reading of Feizi Table 2 is not what the table says.

### G9 answer (does cold in weeks 1 to 3 specifically raise later ascites?)

- Only İpek and Sahan 2006 isolates it: cold applied in weeks 1 to 3 only, everyone at 21 °C afterwards, ascites deaths 9.52% vs 1.90% in days 21 to 42. It is one trial, 105 birds per group, a moderate (4 to 5 °C) reduction, in pelleted-diet winter conditions.
- Moayyedian 2011 starts the cold at day 2 (step-down to 15 °C by day 17), but the cold group also had a richer diet, so the effect is confounded; deaths began in week 3.
- Bahadoran 2010 and Shinder 2011 expose birds to early cold (day 3 to 4; embryo days 18 to 19) and find FEWER, not more, ascites later. So brief early cold can protect; sustained lower brooding temperature raises risk. The sources do not reconcile this beyond the authors' "thermotolerance" interpretation.
- Wideman and Tackett 2000 (35 to 42 d), Julian 1989 (days 22 to 57, per Elicit's table; not read by me, see group C) and Wang 2002 (from day 14) test later cold, not weeks 1 to 3. Daneshyar and Abdulkarimi: schedule not available to me (abstract only).
- So: supported as a direction by İpek (and loosely Moayyedian), but the sources do not show that weeks 1 to 3 are special compared with later cold. The memo claim that cold in weeks 1 to 3 is the key window rests on Merck/Gemini text outside this group (not checked here).

## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation (and contradictions with the raw answers)

- Time course of the live bird (Moayyedian, full text): in the cold group a few birds showed ruffled feathers, reluctance to move and a pale shrunken comb at the end of week 2, some with cyanosis and abdominal distension in severe cases, getting worse to the end of rearing. First ascites deaths in week 3 (3 birds), then 4, 5, 5 per week through week 6. Venous pO2 fell and pCO2 rose measurably only from week 4. Dead birds had straw-coloured fluid in the abdomen, partly with fibrin clots, a dilated right side of the heart with blood clot, and fatty changes in the liver. Source: `work-G/moay/m.txt`.
- Baseline at ordinary temperature is not zero: in Moayyedian the normal-temperature group (21 °C) still had 9 ascites deaths (about 11% of about 80; deaths in weeks 5 and 6: 3 and 6), and in Bahadoran the control group (also given a cold challenge days 14 to 28 at altitude) had 16 of 125. Both are challenge designs, not field rates.
- İpek: the cold group's ascites deaths fell in days 21 to 42 while the group was in compensatory growth, and final weight was the same as the control; so a flock can look fine on weight and still have excess deaths. Heart indices (RV/TV 0.25) were below the 0.28 hypertrophy cut-off, so the deaths were not predicted by mean heart ratio.
- Abdulkarimi (abstract): the cold-ascites birds also had more leg problems and shorter, narrower leg bones; a second welfare cost beyond ascites.
- Bahadoran: a 3-hour 15 °C exposure of 3 to 4 day-old chicks raised plasma corticosterone significantly, i.e. a real stress response; yet by day 42 those birds were 117 g heavier than controls.
- Contradictions with Elicit: (a) Elicit wrote İpek's 9.52% vs 1.90% "at week 3 and week 6"; it is the cumulative day 21 to 42 window. (b) Elicit's Bahadoran row says "reduced ascites mortality"; the paper's own results call it "numerically" lower (8 vs 16 deaths) with no test. (c) Abdulkarimi's abstract reports total mortality, not ascites mortality. (d) Moayyedian's summary says ascites incidence was "clearly higher", but the results text says "numerically" and no test was run on deaths (17 vs 9). No contradiction found with Gemini on G8.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation (with sources)

- Non-fatal cases are common: in Boostani 2010, 17.5% of control birds had ascites on physical examination but only 10.83% died of it, so about 6.7 points of the flock were visibly ascitic and survived to day 42 (simulated 1,890 m plus cold, an induced-challenge setting, not ordinary farm conditions). Source: Boostani Table 2.
- Cold from day 21 to 42 (16 C) gave 5.5% vs 1.5% ascites mortality in Bölükbasi and Emsen 2006, and 13.3% (pellet) in Bölükbasi 2005. Cold birds ate more and were heavier at day 42 in both studies (the cold did not slow growth).
- Feed form is a growth-rate effect, not a separate cause: Kuleile 2020 (Lesotho, 1,650 m, 200 mixed-sex Ross 308, 28 days): pellets 2,470 g vs mash 1,698 g live weight, ascites 6.25% vs 0.75% (p = 0.033), skeletal disorders 5.75% vs 0.75%, total mortality 7.5% vs 0.7%. But Bölükbasi 2005 (Ross PM3) found NO growth difference between feed forms while ascites still differed. So the mash effect does not always run through slower growth.
- Gemini's claim (row 4) that mash "lowers final body weight gain and worsens FCR" is true in Kuleile 2020 but contradicted by Bölükbasi 2005 in the same table of evidence.
- In the field (Lesotho), ascites was reported as more than 50% of winter mortality and 32% of total mortality (Van Biljon 2006, quoted in Kuleile 2020), with SDS 21%; the paper says ascites "later affects bird movement" (citing Saffar and Khajali 2010) and that sudden-death birds die lying on their backs with feet up. These are second-hand quotations from a low-altitude-limited, smallholder Lesotho setting.
- Low-protein (18% CP) is a harsh lever: zero ascites deaths but 16% lower 42-day weight, which a commercial farm paid by weight would not accept. Intermittent 1L:3D lighting from day 8 cut ascites mortality from 5% to 2% with no significant weight loss (2,143 vs 2,160 g).
- Boostani's restriction was time-limited access (8 h/day for 2 weeks), which cut intake only about 5.6% over the whole cycle; it is a mild programme compared with quantitative restriction.
- Altitude thresholds repeated in these papers: "increases significantly at altitudes greater than 1300 m" (Boostani and Kuleile both), and "optimum broiler production requires maximum of 1400 m" (Kuleile). No source here supports "900 m".
- Contradictions with the raw answers: (a) Elicit's Hassanzadeh 2010 row is mis-attributed (see H6b). (b) Gemini's "no significant loss in FCR" for Boostani understates it: FCR improved significantly in R7-21d. (c) Gemini's "da Silva 0% on mash" is right, but it is not in the document Gemini's neighbouring citation points to. (d) Bendheim's abstract says sex was not correlated with PCV or AHI, so the 18% vs 11% male/female contrast should be treated as unverified.

## 3. Extra facts and I10

**I10: is any supplement tested against fast growth at ordinary temperatures? No.** The challenge in each paper:
- Hassanzadeh 1997: dietary T3 (abstract); possibly cold too (unverified).
- Nemati 2017: continuous cold, 15 °C from day 21 to 42 (the normal-temperature control, PC, is a comparison group, not a test of the supplement).
- Geng 2004: cold 15 to 18 °C on days 15 to 21 only.
- Wang 2013: cold cycle 17/14 °C from day 11 to 42.
- Wideman 1995 (both): cool 10 to 15 °C from week 3, in broiler-breeder male by-product chicks, which are not a fast commercial line.
- Bottje 1995: low ventilation.
- Özdemir/Akşit: low ambient temperature, 0 to 6 weeks.
- Ali and Naqvi: field winter, with no randomised unsupplemented comparison.
So the memo claim C6 ("growth rate matters more than ventilation or vitamin C") is neither supported nor refuted by these. They show supplements can reduce mortality under cold, T3 or poor-ventilation challenge. A supplement trial at normal temperature on a fast line is missing from this group.

**Useful for a welfare simulation (all from sources I read in full unless marked):**
- Nemati 2017: the normal-temperature control group (23 to 25 °C, Hybro males) still had 12.62% ascites mortality, and the cold unsupplemented group 20.02%. Haematocrit rose from 29.85% (normal) to 35.02% (cold); T3 rose and T4 fell in cold birds (T3/T4 ratio 0.33 vs 0.13). Cold also worsened FCR (2.25 vs 2.05).
- Wang 2013 diagnostic criteria for a bird counted as ascites: right ventricle hypertrophy with a slack heart muscle, swollen and stiff liver, clear yellowish gelatinous fluid in the pericardium or abdominal cavity. Cold birds also had worse mortality overall: total mortality 11.8% in controls.
- Ali and Naqvi 2022: the farms were characterised by "spells of sudden deaths overnight". In their discussion, citing other authors (secondary, not checked): brief wing flapping for 1 to 2 minutes before sudden death in well-fed broilers (Saki and Hemati 2011), and healthy fast-growing broilers dying suddenly while standing, walking or feeding (Julian 2005). Both are secondary citations.
- Bottje 1995 abstract: PHS birds were lighter, with higher haematocrit and right ventricular hypertrophy at 5 weeks, so affected birds were already smaller before death (abstract only).
- Ali and Naqvi: 4-hour feed withdrawal was used on all farms as a preventive; it is cited as a known way to reduce ascites without hurting growth (secondary).
- No paper in this group measures pain, behaviour, breathing rate or time from first sign to death. They count deaths and organ indices only.

(2) Gemini says CoQ10 (Geng 2004) improved FCR; the abstract says no growth effect. (3) Gemini applies Geng 2004 to L-carnitine; the Poult Sci paper is CoQ10 only. ## 3. Extra facts for the simulation

- All three papers that tested lighting against ascites with real data show the effect works through slower early growth and lower metabolic rate (lower T3, lower haematocrit), not through a direct heart effect. Hassanzadeh 2003 table 3: haematocrit 4 to 6 points lower under IP at days 28 to 35. This supports modelling lighting as an early-growth brake.
- Only Hassanzadeh 2003 used a natural challenge (altitude 2,000 m plus cold nights, no T3), and its mortality gap is 18 vs 6 vs 7 deaths out of 120 per room with no statistical test and one room per treatment. Buys 1998 and Hassanzadeh 2000 used T3 dosing (1.5 ppm), a drug that causes ascites, so their effect sizes do not transfer to untreated commercial flocks. The sim should not use any of them as a precise percentage cut.
- What the living bird goes through is mostly absent. Hassanzadeh 2003 says, in its closing line, that behavioural and welfare effects of these schedules in commercial flocks still need research. Intermittent 1L:3D means about 6 h of light and 18 h of dark in six cycles a day, so feed is eaten in short light windows (the paper notes blood sampled at the end of light periods came after feeding started). Gordon 1997 and the Chinese paper, which might address behaviour or welfare, are unreadable.
- Timing of deaths in the CL room of Hassanzadeh 2003: first ascites death day 14, peak days 29 to 35 (12 of 18).
- The 2014 J Poult Sci review (Hassanzadeh et al., open access, https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpsa/51/3/51_0130063/_pdf) says it is common to raise broilers on continuous or near continuous light with a short dark period "to accustom birds to darkness and to minimize panic in case of power failure", and that IL benefit was larger when applied only days 3 to 14 or 10 to 21 (Hassanzadeh 2005, Eur Poult Sci). That 2005 paper is in Crossref (doi 10.1016/s0003-9098(25)00577-6) but I did not read it.
- Contradiction with the raw answers: Gemini labels the intervention "uninterrupted dark period 6 h or more ... widely used" and claims "improves sleep, melatonin rhythms, immunity, leg health" with an unrelated citation [79] (BCC EU welfare paper). None of the lighting-ascites papers I could read measured sleep, immunity or leg health.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

What the chapter says about the bird in each phase, with its cited sources (the chapter's claims, not independently verified against those sources):

- Hypoxaemia (about 1-2 weeks, awake 98-280 h). Presumed lethargy, weakness, raised heart and breathing rate, breathlessness; "most clinical symptoms are not apparent at this stage to caretakers"; heaviest birds may be seen panting with greatly reduced movement; birds can still eat and drink though drinking frequency "may be reduced". Males worse than females. Sources: human pneumonia in children [38], Wideman 2013 [5], Olkowski et al. 1997 ECG [22]. Intensity is an analogy, not measured.
- Pulmonary hypertension (about 1-3 weeks, awake 98-420 h). Right ventricular failure birds "display reduced growth and may be smaller than their penmates" [9] (Julian 2005). Appetite versus discomfort unknown (probabilities split equally). Sources for human analogies [40-42].
- Fluid transudation (2-5 days, awake 28-100 h). Distended abdomen ("water belly"), "dull and depressed", "reluctant to move", bradycardia, rapid breathing, open-beak panting, cyanosis of comb and wattles (comb may look sunken), dilated superficial veins; compression of abdominal air sacs by fluid causes "severe respiratory distress" [44,45] (Bottje 1998 lung lining fluid; Julian 1998); "birds may survive for a few days with visible ascites symptoms" [9] (Julian 2005). ## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation (and contradictions with the raw answers)

- **What the living bird experiences, from Danish necropsy study:** farmers euthanised 12.4% of the sampled dead birds (311 of 2,508); of these, 42.12% were euthanised for "severe clinical signs related to ascites, septicemia, polyserositis, or uremia", 36.33% for lameness, 14.15% emaciation (Al-Saeedi 2026). Ascites share of dead birds rose with age: 3.4% (under 18 d), 7.6% (18 to 28 d), 12.7% (28 d or older; PR 3.81 vs youngest, 1.91 to 7.58). Cardiovascular collapse overall was commoner at young ages (85.7% under 18 d, 68.3% at 28 d or older), with the cardiac-dilatation form commoner in older birds. The authors treat ascites, heart dilatation, liver cirrhosis and pericardial effusion as "chronic" lesions and lung oedema/congestion as the "sudden" pattern. No live-bird welfare measures (gait, behaviour) were taken.
- **Timing:** ascites in Ross rose with slaughter age in Norway (Forseth 2023 Table 6), which is why Norsk Kylling could not keep Ross past 32 to 33 days (HSI case study).
- **Winter effect:** Ross ascites is strongly seasonal (PR 6.54 in winter; Ross spring/summer/autumn close to each other). The hybrid x season interaction is the paper's strongest cold-linked finding at flock scale.
- **Variance:** most unexplained variation in ascites sits at batch and flock level (about 65% for Ross, 77% for Hubbard), farm level 23 to 27%.
- **Transport:** Ross DOA 0.063% vs 0.015%; 94% of flocks above the 90th percentile of DOA were Ross (369 of 392); coldest-temperature IRR 4.70. Only 6% of Ross flocks had zero DOA vs 29% of Hubbard flocks.

Overall mortality did not differ between strains, which contradicts the Norwegian 2x finding; the authors suggest Rustic Gold grows only moderately more slowly.
- **BCC report (006) side facts, with the citations it gives:** enlarged right ventricle in 34.4% of DOA birds vs 4.1% of slaughtered birds (Nijdam 2006); ascites mortality 0 to 30%; about 8% of US broiler mortality attributed to ascites, over $100 million a year (Pavlidis 2007); up to 40 minutes of "excruciating pain" in the fatal stage (Schuck-Paim and Alonso 2022). All secondary; not independently checked.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation, and contradictions of the raw answers

- Contradiction: Gemini says EFSA 2023 concluded that fast-growing broilers "should no longer be kept". EFSA 2023 recommends limiting growth to a maximum of 50 g/day to keep birds healthier and more active; the stronger wording comes from the Animal Law Europe position paper. Gemini also cites [72] (Forseth 2024) for EFSA's view on ascites, which is the wrong paper.
- EFSA 2023 does not list ascites, pulmonary hypertension or cardiovascular disease among its 19 highly relevant welfare consequences; the on-farm opinion is mostly about leg problems, stocking density, litter, temperature and light. Use EFSA 2023 for growth-rate, mortality and condemnation framing, not for ascites mechanism.
- EFSA 2023 also says (section 3.6) that ad libitum fed broilers kept past slaughter age "become obese, develop heart and leg problems"; it gives a growth-rate scale (fast above 60 g/day, intermediate 50, slower 45 or less) and says Aviagen Arbor Acres males can gain above 100 g/day.
- EFSA 2010 on mortality: a low 2010 expert weighting for ascites (welfare impact median 0.40) and an industry claim that ascites had declined in the previous decade, both based on industry data, "anecdotal" in its own words. EFSA 2010 also says management levers (feed restriction, long dark periods, lower energy, low light) cut ascites and SDS but raise production cost.
- EFSA 2010 notes that 14 to 18 C house temperatures triggered ascites at low altitude (Albert and Frankenhuis 1990) and gives Hernandes 2002 numbers: Hubbard 4% (thermoneutral) and 41% (cold) ascites mortality versus 0% in naked neck both times. That is a usable "cold challenge" magnitude.
- RSPCA trial sizes: protocol uses 2 trials x 4 pens x 50 birds (200 per breed per trial). Real growth ceilings: indoor 60 g/day. A breed at the cap would still be about 1.0% ascites deaths allowed, 1.0% heart-attack deaths allowed, 3.0% total deaths allowed, which is a usable benchmark for a "good" fast-growing flock in a controlled environment (not a farm).
- The RSPCA 2018 trial, which includes three conventional hybrids, recorded heart attack but no ascites deaths as a category; 8% of 141 deaths and culls were heart attacks. A small controlled-environment trial found no named ascites mortality in modern hybrids at 35 to 38 days on non-restricted pelleted feed. (This reads as evidence that ascites was rare under good brooding and ventilation, but the report does not say so explicitly.)
- Van Middelkoop 2002 (via de Jong): fast Cobb mortality 5.6% vs 1.5% slow Hubbard, mainly heart and circulation problems.
- Eat Sit Suffer: irregular heart rhythm already detectable at 7 days and up to 27% of a fast flock (Olkowski and Classen 1998); SDS just over a third of on-farm deaths (Kittelsen 2015). Both are SDS rather than ascites.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

- Timing of deaths under high-altitude challenge (Bahadoran 2010, Ross, 2,100 m farm, cold nights days 14 to 28): ascites deaths started at day 19 and 24 and were spread over weeks 3 to 6, with the most deaths in weeks 5 and 6 (Lin 5 and 7, Hin 4 and 3). Source: Bahadoran 2010 Table 2.
- Survivors carry the disease too: in that study 21/50 low-altitude-hatched survivors had RV:TV ≥0.29 at day 42 against 13/50 for high-altitude-hatched, so many birds have the heart lesion without dying. Same table.
- Chamber studies show growth cost and blood changes before death: 2,896 m birds weighed about 500 g less (Witzel 1990); body weight lower from day 7 at 2,133 m and total mortality 20.3% vs 4.6% (Yersin 1992). Abstracts only.
- Witzel 1990: corticosterone was lower, not higher, at 2,438 and 2,896 m. This runs against the idea that these birds show a plain stress-hormone rise; it is one abstract and I could not read the methods.
- Daily-life signs, from the Ethiopia paper's review section (second-hand, from Merck and similar): affected birds are cyanotic, have a distended abdomen, breathe faster, tolerate exercise poorly, "often just sit and pant", and "frequently die on their backs"; death may come suddenly before signs; growth stops as right-heart failure develops so affected birds may be smaller than pen mates, though sometimes the largest birds are affected; males more often than females; mortality greatest after 5 weeks. Not primary data.
- The İpek 2015 trial (Cobb 500, good management, 20 °C from week 3) shows how weak the link between heart changes and death can be: RV:TV 0.33 and fluid in 7 birds in the high-EST group, yet zero ascites deaths in 240 birds. The authors say deaths would "presumably be very high" under poorer housing, which is their opinion, not a result.
- Incubation altitude can lower later ascites (Hassanzadeh 2004 abstract; Bahadoran 2010 figures), but a hot incubator (38.9 °C from day 7) raises it (Molenaar 2011 abstract), and the Maatjens thesis suggests 35.6-36.7 °C in the last week builds a bigger heart at hatch without yet showing any ascites effect. For a simulation lever, only the Molenaar direction has an ascites outcome behind it.
- Contradictions of the raw answers: (a) Gemini says the SciELO paper is Molenaar's and that it shows higher ascites mortality at high EST; the paper in that URL shows none. (b) Gemini says Maatjens gives "0.7% fewer second-grade chicks"; the thesis gives 1.1% at 38.9 °C against 0.0% elsewhere, and the 0.7% belongs to Molenaar's high-EST group in the other direction. (c) Elicit's Bahadoran summary ("lower ascites mortality") is right but the paper gives only counts (8 vs 15 of 125) with no significance test, and the cold challenge plus 2,100 m altitude means it is a stressed-farm result.
