## 3. Extra facts for a welfare simulation (all from the abstracts above; ⚠️ abstract only)

1. Only 3 of the 23 SDS deaths (13%) had a history of ventricular arrhythmia on the scheduled ECGs in the 1998 study (and 1 of 13 ascites birds; 5 ascites birds had conduction block). So a bird that dies of SDS usually did not show an arrhythmia on the periodic ECGs beforehand. This is the main limit on using arrhythmia prevalence (17%) as an early-warning signal in a simulation, and it contradicts the implied reading that arrhythmic birds are the ones that die. Source: 9591472 abstract.
2. SDS is male-skewed in this flock: males were 74% of the 23 SDS cases (about 17 birds) though males were 52% of the 505 birds (265/505). Source: 9591472.
3. Heart rate: SDS-dying males had a higher heart rate than their flock mates; ascites birds had a lower rate. Source: 9060143. A simulation could use tachycardia as a pre-event marker for the SDS pathway and bradycardia for the ascites pathway, but with only 8 SDS cases (and ECGs at only three timepoints).
4. In the 300-male study, 8 SDS deaths = 2.67% of the cohort over the monitoring window (12-15 days to about 32-35 days plus the following period; end date not in the abstract), 4 ascites deaths = 1.33%, plus 12 ascites condemnations at slaughter = 4.0% (so ascites in total 5.33% of males counting condemnations). My arithmetic from the abstract's counts.
5. Terminal-episode timing conflict to flag for the orchestrator: Olkowski and Classen 1997 give "within four to six minutes of the onset of clinical signs" until a flat ECG. Gemini and group D report a mean of 53 seconds for the attack in Newberry 1987 (group D checks that). These do not directly conflict because they measure different things (onset of clinical signs to cessation of electrical activity versus the attack itself), but the 4-6 minutes should not be read as 4-6 minutes of conscious suffering. In the controls of the same paper, a normal ECG pattern persisted for two to three minutes after cervical dislocation, which means heart electrical activity is not a measure of consciousness in this paper; consciousness was not assessed in any abstract. The consciousness interpretation is mine, not the authors'.
6. The 1998 abstract says the comparison for "broilers are more susceptible" is against breeders and two non-broiler breeds, not against slower-growing broiler strains. It cannot support claims about growth rate within broilers (memo C8).

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

1. **Handling and catching as triggers (secondary report of Olkowski 2008).** Scorza 2010 says Olkowski and colleagues "observed increased incidence of SDS when chickens were subjected to some stressful activities such as blood collection, weighing, or during routine catching, loading and transportation" (their ref 18 is the 2008 paper). I could not check this inside the 2008 text, so treat it as Scorza's report. It supports putting catching, weighing, loading and other handling events in the trigger table. The same passage lists overcrowding, antagonistic behaviour and metabolic stress from rapid growth as commercial stressors, again as Scorza's summary of Olkowski.
2. **Deaths came days after the stress test, not during it (2008 abstract).** The abstract says the most severely affected birds died "within several days" of the epinephrine challenge. In the simulation this fits a delayed-risk model (a stressed bird with a severe arrhythmic response has raised risk over following days), and not an instant stress-then-death rule.
3. **Baseline arrhythmia on a normal commercial diet (Nain 2007 abstract).** The control group had ECG arrhythmia in 11.8% of birds (male commercial broilers, trial of unknown length, ECG at intervals). The high-D3 group had 22.6%. That is another figure for the arrhythmia prevalence among birds that look healthy.
4. **Day 32 is when Nain challenged birds.** Epinephrine 100 microg/kg BW on day 32 under continuous ECG; that is inside the 2 to 4 week to day-35 SDS window in the memo.
5. **Where "17 to 30%" may have come from (not settled; group A owns it).** I found three different statements, none a clean "17 to 30%": (a) Olkowski and Classen 1998: 17% dysrhythmia at 42-44 d (group A). (b) A Spanish seminar PDF credits Olkowski 2008 with "3% to 6% of normal broilers succumb" and "17-35% show cardiac arrhythmia on ECG"; I cannot verify that against the 2008 paper, so treat as unverified secondary. (c) A trade interview with Olkowski (Canadian Poultry magazine, "Healthy hearts for healthy birds", by Lilian Schaer, dated February 5, 2021, `https://www.canadianpoultrymag.com/healthy-hearts-for-healthy-birds/`, read to the end) says multiple studies concluded that "between 20 and 30 per cent of broilers may be affected by heart pump problems", visible or not. That is heart-pump (heart failure) prevalence, not arrhythmia prevalence. The same article quotes Olkowski saying breeder selection has reduced the direct losses from SDS and ascites compared with the 1990s. Gemini's phrase "17% to 30%" therefore looks like a splice of differently defined numbers.
6. **Nothing here on what the living bird feels.** None of the four papers describes illness before death or gives any consciousness data. Scorza's seizure comparison is an analogy by its authors. Greenlees found no change in blood pressure, heart rate or blood gases in awake restrained birds across groups, which is a (weak, 1989, small) sign that fast-growing 3-week birds are not obviously physiologically abnormal at rest; it says nothing about subjective state.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

- What the living bird experiences before death (p. 8, citing Newberry 1987 [51] and Ononiwu 1979 [50]): birds "appear to be perfectly healthy", "engage in normal behaviors such as feeding, standing, walking, and lying", with "no reduction in activity levels, no difference in behavioural patterns between healthy birds and those that succumb to SDS, no difference in appetite or feeding behavior, no reduction of maintenance activities such as preening, and no diminished responsiveness to external stimuli". WFP's cardiac-history point (E2) is the only sign of earlier compromise, and it is invisible to a caretaker.
- Terminal episode (p. 8): "violently flapping their wings and extending their necks", "strong muscle contractions and a severe loss of balance, causing them to fall over", some birds give a "squawk" vocalisation. "The vast majority of birds are found deceased lying on their back" (no percentage), others on the sternum. Cause of death "believed to be ventricular fibrillation [52,53]" (Julian and Bowes 1987; Olkowski and Classen 1997). Underlying mechanism "believed to be caused by a lack of oxygen to the myocardium, leading to acute heart failure" (p. 2).
- Triggers WFP names (p. 2, 8): arrhythmia plus stress raises sudden-death risk; high stocking density "lead to increased mortality rates by SDS [14]" (Imaeda 2000); "metabolic strain from rapid growth or poor environmental conditions such as overcrowding" [54] (Olkowski 2008).
- Recording difficulty, relevant to the memo's under-recording claim (p. 3): "accurate diagnoses of the underlying causes of mortality can be difficult for conditions such as SDS, where no specific post-mortem lesions are present"; slaughter-line surveys "disregard on-farm and transport mortality". WFP states diagnostic difficulty but does not say farms fail to record SDS as a category.
- Sex: WFP gives no male share for SDS; ascites is said to hit males more.
- WFP's SDS prevalence is an assumption range (0.5 to 4%) taken from a veterinary manual and a magazine-style review (Banday 2011 on Engormix), not a measured modern flock figure; WFP itself notes no recent commercial data (research gaps, p. 11, mostly about ascites).
- Sleep assumption (p. 4): WFP assumes no negative state while sleeping; irrelevant for SDS because the episode happens awake.
- Overview page: heat stress outweighs cardiopulmonary pain for the average bird "given their relatively low prevalence"; fatal ascites "nearly 40 minutes in Excruciating pain".
- Contradictions with the raw answers: Gemini's "mean 53 seconds" is Newberry's (not in WFP); Gemini's "SCAHAW (2000, p. 41) and WFP ... 37 to 69 seconds" is wrong on the attribution: WFP cites only Newberry 1987 [51] for 37 to 69 s and does not cite SCAHAW anywhere in this chapter (SCAHAW does not appear in the reference list). Elicit's "2004" for the Vet Res Commun ECG paper is contradicted by WFP's reference (1997).

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

What the living bird experiences (the main gap in this group):
- None of the five papers observed a bird dying or timed the terminal episode. All are necropsy or mortality-count studies. Ononiwu 1979b says the barns were "kept under regular surveillance for observation of the clinical signs of broilers dying suddenly" but reports no behaviour.
- Bowes and Julian 1988 Abstract: death "occurs suddenly with a short, wing-beating convulsion". Introduction: "Respiratory distress has been reported before affected broilers convulse and die", citing their own 1985 conference abstract and the Merck Manual (so a second-hand statement, not a measurement). The same paper's Discussion says a full gut and recently eaten feed with an empty gall bladder "indicates that the sudden death is not preceded by morbidity". So the two statements pull in different directions within one paper. This matters if the memo says "no distress before death".
- Ononiwu 1979a (Discussion) argues the opposite mechanism from the later arrhythmia work: heart damage leads to lung oedema so the birds "are unable to breath", with hypoxia and peripheral circulatory failure (shock); lung congestion and oedema "considerably reduce gaseous exchange and enhance respiratory distress". That implies a terminal period with some respiratory distress. Bowes and Julian 1988 offer two alternatives: lung congestion from lying on the back after death, or left ventricular failure while the right ventricle keeps pumping. Merck (group G) says lung oedema rises after death; I did not check that here. The pathology therefore does not by itself settle that the death is instantaneous.
- Ononiwu 1979a: the empty or shrunken gall bladder in 100% of cases (142 birds) is read by the authors as evidence that the birds ate normally right up to death; the full crop and gizzard in every bird agree. Brigden and Riddell 1975 (feed in crop, full intestine) agree. This supports "healthy-looking until the moment of death" but is indirect.
- Julian 1986 (Introduction): "affected broilers die and do not continue to consume food", and the paper repeats that birds are normal-looking, fast growing males.

Incidence, sex and timing numbers from this group (for memo C1, C2 and C6), all of birds placed:
- Brigden and Riddell 1975 (western Canada, 4 flocks, 1.13% average, 0.45 to 2.13% by flock, peak week 3, 74 to 79% male).
- Ononiwu 1979b (one commercial farm, 47,200 birds, 1.10% over 7 weeks; SDS was 37% of all deaths; peak weeks 3 to 4; only 61% of the SDS deaths were male, 319 of 520, by my arithmetic, because the pens were roughly half male and half female and the male SDS rate was 1.36% against 0.84% for females). So the "70 to 80% male" figure is not universal in this group: it was 74 to 79% in Brigden and Riddell, 76% in Julian 1986, 77% of 142 necropsied birds in Ononiwu 1979a, but 61% in Ononiwu 1979b.
- Julian 1986 trial 1: 954 SDS deaths in 96,000 sexed birds (0.99%); 726 male (1.51% of 48,000 males) vs 228 female (0.48% of 48,000 females), 76% male, P under 0.0001. Trial 2 (unsexed): 1.54%. Barn and diet made no difference. Intro: SDS "mortality of 0.5 to 2% in most flocks"; "occasionally producers report SDS mortality as high as 4% in all-male flocks"; peak 2 to 4 weeks. (This is the original source of the memo's "0.5 to 2%" and "up to 4% in all-male flocks"; Julian 1986 cites Riddell and Springer 1985 for it.)
- Bowes and Julian 1988: SDS is "a loss of 1.5 to 2.0% of all broilers grown in Canada", "the primary cause of broiler mortality", 70 to 80% male, peak 2 to 4 weeks in Ontario (cites Riddell and Springer 1985, Riddell and Orr 1980, and their own 1985 conference paper).
- Julian 1986 Discussion: the incidence "can be lowered by slowing growth and will drop markedly for several days after a severe infectious bronchitis or Newcastle disease vaccination reaction or if birds are without feed for a 12 to 24 hour period". Evidence only for the memo's growth and feed-restriction lever, as an opinion, no data in this paper.
- Julian 1986 also notes many SDS-dead broilers had moderate to marked tibial dyschondroplasia, with no difference between diets.

Body weight: Ononiwu 1979b SDS birds slightly heavier than flock average (n=15 each: +5.5 g at 2 weeks, +8.7 g at 3, +4.3 g at 4, which is 2.34%, 2.2% and 0.79%); Brigden and Riddell found SDS birds at average weight (one flock). The popular idea that "the biggest birds die" has only weak support here.

Contradicting or tightening the raw answers:
- Gemini's reading of Brigden and Riddell (70 to 80% of male mortality, 20 to 25% of female mortality) is wrong, as above. Gemini also admits in its own coverage note that it could not open Brigden and Riddell, Ononiwu 1979b, Julian 1986 or Bowes 1988, and its Julian 1986 line (potassium) is a misreading.
- Gemini's story that Julian 1986 and Bowes and Julian 1988 show organ weights "match those of healthy flock-mates" overlooks the heavier liver; Bowes and Julian infer possible metabolic disease from it.
- Elicit q1 line 19 ("SDS affects broilers from about 2 days ... Ononiwu et al. 1979"): the "about 2 days" start is not in Ononiwu 1979a or 1979b (1979b bins deaths by week, from week 1; Brigden and Riddell show low ADS mortality in week 1). I did not look for the source of "2 days".
- Elicit q1 cites the Ononiwu 1979b lighting study as "abstract only"; the abstract is accurate for direction (continuous more SDS, cockerels more than pullets, weeks 3 to 4) but does not carry numbers.
- Memo C6 (longer uninterrupted dark lowers SDS): in Ononiwu 1979b, the "intermittent" programme was short repeated dark periods (1 to 3 hours) after day 10, not one long uninterrupted dark period. It shows that some darkness beats continuous light; it does not test dark-period length.

## 3. Extra facts and contradictions useful for the simulation

1. **Seasonal evidence points both ways.** Gardiner (British Columbia, males): highest SDS in January, lowest in July. Imaeda (Japan): SDS rose in summer and in winter, but only at 18 birds/m2. Karki (Nepal): the outbreak ran July to October. So "winter peak" or "summer peak" is not a stable rule; the one controlled finding is a stocking-density by season interaction at 18 birds/m2. (Gardiner abstract; Imaeda abstract; Karki raw text.)
2. **Age curve from Gardiner (males, abstract).** The SDS death rate climbs to about day 21, plateaus at 80 to 86 deaths per 100,000 chickens per day from day 21 to 39, then declines slowly to 54 per 100,000 per day at day 63; the maximum is between 21 and 27 days. My own rough arithmetic, not in the paper (assuming straight-line rise and fall between the stated points): that is about 2.5% of males dead of SDS by day 42 and about 4% by day 63. Treat only as a plausibility check against the 1.5 to 4% headline range.
3. **Body weight, not just age.** Gardiner reports the SDS death rate rises significantly with body weight (abstract). That supports a growth-rate driver in the model.
4. **Wideman, modern crosses (US, 2012):** about 1.2% of birds died of SDS between day 14 and 56 (both experiments pooled), roughly 1.5 times the ascites deaths in E1 and about three times in E2, with no difference between standard and high-yield crosses or between sexes. The sex-pooled result is at odds with the usual male-skew claim; the paper says the sexes did not differ but shows no numbers. The setting (wire floor lameness model, 60 to 80 birds per pen, cull at day 14) is not typical commercial.
5. **What the living bird looks like (Karki, field):** apparently normal, eating, drinking and walking until seconds before; then neck extension, squawk, wing beating and leg extension, then falls on its back. No duration is given. Siddiqui 2009 (secondary) says death "occurs within minutes" and that diagnosis rests on "sudden death within one minute by violent wing flapping, convulsion and death"; it cites Newberry 1987 for the video study but garbles the duration ("just under one month"), so do not use Siddiqui for timings. ### 3b. Findings that qualify or contradict the raw answers and the memo (C3, C6)

1. The single paper that carries both a "slower growth" test and an SDS count, Mollison Exp 1, found that slowing growth by restricting to 90% of ad libitum did not change SDS (abstract). Scott 2002 also separates growth from SDS: mash slowed 21-d growth but did not lower mortality, and low-density diets cut SDS (0.72% versus 1.81%) even though those birds were heavier at 35 d. Bowes 1988 is the one paper in the group that ties SDS to growth rate. So "slower early growth lowers SDS" (memo C3) is supported by Bowes only, and Scott and Mollison argue against it as a general rule. ⚠️ (abstract level for all three)
2. Scott 2002 on lighting (memo C6): intermittent lighting (four 6-hour cycles) did no better than constant light (1.68% versus 1.53%). Only the programme with variable, extended dark periods (DDI) was lower (0.80%), at the cost of lower 35-day weight. This weakens a plain statement that "intermittent or longer dark periods lower SDS" and fits what Ononiwu 1979b reported only partly; Sosnówka-Czajka 2022 describes Scott's result the same way. ⚠️
3. Gwavuya 2014: no SDS difference between 23L:1D and 12L:12D in 17,959 Cobb 500 birds over days 15 to 45, but with 3 houses per treatment. ⚠️
4. Restriction trials seen only through reviews (not opened by me): Sosnówka-Czajka 2022 says Lippens et al. 2000 saw "only a tendency" toward lower SDS with early restriction; Madrigal et al. 2002 found diet type had no influence; Sahraei 2012 says Deaton 1995 and Scheideler and Baughman 1993 found no significant restriction effect. It also says Tumová et al. 2019 restricted feed from day 7 to 14 to 80% or 65% of ad libitum and saw SDS mortality of 19, 12 and 10% (cockerels) and 12, 9 and 3% (pullets). Those numbers look high for SDS and could be total or metabolic mortality, so Tumová 2019 is a lead worth a primary-source check, not a usable figure. A Brazilian (UNESP) paper found by web search ("Uso da restrição alimentar quantitativa...") is quoted in a search snippet as 3.67% SDS ad libitum versus 0.33 to 1.33% at 10 to 50% restriction, not significant; I did not open it. All ⚠️ second-hand.
5. Mortality in the Nigerian trials (Oyedeji and Atteh, 7.5 to 10.6% over 8 weeks in unsexed birds) is total mortality in tropical conditions and says nothing about SDS.
6. Elicit q2 (checked against the abstracts): its Bowes, Nassef and Scott design details are right except the Scott "6 pens of 50 per trial" line; its Toplu and Oyedeji (nutrient density and photoperiod) rows are right; its Gwavuya "no difference in SDS" is right; its Mollison/Gwavuya/Imaeda rows are mislabelled (I2d).
7. Gemini overreach in these sources: "30 to 40% for 7 days" (Bowes), "50%" (Mollison), skip-a-day for the nutrient-density paper (Oyedeji and Atteh), "feeding at 90% of control improved ... immune organ weights" (the Mollison abstract mentions feed efficiency and fat pad, not immune organs; Gemini cites a different source [68] for that), and "Scott ... SDS and ascites" (SDS confirmed, ascites rates not in the abstract). Gemini admits it could not open Mollison and Bowes full text (its own coverage note), and did not read Scott or Nassef at all.

What the living bird goes through (these sources say little; mostly second-hand):

- Rotter thesis, lit review: Newberry et al. (1985 abstract) "concluded that SDS birds could not be distinguished from other flock members by behavioural characteristics".
- Chung thesis, lit review (second-hand, Newberry 1987): all SDS birds behaved normally before onset, then loss of balance, violent wing beating and strong muscular contraction "which lasted over a period of 53 seconds before death"; Julian and Bowes 1987 (second-hand): 70% of affected birds "flipped over" during the convulsion and were found on their backs. Discrepancy inside the report: the text and abstract give the mean duration as "53 +/- 3.1 s" (page image confirmed), but the ten durations in Appendix 7 (39, 42, 48, 37, 46, 44, 69, 61, 46, 46 s) average 47.8 s with standard error 3.12 s. The standard error matches the appendix data, so the mean of 53 in the text looks like an error; the range of 37 to 69 s is consistent with both. Anyone using "mean 53 s" (open.alberta.ca description, probably Newberry 1987 and Gemini) should check against this.
3. Last 12 hours: no significant difference between SDS birds and matched controls in 11 short behaviours (agonistic, shaking, scratching, running, wing flap, wobble, etc.) or in time spent in 8 long behaviours (lying 74.0% vs 77.3%, feeding 7.4% vs 6.1%, upright 17.7% vs 15.7%), nor in breathing rate (47 vs 43 per minute). Two SDS birds walked with a slightly wobbly gait and one had protruding tibias and a stiff gait. All but one bird were seen feeding and drinking within 2 hours of death. The authors conclude SDS birds "do not experience pain, depression or other unpleasant emotions associated with disease until the onset of sudden death". They also note the birds did not go into hypoglycaemic coma as in FLKS.
4. Triggers: none invariable. Behaviour just before loss of balance: lying 4, standing 3, feeding 2, walking 1; two had been drinking within a minute. Humans were in the barn for 3 deaths and one bird was bumped by another just before collapse; none died after a sudden loud noise, fight, cannibalism or piling. Six of ten died between 08:00 and 16:00.
5. Consciousness during the episode is not assessed in this report (no EEG or response testing); the 37 to 69 s is movement duration from video only.
6. Incidence in the tissue-lipid flock (5,000 males and 5,000 females, Peterson x Arbor Acres, to 42 days, 633 cm2/bird): SDS 1.34% of males and 0.44% of females by day 21; 2.38% of males (119 birds) and 0.66% of females (33) by day 42; 1.52% of all placed. Total mortality 5.40% of males and 2.52% of females. That means SDS was about 44% of male deaths and 26% of female deaths in this flock. Report's own general statement: SDS is 0.5 to 5% mortality, 16 to 84% of total flock mortality (citing a 1984 University of Manitoba progress report), 70 to 80% of SDS deaths in males, prairie survey average 1.95% (Riddell and Springer 1985).
7. SDS diagnosis criteria used: well fleshed, feed in digestive tract, normal bursa and proventriculus, empty gall bladder, congested lungs, no other disease.
8. From Whitehead and Randall 1982 (memo claim C5, under-recording): they note it is uncertain whether lung congestion and oedema occur at the time of death or after it (citing Riddell and Orr 1980), that "the diagnostic basis for SDS is limited", and that "the manner of death was not observed". Birds in their trials were reared with 1 hour of darkness a day.
9. Contradiction between sources: Alberta report finds plasma potassium HIGH in SDS birds (post-mortem, with artefact caveat); Shabani 2013 reports serum potassium and sodium LOW in dead control birds sampled 30 minutes after death. The latter paper's methods are weak (see K6), so I would not weigh it equally.

## 3. Extra facts useful for a welfare simulation

From the Danish necropsy paper (PMC13625790):
- Age pattern of collapse among dead birds (n = 927 under 18 d, 900 at 18 to 28 d, 680 at 28 d or older). Collapse overall: 85.7%, 86.3%, 68.3%.
