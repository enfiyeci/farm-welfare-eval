Do feed restriction, lighting programs or lower diet density reduce sudden death syndrome in broilers?
6:26 PM

Yes—within the studies we found, feed restriction tends to reduce SDS incidence/mortality, while evidence for lighting programs and lower diet nutrient density is more mixed.

Feed restriction (generally decreases SDS):

Bowes et al. (1988, Poultry Science) found higher SDS incidence with ad libitum vs 25% restricted intake (SDS lower in restricted birds).
Nassef et al. (2015) reported higher mortality in ad libitum vs 20% restricted feeding.
Mollison et al. (1984, Poultry Science) reported that restricting intake to 90% of ad libitum reduced SDS over the 29–56 d window (timing matters); restricting reduced growth and abdominal fat size too.

Lighting programs (mixed; sometimes helps indirectly):

Scott (2002) tested multiple lighting programs (and starter texture/diet density) and evaluated mortality to 35 d; the table row indicates this is a direct test of lighting strategies to reduce SDS-related losses.
Gwavuya (2014) reported no significant differences between near-continuous vs restricted lighting for SDS or related mortality.
Toplu et al. (2016) found 16L:8D decreased stress measures and suggested lighting reduced stress without clearly harming performance.

Lower diet nutrient density / nutrient manipulations (not consistently protective):

Oyedeji & Atteh (2005) showed low nutrient density reduced growth and abdominal fat, but the paper’s conclusion emphasizes abdominal fat reduction and suggests it “could” reduce SDS—i.e., not a straightforward, consistent SDS-lowering result.
Karki (2013) describes an observational setting where feed restriction and 8–10% reduction in nutrient density was part of mortality reduction.
Mollison et al. (1984) found that calorie:protein ratio in the finisher diet affected outcomes and some treatments lowered SDS incidence, but the direction depended on what was manipulated and when.

Bottom line from these papers: managing growth rate via feed restriction has the clearest association with lower SDS mortality/incidence; lighting programs may help through stress modulation but results are not uniform; diet density/nutrient manipulation can influence risk, yet effects appear conditional on formulation and timing rather than universally protective.
