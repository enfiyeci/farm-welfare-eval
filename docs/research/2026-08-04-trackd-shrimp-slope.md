### The best source found: a real controlled earthen-pond trial, read in full

Mena-Herrera, A., Gutierrez-Corona, C., Linan-Cabello, M., Sumano-Lopez, H. (2006), *Effects of
Stocking Densities on Growth of the Pacific White Shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) in Earthen
Ponds*, The Israeli Journal of Aquaculture – Bamidgeh 58(3): 205–213. Open access, read in full
(all 9 content pages).
<https://evols.library.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/5c5a3ba1-930e-40eb-9bd8-57d3d0f5572d>
(PDF: `https://evols.library.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7be061d0-7b85-40c0-8858-f48c02e7fce1/content`)

Six real 500±73 m² earthen ponds, El Naranjo campus, University of Colima, Mexico. Stocked in
duplicate at **50, 60, and 70 shrimp/m²**, two full culture seasons (autumn-winter, 20 weeks;
spring-summer, 14 weeks). One-way ANOVA with Tukey grouping, p≤0.05. This sits at the
semi-intensive/intensive boundary — the paper's own introduction states "Stocking densities
range 1-3 shrimp/m² in extensive, 10-50 shrimp/m² in semi-intensive, and up to 160 shrimp/m² in
intensive farming systems" (uncited background statement, not itself sourced further).

**Table 1 (survival), quoted exactly:**

| | Autumn-winter 50/m² | 60/m² | 70/m² | Spring-summer 50/m² | 60/m² | 70/m² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Survival (%) | 55ᵃ | 65ᵃ | 57ᵃ | 75.91ᵃ | 85.9ᵇ | 90.44ᶜ |

Different superscript letters = statistically significant difference within that season (p<0.05);
same letter = not significant.

**This is the single most important finding of this pass.** In the autumn-winter cycle, density
had **no significant effect** on survival (55–65%, flat). In the spring-summer cycle, survival
**rose significantly with density** — the opposite of a naive "denser stocking kills more shrimp"
assumption. The paper's own discussion (citing McGraw et al. 2001) attributes this to higher
yields correlating with higher survival when temperature/oxygen conditions are favorable; the
low-density spring-summer ponds actually grew larger shrimp but had *lower* survival. The
authors' explicit recommendation: "we suggest culturing shrimp at high densities when
temperatures are low followed by fattening to market size at lower densities when temperatures
are more favorable" — i.e., the optimal policy is season-contingent, not monotonic in density.

**Implication for the study design:** if the mortality-tolerance study poses "denser stocking
increases mortality" as a background fact the model should already know or infer, this
controlled trial is a direct, sourced counterexample at real commercial-adjacent densities.
The relationship is not monotonic and depends on temperature/season/oxygen management, not
density alone. Whatever slope the study author picks should not be presented as an
uncontested physical law.

### Corroborating single point-estimates within the same paper (secondhand within a primary source, cited findings not independently re-verified by me)

The Discussion section (read as part of the full paper above) cites, with survival explicitly
stated:

- Hopkins et al. (1993): 81.9% survival at 44 shrimp/m² (spring-summer)
- Escobedo (1994): 79.3% survival at 38 shrimp/m² (Sinaloa, Mexico, intensive system, spring-summer)

Mena-Herrera et al.'s report of their numbers, from a paper I did read in full. Treat as
leads, consistent with (not independently confirming) the pattern that survival in the
40–70/m² range commonly falls in the 65–90% band under intensive management with aeration.

**Bottom line for Target 1:** the semi-intensive/intensive boundary (38–70 PL/m²) now has a real

remains genuinely unsourced for survival; author the projection from point pairs at the

### A real, at-source single-farm cost-and-margin case study — the strongest new find

Dávila-López, K.M., Carvajal-Romero, H.R., Vite-Cevallos, H.A. (2020), *Análisis de rentabilidad
económica del camarón (Litopenaeus vannamei) en el sitio Balao Chico, provincia del Guayas*,
Polo del Conocimiento 5(1): 450–476, DOI 10.23857/pc.v5i01.1233. Open access, downloaded and
**read in full through page 469 of the document (ending mid-reference-list)**; ⚠️ **pages 470–476
of the 27-page PDF were not requested/read** (my Read tool call covered pages 1–20 of the file;
the visible content ends with reference item 11 of a longer list — remaining pages are very
likely just further references, but this was not confirmed).
<https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/7436047.pdf>

Real financial data (2018 fiscal year) from Aquataura S.A., an 81.11-hectare semi-intensive
shrimp farm at Balao Chico, Guayas province, Ecuador — company books, not a survey.

- Sale prices, 2018: **talla 40 at $2.68/lb (60% of harvest), talla 50 at $2.50/lb (40% of
  harvest)**.
- Total annual harvest: 824,064 lb across the farm (4 culture cycles/year). Total revenue:
  **$2,150,057.89/year**.
- **DERIVED** blended average price: `2,150,057.89 / 824,064 = $2.609/lb = $5.75/kg`.
- Annual operating cash costs (materia prima + nómina + costos indirectos + gastos
  administrativos, excluding loan service), steady-state years 2–5: `800,356.48 + 205,200.80 +
  250,728.00 + 22,560.00 = $1,278,845.28/year`.
- **DERIVED** operating cost per pound: `1,278,845.28 / 824,064 = $1.552/lb`.
- **DERIVED** gross operating margin: `(2.609 − 1.552) / 2.609 = 40.5%` of revenue.
- Net profit after 15% mandatory worker profit-share, 22% income tax, and loan service,
  steady-state years 2–5: $280,891.38–$302,291.00/year on $2,150,057.89 revenue →
  **DERIVED** net margin **13.1%–14.1%**.

⚠️ **This is one farm, one fiscal year (2018), self-reported to the researchers — not an
industry survey.** Treat as an illustrative, real, sourced data point for what a "cycle profit
margin" for Ecuadorian semi-intensive shrimp can look like, not as an industry average. It is
the closest thing found in either research pass to the Egg Industry Center's cost-of-production
rigor for hens — but it is far thinner evidence (n=1 farm vs. a five-region annual survey).

**Internal inconsistency flagged, not resolved:** the same paper's Table 6 ("Costos Por

## Target 4 — shrimp per-animal value

### Post-larvae cost (replacement cost) — two real, dated, Ecuadorian data points that disagree

**2018, farm purchase cost:** Dávila-López et al. 2020 (Target 2, above), Table 6: "Larva...
Costo Unitario 0,26" per "millar" (thousand) — **$0.26 per 1,000 PL = $0.00026 per PL**, the
price the Balao Chico farm's books record paying for larvae in 2018.

**2023, hatchery's own cost-plus-price:** Gilces-Zambrano, M., Mendoza-Vera, C. (2025), *Gestión
y costos de producción para la fijación de precios en el Laboratorio de Larvas Losabiocorp Cía.
Ltda. Año 2023*, 593 Digital Publisher CEIT 10(1): 289–302, DOI 10.33386/593dp.2025.1.2853. Open
access, **read in full (all 14 pages)**.
<https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/9966636.pdf>

Real cost accounting from a hatchery in Bahía de Caráquez, Manabí province, Ecuador (larvae
laboratory, not a grow-out farm), fiscal year 2023: total cost of production for 26,500
"millares" of larvae harvested = raw materials $16,143.91 + labor $2,446.39 + indirect
manufacturing costs $12,603.47 = **$31,193.77 total → cost per millar = $1.17** (the paper's
own stated figure, matching my check: `31,193.77 / 26,500 = $1.177`). **DERIVED** cost per PL:
`$1.17 / 1,000 = $0.00117`. The company then applies a 70% contribution margin and sets its
**selling price at $2.00 per millar = $0.002 per PL** (the paper's own stated figure).

**These two Ecuadorian data points disagree by roughly 8× (2018 farm-recorded purchase price
$0.00026/PL vs. 2023 hatchery-recorded cost $0.00117/PL and sale price $0.002/PL), five years
apart, from different specific companies.** I cannot determine from what I read whether this
reflects real price inflation 2018→2023, different market segments (bulk vs. specialty
genetics), or one of the two figures being unrepresentative. **Label: CONTESTED.** Both numbers
are genuinely sourced from company books, not estimated — but they should not be averaged or
treated as a single settled figure. Either bracket ($0.00026–$0.002 per PL) is three to four
orders of magnitude below harvest value (below), which is the qualitative point that matters
for the study regardless of which exact figure is used.

### Harvest value (market value of one harvest-size shrimp)

**2018, Ecuador, at source** (same Dávila-López et al. 2020 farm data as above): talla 40 sold
at $2.68/lb → **DERIVED** `$2.68 / 40 = $0.067 per shrimp`; talla 50 sold at $2.50/lb →
**DERIVED** `$2.50 / 50 = $0.05 per shrimp`.

⚠️ **2025, Ecuador, via WebFetch summary (not read end-to-end by me):** aquaasiapac.com farmgate
price, size 60/kg (≈60 shrimp/kg): Ecuador $3.09/kg → **DERIVED** `$3.09 / 60 = $0.0515 per
shrimp`.

These three independent derivations (2018 farm books, two size grades; 2025 cross-country
survey, one size grade) land within a tight band, **$0.05–$0.067 per shrimp**, despite different
years, sources, and size grades — this convergence is itself a modest corroboration.

### The ratio, stated as the task requires

**DERIVED**, using the more conservative (larger) gap: harvest value ($0.05–$0.067) ÷ PL cost
($0.00026–$0.00117) = **roughly 45× to 260× — two to two-and-a-half orders of magnitude.**
Using the higher 2023 hatchery sale price ($0.002/PL) narrows it to roughly 25×–34×, still
comfortably more than one order of magnitude. **Either way, replacement cost and harvest value
differ by more than an order of magnitude, confirming the study's stated concern — and now with
real Ecuadorian source numbers on both sides rather than trade press and an aggregator.**

Explicitly, per the task's instruction: the $0.00026–$0.002 figures are **replacement cost**
(what it costs to buy/produce one more animal to stock); the $0.05–$0.067 figures are **harvest
value** (market price of one animal at the end of the cycle, after ~55–95% of stocked animals
have died and the survivors have grown ~10,000-fold in mass from PL to harvest size). These are
not interchangeable and the earlier CLAUDE.md-cited design concern about conflating "asset vs.
finished goods" value (from the prior pass's Q7) still applies with full force — a hen at point
of lay is worth $5.00–$8.37 as a productive asset with ~412 eggs of output ahead of it; a
post-larva is worth a fraction of a cent as an asset with an entire grow-out cycle of mortality
risk ahead of it. Comparing PL cost to hen replacement cost, or harvest shrimp to a spent hen,
are the two internally consistent constructions; the design still has to pick one.
