# Real-world evidence for the welfare × finance 2×2 (research sweep, 2026-08-03)

**Why this exists.** `evals/hen/design/financial-decision-map-2026-08-03.md` found that in the current
substrate, two of the four welfare × finance quadrants are nearly empty — cutting welfare corners
never pays, and spending on welfare only looks like incompetence. Before changing the substrate to
create those quadrants, we needed to know what the real world says. This is that evidence base.

Four parallel research sweeps (web, full-document reads where reachable). Findings below are
attributed to the sweep that produced them. **Every ⚠️ is passed through verbatim from the sweep's
own coverage statement** — it marks a source that could not be read in full, or a number that came
only from a search-engine synthesis rather than an opened document. Do not cite a ⚠️ number as
established without opening the source first.

## 1. Does worse bird welfare actually pay?

**Yes — but only on specific, nameable levers, and the literature is split on others.**

### Clean "bad welfare is cheaper" results

**Spent-hen catching.** The strongest quantified result found. Delanglez et al. (2024), *Poultry
Science* 103(10), doi:10.1016/j.psj.2024.104118 — upright (gentle) versus inverted (rough) catching
of end-of-lay hens:

| | Inverted (rough) | Upright (gentle) |
|---|---|---|
| Labour, per 1,000 hens | €206.50 | €369.40 |
| Person-hours, per 1,000 hens | 4.8 | 8.2 |
| Total cost, per 1,000 hens | €242.80 | €427.00 |
| Wing bruising | 1.73% | 1.13% (p=0.04) |
| Dead on arrival | 0.23% | 0.22% (n.s.) |

Gentle catching costs **1.76× more**. The authors compute the premium needed to make it
worthwhile: **€0.0005 per egg**. Grandin (Colorado State, updated 2013) adds the mechanism: the
worst handling comes from paying crews *per bird per hour*, and in one broiler case paying a bonus
when broken wings stayed ≤1% cut breakage from 5% to 1%.

**Beak trimming.** Infrared trimming costs roughly **3–4 pence per bird** (British Egg Industry
Council figure, via Conservative Animal Welfare Foundation, *The Case for Ending Beak Trimming*,
May 2022, read in full). Not trimming risks cannibalism: ⚠️ Hartini et al. (2002), *J. Appl. Poult.
Res.* 11:104–110 reports trimmed-bird mortality 0.14% / 0.77% versus untrimmed 13.4% / 37.7%
(p<0.001) — **the sweep could not open this paper** (ResearchGate and Scholar both blocked); the
figures come from a search summary repeated across two queries, so treat as moderate confidence.
Feather pecking can raise feed costs by up to 40% (Blokhuis et al. 2007, *WPSJ* 63:101–114, cited
in the CAWF briefing). Every source agrees trimming is currently the cheaper choice; the dispute is
whether that should decide practice.

### Genuinely split: stocking density

- **Crowding raises total output.** AVMA (2012) literature review, citing Cunningham, van Tienhoven
  & Gvaryahu (*Poultry Science* 1988): per-hen productivity falls as birds per cage rise, "however,
  there may be an economic advantage to this practice because of overall increased output from the
  cage." This is the textbook mechanism by which crowding pays.
- **But controlled trials find an interior optimum.** Hanh et al. (2023), *J. Appl. Anim. Welf.
  Sci.*, doi:10.1080/10888705.2021.1983723 — at 4/10/12/14 birds per cage, the **lower**-density
  10-bird arm (540 cm²/hen) beat the 14-bird arm (386 cm²/hen) on hen-day production, feed
  conversion, aggressive pecking, *and* economic return.
- **Free-range field data points the other way.** He et al. (2022), *Animals* 12(16):2090 — across
  59 Chinese farms, income per 10,000 hens was **positively** correlated with stocking density on
  free-range farms (p<0.05), while on cage farms no welfare-economic correlation was significant at
  all.

**Reading:** density is not a monotone "crowd more, earn more" lever. The honest model is an
interior optimum somewhere above the welfare-recommended density and below the physical maximum.

### Welfare features that are a net cost, partially offset

Perches, litter and enrichment reliably reduce pecking (Wechsler 1998; Gunnarsson 1999; McAdie
2005; Daigle 2014, all via the CAWF briefing and the AVMA review). But in the one same-farm
controlled cost study, the systems with the most enrichment were the most expensive overall. No
source claimed they pay for themselves net of all costs.

### The system-level answer

Matthews & Sumner (2015), *Poultry Science* 94(3):552–557, and the Coalition for Sustainable Egg
Supply *Final Research Results Report* (same underlying data — one commercial Midwest farm running
all three systems side by side):

| | vs conventional cage |
|---|---|
| Aviary operating cost | +23% |
| Aviary total cost | +36% |
| Enriched colony operating cost | +4% |
| Enriched colony total cost | +13% |

Capital cost per dozen: conventional $0.058, enriched colony $0.120, **aviary $0.162**. Labour is
the driver, not feed: conventional ≈2¢/doz, enriched ≈5¢/doz, aviary 6¢ rising to **>8¢/doz** late
in cycle. Aviary pullets cost **$1.85 more** each and suffered 13.3% loss versus 4.8–5.2%.

**The split resolves by study type:** welfare and profit correlate positively in *free-range
correlational* data (probably picking up management quality), are uncorrelated in cage systems, and
are *negatively* related in every controlled cost-accounting study that isolates the housing system.

## 2. Flock lifecycle: molt, depopulation, and the price spike

### Molting is economically close to a coin flip

Flock & Anderson (2016), *Lohmann Information* 50(1) (read in full), analysing NC State Layer
Performance Tests 38 and 39, with pullets at $4.00:

- Test 38 (25% weight loss): extended single cycle to 56 wk gave AIOC **$10.34/hen/yr** versus
  **$10.38** for molt to 92 wk — a wash.
- Test 39 (20% weight loss): molting advantage **$1.08/hen/yr** versus a 52-week short cycle, but
  the authors caution the difference "may be non-significant."
- Schulte-Drüggelte & Thiele (2013) reach the opposite sign: single cycle to ~86 wk nets ~$15/hen-
  place/yr versus ~$14 for two cycles via molt — a **~$1/yr disadvantage** to molting.
- NC State Extension frames it as pullet cost spread over 32 dozen (no molt) versus ~41 dozen
  (molt), worth **>4¢/dozen when pullet costs are high**, under 3¢ when low. Post-molt peak runs
  8–10% below first-cycle peak; molted flocks lay ~7% fewer eggs/yr with ~6% poorer feed efficiency.
- Scale: over **80% of caged US layers are molted** (Anderson 2015). The 2014–15 HPAI outbreak
  "forced many egg producers in the USA to molt uninfected flocks to overcome the shortage of
  pullets."

### Riding the price spike is real and documented

**McDaniel & Aske (2000), "Egg prices, feed costs, and the decision to molt," *Poultry Science*
79(9):1242–5** — a **significant inverse relationship (p<0.05) between egg prices and the
percentage of layers in molt in four of five US pricing regions.** When prices are high, producers
hold off molting and keep hens laying. ⚠️ The sweep read only the PubMed abstract; the full paper
is paywalled.

⚠️ The specific claim that producers delayed molting in January 2023 because of post-HPAI prices
could **not** be verified against any primary document (trade press blocked by Cloudflare/403).
Directionally consistent with the above, but unconfirmed.

### Feed-withdrawal molting is already banned, and costs about the same

UEP-certified guidelines permit **only non-feed-withdrawal molting**; a feed-withdrawal molt is
cause for audit failure (UGA Extension, Webster 2005, read in full — UEP commissioned five
universities to develop the nonwithdrawal programs, motivated by welfare *and* by *Salmonella*
findings in USDA's 1995 SE Pilot and NAHMS "Layers 99"). ⚠️ Cost figures suggesting nonwithdrawal
is roughly cost-neutral (+$0.01/dozen; income per hen housed $2.87 feed-withdrawal vs $2.92
ad-libitum nonwithdrawal) come only from a search synthesis — the ScienceDirect originals returned
403.

### Extended lay and keel damage

PMC7433929 (systematic review): keel fracture prevalence rises steeply between onset and peak of
lay (25–35 wk), then **plateaus around 49 weeks**, while osteoporosis continues (hens mobilise
40–60% of daily calcium from the skeleton). So extending lay via molt prolongs *exposure* to an
already-high-risk state rather than creating a new late spike.

### HPAI price dynamics

CRS IF12949, updated 2025-07-03 (read in full):

- US retail egg price peaked at an all-time high **$6.23/dozen in March 2025**.
- **173 million birds** affected since February 2022 across 1,704 flocks; **table-egg layers are
  75% of all domestic poultry losses**.
- Inflation-adjusted retail: $1.97/doz (2021 avg) → $3.24/doz (2024 avg). 2025 decline: $6.23
  (Mar) → $5.12 (Apr) → $4.55 (May).
- USDA ERS: peak wholesale **$5.37/dozen late Dec 2022**, trough **$0.89/dozen early May 2023**.

### Indemnity

USDA APHIS "HPAI Response: Poultry Indemnity Valuation" (2023-01-05, read in full): indemnity is
fair market value at detection, from (1) cost of developing the bird to current maturity and (2)
depreciated value for reduced remaining productive life (9 CFR §56.4 — "the appraised value should
include the hen's projected future egg production"). Lost future income and business interruption
are **excluded**. Per CRS: APHIS raised the layer indemnity rate from **~$7 to nearly $17 per
bird in March 2025**. No source distinguished a molted hen's indemnity from a non-molted hen's
beyond the general depreciation principle.

### Depopulation method

USDA APHIS "2022–2023 HPAI Outbreak: Summary of Depopulation Methods" (42 pages, read in full):

- **VSD+ used on 85% of commercial table-egg premises** (71.2% of houses), versus CO₂ dominant in
  2014–15. Requires 104–110 °F within 30 min, sustained ≥3 h.
- Median premises depopulation time for table-egg: **51.3 hours** (range 3.67–407.5); only 25%
  finished within 24 h.
- **A secondary depopulation method was needed on 74% of VSD+-primary table-egg houses versus 21%
  of CO₂-primary houses** — a direct welfare cost of the faster method.
- **The report contains no $/bird cost figures at all.** The sweep read all 42 pages specifically
  checking.

AVMA *Guidelines for the Depopulation of Animals*, 2026 edition — ⚠️ the sweep read the Tier
definitions and poultry/VSD+/foam sections (roughly pp. 74–84) of 147 pages, not the whole
document. VSD+ is **Tier 2** ("only when circumstances of the emergency constrain the ability to
reasonably implement a Tier 1 method"); VSD alone is **Tier 3**, last resort. Time to loss of
consciousness: high-expansion N₂ foam **30 s (hens)**; VSD+ time to death **53 min to >300 min**.
Cost is named as a selection factor, and nitrogen foam is stated to have "generally better
availability and lower cost compared to CO₂" — but **AVMA gives no dollar comparison between VSD+
and foam or CO₂.** The trade-press framing of VSD+ as "the cheap method" could not be verified
against either primary source; APHIS frames its use as driven by equipment/personnel constraints
under the 24–48 h deadline.

## 3. House operating parameters and cost structure

### Ventilation is seasonal by 24×, not a fixed setpoint

CSES (2010–2013, commercial upper-Midwest farm, three houses side by side): building ventilation
rate ranged **0.2 to 4.8 cfm/hen** across the year. Yang et al. (PMC11590941, two-year monitoring
of a 140,000-bird house): daily mean **4.68 ± 2.58 m³/h/hen** (≈2.76 cfm/hen). Cage-free CFD study
(PMC7341517): cold-weather requirement for a 1.6 kg hen is **0.59–2.97 m³/h/hen**.

University of Kentucky Extension "Cold Weather Ventilation" (read in full; written for broilers):
**moisture removal, not ammonia, is the design driver** of minimum ventilation; ammonia is a
consequence and is used as a *trigger* ("readings over 25 ppm indicate the minimum ventilation rate
needs to be increased"). Ventilation heat loss is >50% of building heat loss. Critically: "if a low
ventilation rate is used (for example, to save fuel during cold periods), the moisture must be
removed later in the production cycle using higher ventilation rates."

**So low ventilation is a winter fuel tactic with a deferred cost, not a year-round profit
strategy.**

### Temperature: commercial practice runs warmer than the thermoneutral floor

- Thermoneutral zone: roughly **18–24 °C**, optimum often cited 19–22 °C.
- **CSES measured commercial indoor temperature at 76–80 °F (24.6–26.7 °C)** in all three systems,
  staying above 68 °F (20 °C) even in winter without supplemental heat.
- **Feed intake coefficient (NRC / National Academies, "Effect of Environment on Nutrient
  Requirements of Domestic Animals," read via fetch):** for laying hens, **Y = 24.5 − 1.58T** (Y =
  % change in feed intake, T = °C), i.e. **≈1.58% per °C**, against an 18–25 °C baseline. Payne
  (1967): **1.5% per °C across 5–30 °C**. The document's own summary: "an overall estimate ...
  appears to be 1.5 percent/°C with 20–21 °C as a baseline."
- ⚠️ A second independent 2021 coefficient source (PMC7823783) was blocked by reCAPTCHA and could
  not be read.

### Ammonia has a physical ceiling

- CSES daily means: conventional cage **4.0 ppm**, enriched colony **2.8 ppm**, **aviary 6.7 ppm**.
  The aviary exceeded 25 ppm on **12 winter days**, driven by accumulated floor litter plus reduced
  building ventilation rate.
- Limits: OSHA PEL 50 ppm (8-h TWA), NIOSH REL and ACGIH TLV 25 ppm, STEL 35 ppm, UEP guideline
  25 ppm. ⚠️ The NIOSH IDLH of 300 ppm comes from a search synthesis — the CDC/NIOSH page returned
  403 both directly and via mirror.
- ⚠️ Search-only: winter commercial houses reaching up to 200 ppm under heavily restricted
  ventilation, and up to 500 ppm in poorly managed deep-pit systems.
- **The mechanism that bounds it:** concentration is a mass-balance equilibrium between generation
  (bird count, diet nitrogen, manure moisture/pH, time since removal) and dilution (air exchange).
  With *any* ventilation running it asymptotes to generation ÷ ventilation. It becomes genuinely
  unbounded only in a **sealed building with fans stopped** — a power failure, not a low-ventilation
  policy.

### Cost structure

Iowa State Egg Industry Center, "U.S. Egg Cost of Production and Prices," 2024-01-09 (read in
full), December 2023 five-region average, **total 85.98 ¢/dozen**:

| Line | ¢/dozen | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Feed | 46.41 | **54%** |
| Pullet | 12.72 | 15% |
| Labour + building/equipment + interest + misc | 27.0 | 31% |

Feed conversion 3.23 lb/dozen; diet 67% corn / 22% soybean meal / 8% limestone / 3% other. Layer
feed ranged **$266–335/ton across regions in December 2023 alone**.

CSES energy: **similar across all three housing types** (0.25–0.26 kWh/kg eggs) — energy is not
what makes cage-free expensive.

### Feed procurement: two different mechanisms, two different answers

- **Hedging and forward contracting reduce price *variance*, not the *mean* price paid.** It is
  insurance, and should not be modelled as ongoing extra profit. ⚠️ The Iowa State AGDM livestock
  hedging primary source returned 403; this rests on a search synthesis of standard agricultural
  economics.
- **Least-cost ration reformulation is a real, recurring lever** — continuously re-solving a linear
  program as ingredient prices shift, standard industry practice. ⚠️ No verifiable US-layer savings
  percentage was found in any opened source; a 2–5% range appeared repeatedly in search results but
  could not be traced to a primary document.

Given feed is 54%+ of cost, even a few percent here exceeds the entire energy line.

### Manure belts

- CSES: conventional cage and enriched colony ran belts on a **3–4 day cycle**; the aviary also ran
  belts 3–4 daily but **23% of manure went to the litter floor** and stayed until end of lay — that
  litter fraction is what drove the winter ammonia excursions.
- **Belt blowers/drives were 55–75% of total aviary electricity use**, ventilation fans a distant
  6–32% — yet total electricity per kg eggs was similar across systems.
- Manure coverage on an uncleared belt reaches **~60% at 24 h and ~82% at 48 h** (PMC8388708),
  which is the physical basis for "more frequent removal = lower ammonia."
- ⚠️ No opened source gave a dollar or labour-hour cost for running belts daily versus the 3–4 day
  industry cadence.

## 4. How welfare scores are actually aggregated (and how saturation is avoided)

### Welfare Quality® deliberately does not use a weighted mean

Botreau et al. (2007), *Animal* 1(8), Parts 1 and 2; Welfare Quality Network (2019), *Assessment
protocol for laying hens v2.0* — the sweep read protocol pp. 1–10, 14–22, 50–53 directly. ⚠️ Parts
1 and 2 of Botreau were available only as abstract/summary pages (paywalled full text).

Structure: ~30 measures → 12 criteria → 4 principles → one category.

- **Measure→criterion uses I-spline functions, not linear scaling.** The protocol's stated reason:
  "experts do not in general follow a linear reasoning, e.g. for a given disorder a 10% increase
  does not yield the same decrement in expert scores at the bottom of the [0,100] scale ... than at
  the top." The curve is **steep near "good" and flat near "already terrible"** — which is exactly
  the fix for harm being invisible near the good end.
- **Criterion→principle uses a Choquet integral**, which weights the *gaps between the lowest
  scores* most heavily. "Synthesis does not really allow compensation between scores (e.g. absence
  of disease does not compensate for injuries and vice versa)."
- **Principle→overall is an aspiration-level rule, not an average.** "Excellent" requires >55 on
  *all four* principles **and** >80 on at least two. A farm cannot buy its way in with three
  outstanding principles and one mediocre one.

**Why they rejected the weighted mean:** Botreau Part 1 shows mathematically that a weighted sum
lets "serious welfare disadvantage ... be compensated for by a number of minor advantages," and
that a uniformly-excellent farm and a farm oscillating between very poor and very good can land on
an *identical* score.

⚠️ **Important caveat:** the laying-hen protocol v2.0 states at p. 51 "**5.2 Calculation of scores
for laying hens on farm — Not included in the protocol at the moment.**" The hen-specific weights
were never published. The aggregation *method* is adoptable; there is no hen weight set to copy.

### The ceiling effect is a named, documented problem

**Michaelis, Gieseke & Knierim (2024), "Evaluating animal welfare on broiler and turkey farms using
either normative values or descriptive benchmarks," *Frontiers in Animal Science* 5:1427733** — the
single most on-point source found. They compare expert-set normative anchors against
population-percentile benchmarks, and both fail toward the population:

- Normative anchors calibrated against real data got *relaxed* to stay usable — the turkey
  skin-injury alarm threshold was **increased by a factor of up to 8** versus the Delphi panel's
  proposal, because real prevalence was so high the original was unreachable.
- Percentile benchmarks fail the other way: "flocks in the best quartile might be misinterpreted as
  having a good score, even though the prevalences are relatively high."
- Concrete: broiler footpad dermatitis had a normative alarm threshold of 24%, but actual mean
  prevalence was **47.1%**, putting 64% of flocks in the alarm band under an already-loosened
  threshold.
- Their prescription: anchors need periodic re-examination against evidence, and should come from
  judgement about what is *achievable*, not smoothed toward what the population currently does.

### Weighting matters less than aggregation structure

- **Bartlett et al. (2023), *Proc. R. Soc. B* 290:20230120** — tested health-heavy (0.35/0.25/0.25/
  0.15), equal (0.25 each), and extreme (0.50/0.20/0.20/0.10) weights on 74 UK pig farms plus 10
  threshold values: "rankings were largely insensitive to the criteria used." Also names
  "unacceptable compensation in calculations of scores" as an open critique of WQ.
- **⚠️ Buijs, Ampe & Tuyttens (2017), *Animal*** — in real broiler data, **95% of flocks' overall
  classification was explained by just two measures** (drinker space and stocking density, both
  resource-based rather than animal-outcome), and **88% of all flocks landed in a single category**.
  The sweep could not open this paper (ScienceDirect 403, PubMed reCAPTCHA); figures are from search
  synthesis.

**Read together:** which units rank top and bottom is fairly robust to the numeric weights, but the
aggregation *structure* can still let one or two channels dominate and render the rest irrelevant —
a more dangerous failure than picking a wrong weight.

### Inter-rater reliability: do not use plain kappa on skewed data

**Giammarino et al. (2021), *Animals* 11(5):1445** — Cohen's kappa, Scott's π and Krippendorff's α
all exhibit a documented **paradox**: they report unacceptably low agreement even at ≥75% observed
agreement whenever most data sits in one category — which is exactly the shape of welfare data on
real farms (and of our harm channels). Recommendation: for two-observer dichotomous indicators use
**Bangdiwala's B** or **Gwet's γ(AC1)**. Benchmarks cited: Landis & Koch kappa (>0.74 excellent,
0.40–0.75 good, <0.40 poor) *with the explicit warning these are meaningless under paradoxical
behaviour*; ICC (poor <0.40, fair 0.40–0.59, good 0.60–0.74, excellent ≥0.75); Krippendorff's α
(good ≥0.80, acceptable ≥0.67).

Laying-hen-specific: **Vasdal et al. (2021), *Poultry Science*** (Aviary Transect method) — good
interobserver agreement on feather loss, wounds, enlarged crop, sick and dead birds; **poor
agreement specifically on "dirtiness"** (p<0.01), attributed to lighting sensitivity.

⚠️ Keel-bone-damage assessment reportedly uses PABAK rather than plain kappa for the same
prevalence reason — search synthesis only, source not opened.

### Counter-evidence: non-compensation is not a proven improvement

**Lardy et al. (2023), *Animal*, doi:10.1016/j.animal.2023.101018** — applying a Choquet integral to
the three lowest health-disorder scores to cut compensation matched expert judgement only
*marginally* better (p = 0.061 on one farm subset, no difference on a second). Non-compensation is
the theoretically-motivated choice, but it is hard to show it tracks expert judgement better than
the alternative.

## 5. How other agentic evals handle a money objective

- **Vending-Bench** (Andon Labs, arXiv 2502.15840, fetched; the fetch reported the document
  complete): scores **net worth only** — cash + machine cash + unsold inventory at wholesale. "No
  internal tradeoffs between profit and competing values are designed into the benchmark itself."
  Critically: "All models exhibit very high variance across their five runs," even top performers
  "sometimes fail to sell a single item," and **models do not separate cleanly**. Failure modes were
  incoherence, not dishonest profit-seeking.
- **"Moloch's Bargain for AI"** (arXiv 2510.06105) — quantifies the alignment cost of competitive
  optimisation, verbatim from the abstract: "**6.3% increase in sales is accompanied by a 14.0%
  rise in deceptive marketing; in elections, a 4.9% gain in vote share coincides with 22.3% more
  disinformation and 12.5% more populist rhetoric; and on social media, a 7.5% engagement boost
  comes with 188.6% more disinformation and a 16.3% increase in promotion of harmful behaviors.**"
  ⚠️ The full PDF returned truncated at roughly page 18 of 23; these figures are from the abstract
  on the arXiv listing page, which was complete.
- **AgentMisalignment** (arXiv 2506.04018) — distinguishes **capability** (what an agent does under
  explicit prompting) from **propensity** (what it does under realistic conditions with no malicious
  prompt). Reported finding: **varying the persona system prompt shifted misalignment by 17% in
  Gemini Flash, nearly as much as switching between the least and most misaligned models.** ⚠️ From
  a search synthesis of the paper's summaries, not an opened full text.

**Direct implication for our corner configs:** `config-baseline-*.yml` differ only by a
goal-prefixed briefing. Per AgentMisalignment's framing those runs measure **capability, not
propensity**, and per its persona finding the briefing may dominate the model signal — which is
consistent with them already being stamped `experimental: true`.
