# Dietary phosphorus restriction and skeletal welfare in laying hens

## Bottom line

The evidence base supports a **qualified but fairly strong claim** that sufficiently low dietary available/non-phytate phosphorus can cause genuine skeletal pathology and welfare harm in laying hens, including osteoporosis, loss of bone strength, vertebral compression fractures and the clinical syndrome historically called cage-layer fatigue/cage-layer osteoporosis. Crucially, this is **not just a Northeast Agricultural University finding**. The strongest independent replication is actually much older: work from the University of Connecticut and Auburn University produced clinical osteoporosis/paralysis and mortality by manipulating phosphorus, with necropsy confirmation of skeletal disease. citeturn22search3turn28search5turn28search11

However, I would **narrow three parts of the proposed claim**.

First, I did **not locate a convincing non-NEAU controlled phosphorus-restriction experiment that specifically scored modern keel-bone fracture/deviation outcomes**. The independent replication is strong for *skeletal harm generally*—clinical cage-layer osteoporosis, bone mineral density/content, long-bone strength and histopathology—but direct replication of **low P → keel fracture/deviation** appears weak or absent in the literature I could locate. Modern keel studies overwhelmingly manipulate housing, genetics, exercise, calcium or management rather than phosphorus itself. Thus, the keel-specific causal claim presently rests disproportionately on the NEAU series. citeturn19search3turn19search6

Second, the evidence does **not justify a universal threshold saying “≤0.18% NPP causes bone harm whereas 0.22% is always safe.”** A recent experiment found **2.2 g/kg NPP (0.22%) safe even at 66–81 weeks**, with 4.3% Ca and no adverse bone outcome over 102 days. Conversely, Auburn work found marked osteoporosis/mortality at 0.18% calculated available P and some adverse response even at 0.23% around sexual maturity. The threshold clearly moves with feed intake, calcium, phytase, P source/availability, strain and physiological stage. citeturn22search7turn28search5

Third, the proposition that harm is **“invisible for months while production stays normal” is real in some experiments but should not be stated as universal**. The Connecticut cage-layer-osteoporosis experiments provide striking evidence that egg production can remain nearly unchanged while osteoporosis mortality changes enormously. But severe restriction such as 0.12% nPP in a modern Lohmann Brown experiment eventually depressed production too, beginning about eight weeks into treatment. citeturn24view1turn24view2turn29view1

My evidence-weighted formulation would therefore be:

> **In laying hens, marginal or deficient available phosphorus can progressively compromise skeletal integrity and, under sufficiently severe or prolonged conditions, cause clinical osteoporosis/cage-layer fatigue and fracture-related mortality. Skeletal deterioration can be poorly reflected by egg production, particularly at marginal deficiency. The phenomenon has been independently replicated outside China for whole-skeleton and long-bone outcomes, but direct independent replication for phosphorus-induced keel-bone fractures/deviations is presently much thinner. A single universal percentage threshold is not defensible: approximately 0.22% NPP has been safe in some late-lay diets, while 0.18–0.23% available P has caused pathology under other conditions.**

### What this means for “independent replication”

There is **good independent replication for low-P → skeletal disease**, but it is endpoint-specific.

The Connecticut work goes well beyond surrogate mineral measurements: clinically affected birds were commonly paralysed, and cage-layer-osteoporosis diagnoses were confirmed through gross examination, histology and/or spinal radiographs. Contemporary pathology work showed that the classic posterior paralysis involved a **compression fracture of the fourth or fifth thoracic vertebra with spinal-cord compression/degeneration**. citeturn26view1 That is genuine fracture-associated welfare pathology, not merely a statistical change in tibia ash.

What is *not* independently replicated to the same standard is **phosphorus restriction specifically causing modern keel-bone fracture/deviation scores**. The modern radiographic literature treats fractures and non-fracture keel deformities separately and confirms their high prevalence, but I did not find a Western controlled P-dose experiment assigning hens to low-P diets and then longitudinally scoring KBF/KBD. citeturn19search6turn19search8

That distinction should materially affect the wording of an evidence review: **“skeletal/fracture harm” is well replicated; “keel fracture caused by low P” is much less independently replicated.**

## Cage-layer fatigue: dose, incidence and mortality

### The clearest exact dose-response is Singsen et al.

Singsen et al. is unusually valuable because it reports **cause-specific necropsy mortality**, not merely total mortality, while holding dietary Ca at **3%**. citeturn24view0

For Experiment 3A, the exact results were:

| Phosphorus source | Total dietary P | Egg production | CLF/CLO-attributable mortality | Total mortality |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Dicalcium phosphate | **0.34%** | **66.8%** | **15.00%** | **22.50%** |
| Dicalcium phosphate | **0.76%** | **67.3%** | **0.83%** | **8.33%** |
| Curaçao rock phosphate | **0.34%** | **61.5%** | **23.33%** | **35.00%** |
| Curaçao rock phosphate | **0.76%** | **65.8%** | **13.33%** | **24.17%** |

These are percentages of the starting experimental groups; diagnoses were made at necropsy. citeturn24view1turn24view2

This is a **very large biological effect**. With the more available dicalcium phosphate source, higher P reduced CLO mortality by about **94% relative**: 15.0% to 0.83%. Yet egg production was essentially identical, **66.8 versus 67.3%**. That is probably the cleanest historical evidence for the particular “production is a poor sentinel for skeletal welfare” proposition. citeturn24view2

It also shows why “total P” can be misleading. Even at 0.76% total P, Curaçao phosphate yielded **13.33% CLO mortality**, versus 0.83% with dicalcium phosphate, consistent with substantially lower P availability from the rock-phosphate source. The authors concluded that nominal P concentration could not compensate fully for poor availability. citeturn24view2

The housing interaction was equally dramatic. Pooling diets and P sources, the necropsy incidence of osteoporosis was **13.12% among 480 caged hens, 0.63% among 160 wire-floor hens, and 0% among 220 litter-floor hens**. Thus P insufficiency interacted strongly with mechanical loading/activity/confinement rather than acting as a single sufficient cause of CLF. citeturn24view3

The temporal pattern also matters. CLO deaths were not confined to the moment lay began. In one genetic strain they appeared early and continuously, whereas another had a peak around **8–12 weeks of lay and another during 28–40 weeks of lay**. citeturn24view3 This supports delayed cumulative skeletal failure, while also showing that susceptibility is strain-dependent.

### Rao and Roland: strong dose ordering, but the indexed abstract lacks the requested percentages

For **Rao et al. 1995**, the diet levels are unusually clear: **0.18, 0.23, 0.28, 0.33 and 0.48% calculated available P**. The paper explicitly measured osteoporosis incidence and mortality and states that the **maximum incidences occurred in late-maturing pullets at 0.18%, followed by 0.23%**. citeturn28search5

I cannot responsibly supply exact percentages for those two groups from the material I was able to retrieve: **PubMed provides the ranking but not the incidence table**, and I did not obtain a primary full-text copy containing that table. This is a case where a precise-looking number would be less reliable than explicitly flagging the gap.

The preceding **Rao, Roland & Hoerr 1992** experiment likewise reports “high incidence of osteoporosis” and elevated mortality at **0.40% total P** in late-maturing pullets, but the abstract does not expose the exact incidence percentages. citeturn28search11

### Simpson et al. 1964

The exact citation is:

**Simpson, C. F., P. W. Waldroup, C. B. Ammerman, and R. H. Harms. 1964. “Relationship of dietary calcium and phosphorus levels to the cage layer fatigue syndrome.” *Avian Diseases* 8:92–100. DOI: no DOI located.** The volume index confirms the article and its subject as the calcium/phosphorus relationship to cage-layer fatigue. citeturn25search2turn26view0

The contemporary description by Singsen et al. says Simpson et al. found **histological evidence of bone damage in caged pullets receiving low-P diets**, with signs resembling cage-layer fatigue. citeturn26view1

I did **not** recover the original Simpson tables, so I cannot verify an exact treatment-specific percentage of hens developing CLF or dying from CLF. I would flag Simpson as **“primary paper identified; full quantitative table not retrieved”**, rather than as an abstract-only source—the old paper simply has no useful indexed abstract.

### Roush et al. 1986 is not a CLF-incidence paper

This is an important correction to the candidate list.

**Roush, W. B., M. Mylet, J. L. Rosenberger, and J. Derr. 1986. “Investigation of Calcium and Available Phosphorus Requirements for Laying Hens by Response Surface Methodology.” *Poultry Science* 65:964–970. DOI 10.3382/ps.0650964.** citeturn21search3

It used **324 laying hens for 280 days**, in a 3 × 3 design of **2.50, 3.75, 5.00% Ca × 0.35, 0.50, 0.65% available P**, beginning with hens placed in cages at 18 wk and production data from about 23 wk. citeturn21search3

But its endpoints were **egg production, body weight, egg weight, feed conversion, eggshell percentage and cumulative mortality**. It did **not diagnose cage-layer fatigue or measure bone strength**. Cumulative mortality was related to **low calcium**, not reported as a P-dose CLF effect. The much-cited “optimal” 0.48% available P was an optimization of **eggshell percentage**, not a skeletal-welfare threshold. citeturn21search3

So I would *exclude Roush 1986 from a table of CLF incidence*. It is frequently relevant to historic P requirement discussions, but it does not supply the endpoint the welfare claim requires.

### Harms et al. 1990

I could not unambiguously identify a **Harms et al. 1990 primary study reporting dose-specific CLF incidence/mortality** from the citation as given. One 1990 Florida paper that does surface is:

**Fethiere, R., R. D. Miles, and R. H. Harms. 1990. “Influence of synthetic sodium aluminosilicate on laying hens fed different phosphorus levels.” *Poultry Science* 69:2195–2198. DOI 10.3382/ps.0692195.** citeturn18search1

That is a short dietary-P performance study in 40- and 60-week-old hens, **not a CLF/bone-strength incidence experiment**. citeturn18search1 I would therefore not attribute CLF percentages to “Harms et al. 1990” without a more specific citation.

A later Harms-group phosphorus-deficiency study likewise focused on disrupted laying cycles/ovarian regression rather than diagnosing CLF. In commercial hens fed about **0.30–0.31% total P with no supplemental inorganic P**, production eventually fell markedly; that is evidence of severe P deficiency, but not a bone-welfare endpoint. citeturn20search2

### Field relevance

The controlled CLF phenomenon was not merely an experimental curiosity. **McCoy, Reilly & Kilpatrick 1996**, studying mortalities from a commercial cage flock, found **9.7% overall flock mortality**, of which **35% was attributed to cage-layer osteoporosis**; bones from CLO mortalities had lower density and breaking strength. That corresponds arithmetically to about **3.4% of the starting flock dying with CLO**, although that 3.4% is my calculation, not a number printed by the authors. citeturn16search5

Full citation:

**McCoy, M. A., G. A. Reilly, and D. J. Kilpatrick. 1996. “Density and breaking strength of bones of mortalities among caged layers.” *Research in Veterinary Science* 60:185–186. DOI 10.1016/S0034-5288(96)90017-X.** citeturn16search5

This does not implicate phosphorus specifically, but it validates CLF/osteoporosis as a genuine mortality and mechanical-bone-failure endpoint in commercial hens.

## The bone-harm threshold across the laying cycle

The evidence does **not** support one clean percentage cutoff. The most defensible interpretation is a zone of risk whose location changes with **daily P intake, bioavailability/phytase, Ca concentration, strain and stage of reproduction**.

### Around 0.22% NPP

There is now direct evidence that **0.22% NPP can be bone-adequate in late lay**. In the recent age-comparison experiment, older hens received either **0.36 or 0.22% NPP from 66 to 81 weeks for 102 days**, with **4.3% dietary Ca**. Lowering NPP to 0.22% did not reduce performance or the reported tibial outcomes and actually improved the Seedor index. citeturn22search7

That is a strong argument against writing “0.22% is marginal/unsafe in late lay” as a general proposition.

It is more defensible to say:

> **0.22% NPP has been demonstrated to support bone outcomes in at least one prolonged late-lay experiment, but it should not be treated as a universal safety guarantee because P availability and daily intake differ between diets.**

### Around 0.18–0.23%

The Auburn data make the opposite point. In Rao et al. 1995, **0.18% calculated available P was clearly pathological**, and **0.23% was the second-most adverse treatment in late-maturing pullets**, with measured deterioration in BMC, bone density, breaking strength and increased osteoporosis/mortality. citeturn28search5

This is especially useful because the study did not infer deficiency from egg production. It directly measured skeletal mechanical/mineral traits.

But it occurred from **18 to 24 weeks**, at sexual maturation/peak onset, not late lay. Thus the common intuition “older hens are necessarily more P-sensitive” is not established by this work. In fact, it demonstrates a distinct **onset-of-lay vulnerability**, with late sexual maturation modifying the response. citeturn28search5

### Around 0.24–0.25%

Several independent experiments find no skeletal reason for a high requirement near 0.4–0.5% available P.

Keshavarz’s 1986 factorial experiment found **0.24% available P indistinguishable from 0.44 or 0.64% for tibia ash**, across 3.5–5.5% Ca, over 16 weeks. citeturn20search21

Sell et al. found that **0.25% NPP in late lay performed materially better than 0.15%**; nevertheless, their endpoint capable of detecting skeletal depletion was only whole-body P rather than breaking strength, and it showed no treatment effect. citeturn27search0

Taken together, these are broadly compatible with **~0.22–0.25% being adequate in many practical diets**, but not with an exact universal breakpoint.

### Around 0.15%

This is the most context-dependent region.

Sell et al.’s hens receiving **0.15% NPP at 51–71 wk** consumed only about **150 mg NPP/day** and suffered reduced production/feed efficiency/egg weight; effects were strongest in that late phase. Yet mortality and whole-body P were unchanged. citeturn27search0

By contrast, **Keshavarz 2000** found that a phase program of **0.25 → 0.20 → 0.15% NPP** from 30–42, 42–54 and 54–66 weeks could maintain production statistically comparable with a **0.40 → 0.35 → 0.30%** control in the absence of phytase. The study included tibial chemistry among its endpoints, but the indexed abstract does not provide a treatment-specific mechanical bone-strength result, so I would not use it to claim proven skeletal safety at 0.15%. citeturn27search5

Full citation:

**Keshavarz, K. 2000. “Nonphytate phosphorus requirement of laying hens with and without phytase on a phase feeding program.” *Poultry Science* 79:748–763. DOI 10.1093/ps/79.5.748.** citeturn27search5

A subsequent four-strain Cornell experiment found clearer problems. **Keshavarz 2003** compared four commercial strains through 20–63 wk. A **0.25 → 0.20 → 0.15% NPP** program without phytase reduced production and increased non-prolapse mortality compared with a constant 0.45% control; an even lower **0.20 → 0.10 → 0.10%** program caused drastic production loss and mortality during its 0.10% phase. citeturn27search6

Full citation:

**Keshavarz, K. 2003. “The effect of different levels of nonphytate phosphorus with and without phytase on the performance of four strains of laying hens.” *Poultry Science* 82:71–91. DOI 10.1093/ps/82.1.71.** citeturn27search6

Thus **≤0.15% is a credible risk region**, but the severity is not reproducible enough to present 0.15 or 0.18 as a universal biological cliff.

### Age and “worsening into late lay”

There are two different phenomena that should not be conflated.

**Baseline laying-induced osteoporosis does worsen with cumulative lay.** In the Roslin study, most modern Hisex hens were osteoporotic at 68 wk whereas an older, genetically different Brown Leghorn line was not, illustrating that modern reproductive genotype profoundly affects end-of-lay structural bone loss. citeturn27search3 Modern radiographic keel studies likewise show accumulating keel fractures as hens age. citeturn19search6

But **evidence that the phosphorus requirement for bone rises monotonically with age is much weaker**. The Auburn experiments show severe P susceptibility at *onset* of lay, while the recent 66–81-wk experiment found 0.22% NPP adequate. citeturn28search5turn22search7

So I would phrase the age component as:

> **The welfare consequence of any additional mineral deficit may become more consequential as structural bone reserves are progressively depleted through the laying cycle, but available evidence does not establish that older hens have a universally higher percentage-NPP requirement than younger hens.**

### “Production stays normal while bone degrades”

This part has unusually good historical support, but it needs the word **“can.”**

Singsen’s strongest comparison is almost a natural demonstration: with dicalcium phosphate, **66.8 versus 67.3% egg production** accompanied **15.0 versus 0.83% cage-layer-osteoporosis mortality** at 0.34 versus 0.76% total P. The authors explicitly interpreted this pattern as egg production taking priority over skeletal integrity during progressive P insufficiency. citeturn24view1turn24view2

Rennie et al. also found no phenotypic correlation between egg production and trabecular bone volume among modern Hisex hens, although that observation was about osteoporosis generally rather than P deficiency specifically. citeturn27search3

But severe contemporary deficiency does eventually affect production. In the 2025 Lohmann Brown study, **0.12% nPP** began depressing production from roughly the **31st week**, after treatment started at 23 wk, while also reducing tibia ash, stiffness and maximum load. citeturn29view1

Thus:

**“Skeletal harm may precede or be disproportionate to production loss”** is well supported.

**“Production stays normal for months whenever phosphorus is below requirement”** is too strong.

## Keel deviation versus fracture as welfare endpoints

The evidence quality differs substantially between **fractures** and **deviations**.

For fractures, the welfare interpretation is strong. As you note, Nasr and Armstrong provide experimental evidence of pain; broader reviews additionally find reduced mobility, altered perch negotiation and other behavioral consequences. citeturn19search3 Radiographic studies show that fractures can remain unhealed or continue healing for many weeks; in one longitudinal study, healing ranged up to **36 weeks**, though most lesions healed much sooner. citeturn19search6

For **keel deviations**, the literature is markedly less secure. Riber and Hinrichsen’s peer-reviewed welfare review concludes that deviation-specific welfare consequences remain largely unclear because many studies pool deviations with fractures; proposed effects include impaired movement/rest and potentially elevated fracture risk, but these are not supported by a deviation-specific pain literature comparable to that for fractures. citeturn19search3

Full review citation:

**Riber, A. B., and L. K. Hinrichsen. 2018. “The Influence of Keel Bone Damage on Welfare of Laying Hens.” *Frontiers in Veterinary Science* 5:6. DOI 10.3389/fvets.2018.00006.** citeturn19search3

The morphology literature also cautions against assuming that every deviation is an incipient fracture. In a longitudinal radiographic study of Lohmann Brown and Lohmann Selected Leghorn hens from **22 to 61 weeks**, researchers classified **non-fracture lesions**—including angulations, sclerosis and indentations—separately from fractures. Angulations can therefore exist without a radiographically identifiable fracture. citeturn19search6

Full citation:

**Thøfner, I. C. N., et al. 2020. “Radiographic Evaluation of Keel Bone Damage in Laying Hens—Morphologic and Temporal Observations in a Longitudinal Study.” *Frontiers in Veterinary Science* 7:129. DOI 10.3389/fvets.2020.00129.** citeturn19search6

Newer morphology work reinforces that keel fractures are heterogeneous: some have configurations suggestive of trauma, whereas folding-type lesions in older hens resemble insufficiency/pathological fractures associated with weak bone. citeturn19search8 This makes reduced skeletal strength biologically relevant to KBF risk without proving that a pre-existing *deviation* transforms into a fracture.

Most importantly, **association is not progression**. A 2026 commercial-flock study found deviations and fractures strongly associated with one another, and palpable sharp bends were highly predictive of radiographically verified fractures. citeturn19search4 That may partly mean that what palpation calls a “deviation” is sometimes the residual shape/callus of an existing fracture rather than a precursor lesion.

Therefore I would not currently write **“keel deviations progress to fractures”** as established fact. A safer formulation is:

> **Deviations and fractures frequently coexist, and weak/deformed keels may share risk factors with fractures, but evidence that a true non-fracture deviation itself progresses causally into a fracture is limited. Fractures have demonstrated pain and mobility costs; deviation-specific pain remains insufficiently established.**

That distinction matters for the P argument. If a low-P experiment reports **keel deviation alone**, it is a weaker welfare endpoint than a radiographically confirmed fracture, whole-bone breaking-strength deficit, or clinical CLO paralysis.

## Phytase: how far it can move the phosphorus threshold

There is good evidence that phytase can allow a substantial reduction in **inorganic/non-phytate P**, including preservation of bone outcomes, but the effective dose is diet- and enzyme-dependent.

### Keshavarz 2000

At Cornell, hens from **30 to 66 wk** were assigned six progressively lower NPP phase programs with or without **300 phytase units/kg**. Without phytase, production could be maintained down to the **0.25 → 0.20 → 0.15%** program, while lower programs were inadequate. With **300 U/kg phytase**, even the lowest program—**0.15 → 0.10 → 0.10% NPP**—maintained production statistically comparable with the non-phytase control over the whole experiment. Phytase increased phytate-P retention by about **15%** and greatly reduced total P excretion. citeturn27search5

This is persuasive for P sparing, but because the indexed abstract does not expose a breaking-strength result, I would treat it principally as **requirement/performance and tibial-chemistry evidence**, not as definitive fracture-welfare evidence.

### Keshavarz 2003: strain replication and an important limit

The four-strain experiment is more informative because it included **bone ash** and used Babcock B300, DeKalb Delta White, Hy-Line W36 and ISA-White hens from **20 to 63 wk**. citeturn27search6

The treatments were:

- control: **0.45% NPP throughout**;
- **0.25 → 0.20 → 0.15%** with 0, **150 or 300 U phytase/kg**;
- **0.20 → 0.10 → 0.10%** with 0, **150 or 300 U/kg**. citeturn27search6

The 0.20→0.10→0.10 program **without phytase** produced drastic production loss and mortality during the 0.10% phase. Adding 150 or 300 U/kg restored most endpoints. However, even **300 U/kg at that very low NPP regimen left bone ash significantly below the control**, despite restoring many performance traits. citeturn27search6

That is an important warning for an animal-welfare analysis: **phytase can make production look adequate before every skeletal endpoint is fully restored.**

At the less extreme **0.25→0.20→0.15%** regimen, **300 U/kg** was the treatment that maintained the suite of performance outcomes most consistently across all four strains and reduced P excretion by **55.6%** relative to the 0.45% control. citeturn27search6

### Modern direct mechanical-bone evidence

A particularly useful independent modern experiment is:

**Sacakli, P., M. S. Ramay, A. Ceylan, Ö. Ö. Çınar, F. K. Erbay Elibol, J. A. Harijaona, Y. Shastak, P. Ader, D. Feuerstein, and A. Calik. 2025. “Effects of supplemental hybrid bacterial 6-phytase in low-energy, inorganic phosphorus-free hen diets on laying performance, egg quality, and bone strength.” *PLOS ONE* 20(4):e0322135. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0322135.** citeturn29view1

It used **432 Lohmann Brown Classic hens from 23 to 44 wk for 22 weeks**. The positive control contained **3.7% Ca and 0.38% nPP**; the negative control contained **0.12% nPP with no inorganic phosphate** and 100 kcal/kg less metabolizable energy. Negative-control diets were then supplemented with **300 or 600 FTU/kg** of a bacterial 6-phytase. citeturn29view1

The 0.12%-nPP, no-phytase diet caused genuine skeletal deficits: significantly lower **tibia ash, mechanical stiffness, and maximum load-bearing capacity**. It also eventually reduced body weight, production, feed intake and livability. Both **300 and 600 FTU/kg phytase restored the measured bone variables to levels statistically comparable with the positive control**. citeturn29view1

There is one design qualification: the negative control simultaneously reduced metabolizable energy by **100 kcal/kg**, so it is not a perfectly isolated phosphorus-deficiency contrast. Nevertheless, the direct three-point tibial bending endpoint makes it much stronger welfare evidence than studies relying only on egg output. citeturn29view1

The practical synthesis from these experiments is:

**300 FTU/kg is repeatedly sufficient to spare a substantial amount of inorganic P in conventional layer diets, but it should not be treated as a universal guarantee at arbitrarily low nPP.** At roughly **0.12% nPP**, modern 300–600 FTU/kg enzyme restored mechanical bone outcomes in young-to-mid-lay Lohmann Browns; in the older Cornell enzyme system, 300 U/kg at an extreme 0.10% NPP phase restored many production traits while bone ash remained below control. citeturn27search6turn29view1

## Overall evidential assessment

I would grade the constituent propositions differently.

| Proposition | Assessment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Deficient available P can cause osteoporosis/skeletal weakness in laying hens** | **Strong, independently replicated** | Connecticut, Auburn and other non-NEAU groups demonstrate clinical osteoporosis, reduced bone mineral status/breaking strength, paralysis and mortality under low/marginal P. citeturn22search3turn28search5turn28search11 |
| **Deficient P can cause fracture-associated cage-layer fatigue** | **Strong historical evidence** | CLF/CLO diagnoses were necropsy-confirmed; classic pathology identifies vertebral compression fracture and spinal-cord damage. citeturn26view1turn24view2 |
| **Low P increases keel-bone fractures specifically** | **Plausible but inadequately independently replicated** | I found no strong non-NEAU controlled P-dose experiment using modern keel-fracture scoring; the direct evidence is much thinner than for general skeletal harm. citeturn19search3turn19search6 |
| **Bone harm can occur without an obvious production signal** | **Strong in some marginal-deficiency studies, not universal** | Singsen: 66.8 vs 67.3% production alongside 15.0 vs 0.83% CLO mortality. Severe 0.12% nPP eventually depresses production too. citeturn24view2turn29view1 |
| **The harm is delayed/cumulative** | **Good support** | Singsen documents CLO over many weeks, with genotype-specific early and late peaks, and describes progressive skeletal erosion; modern osteoporosis/KBF also accumulates with laying age. citeturn24view3turn19search6 |
| **Older hens are categorically more P-sensitive** | **Not established** | Late lay carries greater background osteoporosis burden, but severe sensitivity is also documented at 18–24 wk, while 0.22% NPP was safe in a 66–81-wk trial. citeturn28search5turn22search7 |
| **0.22% NPP is safe for bone in late lay** | **Supported under at least one strong set of conditions, not universal** | 66–81-wk, 102-day experiment at 4.3% Ca found no bone harm at 2.2 g/kg. But Auburn pathology near 0.23% avP under different physiological/dietary conditions precludes a universal cutoff. citeturn22search7turn28search5 |
| **≤0.18% NPP/avP causes bone harm** | **High-risk region, but not an absolute threshold** | 0.18% avP caused the strongest osteoporosis response at Auburn; results around 0.15–0.18 vary with phytase, intake and strain. citeturn28search5turn27search5turn27search6 |
| **Keel deviations themselves are painful** | **Uncertain** | Fracture pain is well established; deviation-specific welfare evidence is confounded because deviations and fractures are often pooled. citeturn19search3 |
| **Deviations progress into fractures** | **Not demonstrated sufficiently** | They coexist and share risk factors, but radiography identifies non-fracture angulations as a separate lesion class; association does not establish longitudinal conversion. citeturn19search6turn19search4 |
| **Phytase can prevent bone harm at reduced inorganic P** | **Well supported, dose/diet dependent** | 300–600 FTU/kg restored tibial mechanical outcomes at 0.12% nPP in a modern experiment; older multi-strain data show limits at extreme 0.10% nPP. citeturn29view1turn27search6 |

The single strongest independent citation for your proposed narrative is, in my view, **Singsen et al. 1969**. It gives the combination that most modern P-requirement papers do not: **controlled phosphorus manipulation + clinical osteoporosis/CLF + cause-specific mortality + almost unchanged egg output + a time course + a housing interaction**. At 3% Ca, dicalcium-phosphate diets differing from 0.34 to 0.76% total P yielded virtually identical egg production but **15.0 versus 0.83% osteoporosis mortality**. citeturn24view2

The strongest challenge is that **ordinary end-of-lay osteoporosis is not simply a phosphorus-deficiency disease**. Roslin’s modern-vs-old-strain work found profound osteoporosis in the modern line that could not be prevented by the tested nutritional manipulations, and the recent 66–81-wk NPP experiment found **0.22% entirely compatible with normal bone outcomes**. citeturn27search3turn22search7

Accordingly, the evidence base is strongest for the following narrower causal statement:

> **When available phosphorus intake falls sufficiently below what a laying hen can absorb and utilize—because the dietary concentration is low, the source is poorly available, feed intake is inadequate, or phytate P is not adequately liberated—skeletal mineral reserves can be depleted and bone strength compromised. With sufficient severity and duration this can progress to osteoporosis, vertebral fracture/paralysis and cage-layer-fatigue mortality. Egg output is an unreliable early welfare indicator because marginally deficient hens can maintain laying despite substantial skeletal pathology. This causal chain is independently replicated outside NEAU. What remains much less independently demonstrated is the specific link from dietary P restriction to modern keel-bone fracture/deviation scores, and neither 0.18 nor 0.22% NPP should be treated as a universal cross-diet threshold.** citeturn24view2turn28search5turn27search3turn22search7
